# Chapter XXVI

That night proved to be the most restless night ever passed by four people. Aunt Kate was sure there was something worrying Parepa, she could hear her tossing in the bed across the room from her own. Or was it because she was excited with the expectation of seeing her lover?

Parepa was concerned only about Joseph. She was so sorry for him. She had enjoyed his companionship and jolly good jokes a lot, now she finds he actually loves her. Loves her as much as she loves Frances.

Joseph turned and tossed in fevered torment trying to be brave and take his defeat like a man as he had met all other (and they were many) disappointments.

After leaving the Slay home Frances as if in a dream strolled down to the riverbank. He was sure he recognized the very log on which he and Parepa had sat after their wonderful boat ride on the river. He sat down on the log and tried to decide what was right to do. He found all the old love coursing through his every vein. His body surged and swayed as he sat there alone in the moonlight—the same moon—the same river—Yes, the same old log—but where is the sweet young thing he had held in his arms? Where is the golden voice—the sparkling eyes, the ruby lips, the tawny hair, and the kisses? Oh God! the kisses. Suddenly as if he had been committing some theft he sprang to his feet and ran down the shore for many blocks, then into the street where he slackened his speed until he reached the hotel.

He went straight to his suite. When he was alone with his doors locked he fell upon his couch exhausted. His first impulse was to pack up and go without seeing her. That would be best. He could find out in some way who this "Joseph" is and send him money enough to finish his education and marry Parepa, too. Yes, he would do that. He would establish a $100,000 bank account for him and leave for parts unknown. He had heard Parepa promise him that if her lover had changed she would cling to him. Yes, it was better, it was better that way. They would be happy. They would have children; Parepa would be a real mother. She wanted to mother everyone from Aunt Kate to the tiniest baby.

Then he would see before him the girl he loved walking beside him in the park, crying over Uncle Tom and Little Eva, playing her first game of tennis, rowing on the river in the moonlight, running from him on the shore like a sprite, riding beside him on the twin to his steed, sitting on the side of the hill in old Glen Oak, and that last afternoon on the lake—in the rose arbor—she was so sweet and trusting, always sure she loved him.

But—what is he doing? The locket—he is opening it. He is taking out folded paper after folded paper—he is reading them. His face is clouded, his eyes are misty, his hands are trembling. Now he has the pictures of four women's faces laying on the table in front of him. He studies each one earnestly then he folds the papers carefully, and replaces them in their side of the locket. First he raised the picture of his great, great, grandmother in his hand. He seemed to be studying her very soul. Around her neck hung the large locket. It was thick and heavy made of solid gold and engraved with the old Emmerson crest. It hung from her neck to below her breasts on a fine golden chain of great strength though not heavy.

This locket had been made to order. The key was attached by a small chain. It had a secret lock and each generation learned the combination at the age of fourteen years when it became theirs.

After he had replaced this picture he took up the next one. The same long session ensued. There was something he was looking for. No, he did not find it in this picture either. He laid it in its resting place and raised a third one. No, it wasn't there. He thought once he caught a glimmer, then it was gone. He had only imagined it.

The family locket hung from the neck of each of the four women. His own mother came last. He held it long and lovingly, the expression on his face changed. He sprang to his feet crying—"She has it, she has it. My Mother had sincerity, determination, and courage." He kissed her picture over and over again as he paced the floor with it. "You were right, mother, you were right. It shall be as you wish. Our line shall be broken. I shall break it forever. I was blaming you tonight, but never again."

Reverently he placed this picture on the top of the others, then folding the glass over them and snapping it tightly he turned to the folded papers—messages from his mother. Silently he scanned them. The last one he held in his hand for a long time—apparently wondering what he should do with it. He slowly reached for a match, lit it and held the paper over a metal tray as he lighted one corner of it and deliberately watched it burn. A knock at the door which he knew to be Daddy Wheeler's caused him to drop the paper on the tray.

After replacing the locket on his breast he opened the door to his Daddy, his comforter, his worthy counselor, his father confessor.

"Well, my boy, I didn't look for you in so early tonight. Wasn't Parepa home?" but Mr. Wheeler had seen a look of torment on the young face.

"Yes, but Mrs. Slay is very low and dear little Parepa is taking all the responsibility of caring for her. I hadn't the heart to stay," he answered.

"Has the young one changed much in the two years since we saw her?" Daddy watched the pale face before him anxiously.

"She has seen a lot of sadness; her Uncle George's death and now her Aunt most ready to go, but you know it is not always in the gold days, but in the gray ones that the soul grows and the purpose of life unfolds. She's sweeter, more mature, and oh! so beautiful."

"Has she—has she changed her mind?" and the elder man bit his lips in anger for having asked that question so abruptly.

"No," he said thoughtfully. "She hasn't—but I have."

"Come, come, my boy, don't feel that you are not capable of making her happy. You know she could be the happiest woman in the world with you. I can't imagine a happier union of sympathetic souls," and Mr. Wheeler patted the miserable young fellow tenderly on the shoulder. "Better think it over again," and he left him to retire to a restless bed.

Frances had fully made up his mind to leave Peoria the next day after arranging the money for Joseph Donald. He would write Parepa of his discovery and encourage her to listen to the pleadings of her Sunday School lover. He would assure her that he was convinced it was best that they never see each other again.

The next morning Frances slept late into the day. He awoke with a start. He hadn't intended to go to sleep. Here it is most noon! He dressed and answered Mr. Wheeler's tap on the door.

"Where have you been Frances?" he said half scolding. "Parepa has been calling for you on the 'phone. They finally rang for me. I told her I'd have you call her at once."

Frances did not know what to do. Finally he gave her number halfheartedly.

"Hello there—"

"Yes yesterday,"

"Fine! How are you and your good Auntie?"

"I'm so sorry Parepa. Can I do anything to help you, darling?" the word slipped out so naturally. "All right, I'll be there at two. Give my love to your Aunt Kate."

"Oh, sure I'm sending you some too. Ha! Ha! All right, Bye Bye, dearest." He hung up the receiver and staggered to his chair as if in a faint.

Mr. Wheeler did not comment on this though his keen eye noted every move. They went to lunch together and talked about everything but Parepa. Frances became quite his old self before starting out to see her.

When he arrived Parepa was waiting for him in the tiny living room of the little cottage. The tears that sprang to her eyes started a sympathetic moisture to overflow in Frances's and he found he had grabbed her like a demon to his breast and was covering her with hot tears and kisses. Hardly a word was spoken between them as they stood thoroughly lost in the sublime ecstasy of the moment.

A strangling sound came from the bedroom. It was Aunt Kate. Instantly Parepa flew to her side, Frances followed her.

"See Auntie, here is Frances," said Parepa.

"Hello, Aunt Kate," he greeted but Aunt Kate didn't answer. She opened her eyes, smiled, but could not speak.

"Call the Doctor, please. Call the Doctor, Frances, while I care for her.

"Number 982. Oh, hurry please!"
