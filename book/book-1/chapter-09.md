# Chapter IX

"Won't we all be lonesome though, when he goes home," said Parepa wistfully.

"We will miss him but you will get acquainted with others as you go out more," comforted Uncle George.

"Yes, but I'll never feel so acquainted with anyone as I do with him. Seems most as if our dog had died or something. Anyway I'm going to cry," and she did. Cried as though her heart would break.

"There, there," said Aunt Kate, "You are homesick, that's all. Frances has been company for you and you haven't missed your brothers and sisters. Come now, let's go in and have a lunch."

"Aunt K-K-Kate," stammered Parepa, "Who's that?" and she pointed to a stately important looking man stepping from a carriage in front of Frances's boarding place.

"I wonder if that isn't Frances's guardian," volunteered Uncle George.

"Come, let's all go in now," urged Aunt Kate. They went in and while Parepa and Aunt Kate set out a lunch Uncle George posted himself by the parlor window to watch proceedings across the street. He didn't see a great deal. Frances greeted the rather young looking middle aged man with the warmest welcome and the two disappeared into the house. Both seemed overjoyed to see each other.

"I supposed his guardian would be a real old man, but this fellow is a man in the prime of life, younger than me I should judge," announced Uncle George.

Parepa couldn't eat. Neither Mr. or Mrs. Slay appeared to notice this — but they did.

"I believe I'll go right to bed," said Parepa.

"I'll go up with you," and Aunt Kate tucked her in her bed and kissed her a loving good night and crept quietly down the stairs, glad the day was over for the young girl.

"Poor little thing — she doesn't realize the first twangs of womanly instinct are working in her heart. She's really in love with Frances but she don't know just what is the matter with her," and Aunt Kate sat down real close to Uncle George whose arm some way got 'round her waist just as it had 'bout twenty years before. Their eyes met their lips met and once again they were "way back when."

Here they sat, not speaking a word but living again the old days and the new. In memory they were in youth again. The lights were low and so wrapped were they in their own thoughts that the sharp ring of the doorbell startled them into the present.

Uncle George went to the door to find Frances and Mr. Wheeler there.

"Come in, come in," cordially invited Mr. Slay.

"We can't stay but a minute. I couldn't leave without saying goodbye and having Mr. Wheeler meet my good friends. Mrs. Slay this is Mr. Wheeler, my guardian."

"Where's Parepa?" and Frances looked into the dining room.

"She is sound asleep. Went to bed early. Didn't feel good she said."

Disappointment crossed Frances's face but he said no more about her.

"Aren't you staying awhile, Mr. Wheeler?" asked Mr. Slay.

"No, must leave early in the morning. How I'd love to stay a month. I'm tired of hurrying all the time. Someday I hope to have time to use just as I'd like to. With me it's *rush*, *rush*, *rush*," and Mr. Wheeler dropped into a chair overcome with that feeling of hospitality which everyone feels on entering this home.

"I'm sorry our niece has retired, she would have liked so much to meet you," said Aunt Kate. "She and Frances have become fast friends."

"Yes, he was telling me all about her. I'm so thankful for all the nice times you have given him. This child has had an unusual life, and something really human is worth more to him than the wealth of the whole world."

They heard a step on the stairway and, looking up, saw a vision which was never forgotten in two people's lives. These people were Mr. J.C. Wheeler and Frances LaVerne Emmerson. The figure stood still, clothed only in a long nightie, hair hanging loosely over her shoulders, cheeks flushed, eyes shining, one plump dimpled hand on the banister, and pink toes peeking from under the hem of her gown. She looked like a fairy standing, as if hesitating on the threshold of a mystic flight. She descended three steps and hesitated.

"She must be walking in her sleep, don't startle her — she may fall," said Aunt Kate.

With that Frances gave one spring past her and, running up the steps, grabbed both Parepa's hands and kissed them. "Come on down and meet Daddy Wheeler, Parepa." And down the steps they came together his arm about her. She forgot she was in her gown. In fact she had not yet realized it. "Here, Daddy, is Alice Parepa-Roza," the young gallant announced.

Mr. Wheeler put his arm around her and kissed her. "So this is the kind angel that has made this scamp so happy during my absence, hm? Well, little girl that was mighty nice of you."

Frances pulled her down beside him on the lowest step while Uncle George, Aunt Kate, and their guest returned to comfortable seats in the living room. The children kept up a round of frank chummy talk. So likewise were the older folks with their backs to the stairway. Mr. Wheeler overcome by the charming hospitality of this fine couple, became confidential, that is, much more confidential than this sedate, reserved, closed-mouth gentleman had ever allowed himself to be.

"Frances seems to be such an all-round good chap, and since Parepa has been with us, the two of them have taken off any number of years from our span," said Mr. Slay. "We'll miss him so much."

"The child has had an awful shock this last year. Underwent a very sensitive operation in Glasgow last October. Don't seem to have left any bad effects, but the doctor's orders were — perfect rest for one year. We'll go back now and I hope there will be no more interruptions in the education we have planned. Frances will then be old enough and I hope sensible enough to lay out a life's plan of work without any dictation from me." Mr. Wheeler stopped, cleared his throat and said, "Frances tells me you have a beautiful city here when you get up on the bluff. Wish I had a few days here."

"Can't you make it this time?" asked Mr. Slay.

"Impossible, but I think we'll plan more time here when we come back in two years."

Laughter and unrestrained chatter drifted from the stairway.

"Well, we must go now. Tomorrow will be a hard day on Frances."

Goodbyes were said and they left quickly. Parepa, without a word, turned and went up the steps to bed.

"I wonder if she will discover she has on her gown when she gets up there," said Uncle George.

Aunt Kate tiptoed up to see and found her fast asleep in her bed as if she hadn't been disturbed.

The next morning she was the same bright girl, eager to practice and left for the candy shop without mentioning or giving any indication she even thought of the night before.

Uncle George was puzzled over her actions. He expected she would be all broken up over Frances's departure.

Five days passed — a letter for Parepa bearing a New York postmark came. Aunt Kate could hardly wait until she came home from work.

"Guess what I've got behind me," and Aunt Kate backed against the wall.

"A letter from Frances," she guessed.

"Right, the first guess, but how did you know?"

"He said I'd get one about today," and she took the letter, opened it and read it all to herself. "He says give you and Uncle George his love." And then to Aunt Kate's surprise she took the letter and went quietly to her room without showing it to her. Almost immediately she returned and began her practicing as attentively as if her whole mind was on it and nothing else.

When she had finished Aunt Kate and she sat at the kitchen table, eating supper, and chatting about the day's happenings at the store.
