# Chapter XVI

They were home now and found Uncle and Auntie on the porch waiting for them.

"Let's all go to the show Saturday night," suggested Frances. "Dad will like to go, too, I know."

"It won't be a very high class show," said Parepa, "but I am crazy to go. Some one told me it was mostly acts put on by individuals and not a real play."

"Well, we'll all go anyway if you say so," Mr. Slay said to Frances.

So it was settled and at eight o'clock Frances called for them.

Parepa was not there but Uncle George explained that she would be late in arriving at the box on account of an unexpected turn of affairs. However, they were to leave her ticket at the box office and she would join them when she arrived. Mr. Wheeler was waiting in the lobby and the four of them took their place in the box.

The performance had begun but no Parepa had joined them. The second act was to be a dance and song act interspersed with impersonations. To the surprise of the occupants of a certain box, the actress was unmistakably Parepa-Roza. They sat enthralled with the rest of the audience. Such a surprise! Her dancing and acting was perfect, her singing heavenly. How did she dare do it? The genius of this young actress made a memorable picture. The expression on the faces of these people changed but were non-committal. They were tense with anxiety for her getting through what she had undertaken. However, this sensation was not shared by Parepa. She was as sure of herself as if she had been at it for years. Not a waver, not a hesitation overcame her and when at last she bowed her way off stage the whole house clapped as though they would bring the roof down. I said the whole house—no, the occupants of a certain box did not clap, neither did they talk to each other so bewildered were they.

Look! She is returning to the stage in different attire. This time to sing an encore.

"Marvelous! Wonderful!" exclaimed Mr. Wheeler, the only one in the party who felt uninterested enough in the singer to give himself up to enjoying it. As she left the stage this time the crowd was more eager than ever but all the response they got was a most gracious bow.

In a few minutes she was joining them in the box. After greetings were exchanged she took her place beside Frances who at once grabbed her hand and held it. That act telling her more than words could have done. She sat as calm as if nothing had happened, listening to the rest of the program.

When they were leaving the Opera several reporters confronted them but Uncle George and Mr. Wheeler convenienced them that an interview was impossible. Mr. Wheeler left them at the corner saying he would come out some evening and have a good visit.

As they waited for the street car Uncle George took Parepa's chin in his hand and tipped her face up to his. "Now see here young lady you put one over on the old folks this time and you were a queen, but remember this—never again."

"You were wonderful, Parepa, but I never want to suffer in suspense as I did tonight. I was so afraid you'd—well, you would fail," said Aunt Kate.

"Fail! Why Aunt Kate, I never thought of that," she answered. "When they asked Mrs Breen to substitute for the actress that is sick, she sent them to me, telling them I was fully capable of giving a full evening's program if need be. She called me at the store about one fifteen this afternoon and told me the manager was on his way to see me and that I must do it. I told him I would and Mrs Breen and I went through the whole thing this afternoon. I felt gorgeous in those fine clothes of the sick lady's. Really though I'm so sorry if I hurt you good folks. I thought it would be a grand surprise for you all."

"So it was," said Frances, "and a grand shock too. I had no idea you could do it. I say, Mr. Slay, you and Mrs Slay have a fine piece of workmanship here. Congratulations to you both!"

"They deserve *all* the credit. They have made me what I am. If *I* am a success then *they* are a success." And she grabbed both of their hands and kissed them. "I love them both more than my own life."

The street car was coming now and they boarded it for home. On leaving them at the door of their home Frances promised to call for Parepa to go for a horse back ride some evening the next week.

"You remember we go to Church tomorrow," she reminded him.

"You bet! I'll see you there," and he was off.

Parepa was unshaken by the applause and attention given her, nor did she tell Aunt Kate of the flattering offer received from the manager of the show after leaving the stage that evening.

The next morning at the breakfast table Uncle George held the paper up before Aunt Kate's eyes. She read aloud: "Young actress brings down the house with her wonderful acting and beautiful voice. Turns down offer of large salary made by manager. Is little country lass earning her dramatic and vocal lessons by working in her Uncle's candy store at 313 Main Street. Makes her home with Uncle and Aunt, Mr. and Mrs. George Slay to whom she gives all credit for her success.

"Miss Alice Parepa-Roza Wesley of the Sparland vicinity came to make her home with her Uncle and Aunt just two years ago. Mrs Slay having been before her marriage famous in grand Opera recognized in this slip of a girl the possibilities which she proved so positively were hers. Mrs Slay taught her music and placed her under the tutorage of Mrs Rose Mendenhall Breen, dramatic instructor. The child made such startling advancement that her teacher has trusted her with evening's entertainment.

"She is soloist in one of the large Churches of this city. Though her voice is still childish it is all the more appealing. In refusing the astonishing offer made her by L.M. Parker, Manager, Miss Wesley stated that she was not desirous of a stage career and that no flattering offer would tempt her to follow the footlights."

Mr. Slay put down the paper and stared at Parepa. "Is it possible you are so sensible, little one? Such a success as you were last night, would turn any girl's head."

"Not our girl—remember, George dear, she is an exception to the rule," smiled Aunt Kate so proud she could hardly contain herself.

"Dear Auntie and Uncle, do you think I could be so heartless as to leave you now? Remember you were the making of me. I'll never forget. Now let's get the dishes done and ready for Church."

When she entered the choir loft she smiled at Frances who was surrounded by a group of young men and women.

He came over to her and whispered, "Saw your name in the paper this morning."

"Strange," she answered smiling, "Uncle and Auntie saw it too."

Professor Blough called them to order and the service began.
