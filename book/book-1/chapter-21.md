# Chapter XXI

These two enjoyed many evenings together, and through the month of August they were almost inseparable. For Parepa's birthday Aunt Kate and Uncle George planned a six o'clock dinner party. Mr. Wheeler had returned from his business trip to the West and would be their guest that evening. Uncle Will and Aunt Minnie were invited too. Mrs. Breen, a widow, and Mr. and Mrs. Professor Blough completed the party.

Parepa was delighted with her birthday presents, which began to arrive early in the day. From Mrs. Breen came a beautiful morocco-bound set of Dickens. Uncle Will and Aunt Minnie remembered her with a gold bracelet. Mr. Wheeler's choice was a large Wedgwood vase filled with eighteen most beautiful red roses. The card read: "A rose for every year. To wish you joy, my dear," signed, Daddy Wheeler. Professor Blough sent her such a beautiful etching, Beethoven's Moonlight Sonata. Uncle George and Aunt Kate gave her a safety bicycle. Nothing came from Frances until late in the afternoon, a pair of beautifully jeweled opera glasses nestled in a soft velvet lined leather case. "With best wishes and a fond memory of an evening with 'Uncle Tom' and you."—Frances.

Aunt Kate hired a cateress and assistant to look after the serving of the dinner. Ten covers were laid, the appointments were perfect. The table was most beautiful with a dainty birthday cake as centerpiece. Eighteen white candles stood straight and tall from their bed of fluffy pink frosting edged with a wreath of pink roses and green foliage. The crystal, china, and silver shone and sparkled against the white linen tablecloth. Dainty place cards picturing a beautiful young girl adorned with garlands of roses told each guest their place.

It was arranged that between courses each of the party was to tell their first impressions of Parepa. Uncle George stood up after the fruit cocktail and said: "I believe I should have the privilege of telling you first how this little blue eyed, rosy cheeked, freckled nosed, curly haired youngster came to be in our midst these last two years.

"You see, it was this way. I was in the habit of making my brother's home an overnight stopping place once every two weeks as I drove the tea wagon through the country for over fifteen years. I took a great fancy to little Alice Parepa-Roza from a tiny baby to the day she came home with me in the tea wagon. I always called her my girl. I happened to be at brother John's when they named her. The Alice was for an eastern aunt and the Parepa-Roza was given her more as a joke, for the time being, and not as a permanent name. You see, the real Parepa-Roza was a great prima donna and most beautiful singer. As she was crossing the ocean in a large ship, it sank. She sang to the frantic drowning crew and voyagers until she herself sank to a watery grave. This little baby here," and he reached over and patted Parepa's head, "cried so hard and all the time that her father said she had lungs that would make her a singer like the famous Parepa-Roza. It was decided to name her Parepa-Roza. That was about sixteen years ago." He gave Parepa a mischievous glance.

"She grew and grew like a weed. Seemed to be large for her age always; at fourteen she finished grade school. On her birthday two years ago, I coaxed the consent of her parents to let her come home with me to stay until my return trip two weeks later. After a lot of discussion and advice they consented. I can see her yet sitting there beside me in that old covered tea wagon.

"She chattered along right friendly for several miles, then she seemed to prefer silence on my part as well as her own. She was deep in thought, but I couldn't penetrate her mind. I just supposed she was getting homesick and wished she hadn't come. Finally I ventured to say, 'I wish you were our little girl. Aunt Kate and I would just love to have you live with us always,' and, to my surprise, she turned her shining face up to mine and said: 'Oh, Uncle George, do you really mean it? Would you really keep me?' I had not looked for this, and I hardly knew what to say. Then the little minx begged, 'and then can I work?' I laughed aloud. Her work! What could she do? As we rode along, she grew real talkative on the work proposition and confessed she had hoped she could work out and earn some money to study on. Young as she was, she had a business head on her as old as her uncle beside her. She knew it took money to make dreams come true, and she was willing to earn it. We all know the success she has won. Now, Mrs. Slay, tell them what you thought of her when you found I had brought her bag and baggage home to you."

Mrs. Slay laughed as she said: "I was so happy I could have taken the child in my arms and rocked her to sleep. I know it was the happiest day I had seen since the death of our own little Maude, who would have been eighteen now. Parepa has been the life of us both and we are so proud of her. She has done us both great honor by making the best of the opportunities made possible by us. Now our little girl is eighteen—of age—her own boss—Can you imagine that? Congratulations, darling," and she sank down into her chair, a tear in her eye.

Uncle George watched Parepa closely and wondered if she was going to let the evening pass without making it known that she wasn't eighteen. The next course was being served now, and a jolly group were visiting and telling jokes on each other.

Uncle Will spoke for he and Aunt Minnie. "You can imagine my surprise when brother George came to me and told me that Parepa (as we all like to call her) wanted a job. We both laughed about it and decided to give her a try out at the candy store. I didn't think she would stay. Thought she would get homesick and leave for the country in a few days. However, before the first day was over, I decided that if I could help it she would never leave. That first day she made herself indispensable by building a most beautiful rainbow from colored candies in one of the showcases. I'll never forget the look of triumph on her face when she showed it to me. I appreciated her love for color and realized in her a wonderful asset to our business. She has never disappointed me. We congratulate you, Parepa, and wish you many, many more happy birthdays."

After the meat course was cleared, Mrs. Breen told of the surprises she had received while teaching Parepa. "I might say she taught me nothing was too hard for her to undertake. Her sweet disposition always made me feel as if I had associated with ethereal beings. Congratulations, my dear," and she made a low bow to Parepa.

The usual "Thank you" was Parepa's only response.

Mr. Wheeler stood up and, glancing around the table mischievously, said: "You asked me to tell you what my first impressions were of this young lady. Well, the first time I saw her she stood at the top of these stairs in her nightie. She impressed me as a ten-year old. Surely no older. She looked sweet enough to kiss. Her hair hung over her shoulders and down her back, reminding me of a picture of an angel I once saw. The next time I saw her was on the stage at the opera house. I must say that to Parepa and her dramatic instructor are due great credit. I never saw better acting or heard a sweeter voice in a finished artist. Parepa, I congratulate you on this, your eighteenth birthday."

When he sat down, Frances sprang up. His face aglow and his eyes sparkling. "Parepa was the first playmate I ever felt like really playing with. I have, all my life, felt a restraint which seemed impossible to overcome. The minute I saw her I knew we would be great friends forever. And we are going to be—aren't we, Parepa?"

"Indeed we are," she said, raising from her chair. "I hope we shall all be friends always.

"I want to thank you all for the many nice things you have said about me tonight and for the many unkind things you might truthfully have said that you didn't mention. Now, Uncle George, I am going to ask you if you won't please tell these good people the part of our conversation on the way to Peoria in the tea wagon that you avoided telling them before. The part that will make a difference in the number of candles on my birthday cake and the number of roses in Mr. Wheeler's bouquet."

Uncle George rose laughing and said: "Well, when Parepa suggested getting a job, I told her she was too young. It was against the law to work for wages until you were sixteen. Her face fell, and she showed her disappointment by letting a tear slip down her cheek. The next house we stopped at was Mrs. Jackson's. I always took dinner there. When she saw Parepa, she decided without our telling her that Parepa was about her Ruth's age—SIXTEEN. Parepa didn't forget this remark, and when we started on our way after dinner, I chided her by saying, 'So you are Ruth's age, hey? Just sixteen.' 'Yes, and don't you ever forget it,' she said quick as a wink. 'From this on I am sixteen years old.' 'I won't,' I promised, and I never did. When we got home and told Kate it was her birthday, she said, 'Oh yes, let's see, she is sixteen, isn't she?' Neither of us answered. From that on neither Parepa or I have told a soul her age. But tonight we must admit she is only sixteen."

Parepa sprang up. "Can you ever forgive me, Aunt Kate? I have felt so ashamed of it all this time, but the longer it went, the harder it was to tell you. I shall have to take out two of these candles, but I'll promise you this: In two years from now, when I'm really eighteen, I shall still be here with Uncle George and Aunt Kate. I am never going to leave them and I invite you all to my eighteenth birthday party." Handing the candles, one to Aunt Kate and one to Uncle George, she said, "Here are two years of my life, I give them to you, to do with what you will, they are yours."

They all laughed about it, and Aunt Kate said she was glad they had two years more to boss her.

After dinner was over, they went into the living room, where they visited a while and then sang old songs. When it was time to go home, Parepa took two roses from the vase and gave them to Mr. Wheeler and Mrs. Breen. "Keep these in memory of my sixteenth birthday party," she laughed.

Mr. Wheeler escorted Mrs. Breen home in a carriage he had called from the livery, and Parepa really hoped a little romance might find its way into the perfume of the two red roses.

They were all gone now but Frances.

Aunt Kate and Uncle George excused themselves and, kissing their little girl goodnight, went to bed.

Frances pulled the flushed and smiling girl down beside him on the sofa. "Dearie, can you ever forgive me for asking a life's promise from a mere child? You are not old enough to know your mind, darling. You may wish to change it many times in the next two years. It was bad enough for me to expect an eighteen year old girl to realize what it meant. Please forget it and let's begin where we were two years ago."

She sat perfectly still and did not answer.

"Come, darling, speak to me. What do you think?"

"I think I'd love to live these two years over again," she smiled. "But what if you would change your mind now that you know I am a 'mere child'?" she answered.

"Parepa, do you know how old I am?" he asked.

"No," she said, "but I'd guess you eighteen."

"You are two years too late," he laughed. "I am twenty years old today."

"Today?" she said. "Oh, Frances, how funny. Why didn't you tell me this was your birthday? I haven't a present for you. This could have been your party, too," and she patted his cheek lovingly.

"Yes, you have a present for me," he answered.

"What is it?" And she listened for his answer.

"A promise," he said, "a promise that you will enjoy the next two years and forget your promise to me."

"It really doesn't matter to me how old you are, Frances. You are just the same to me."

"And you to me, only I refuse to hold you to your promise. When I come back in two years, if you still feel the same toward me, we will renew our vows. You must feel free these next years to change your mind. I will not be near you to show you the good times a youngster of your age should have, and I want you to give yourself up to the impulses of youth. Write me of all your good times while I am away. You see, we leave Peoria the 24th, only one more week and we will be far apart for two long years. A long, long time, darling," and he raised her face to his and kissed her burning lips.

"Yes, so long, Frances. I'll miss you so much. Will you promise me you will never forget our wonderful times together? That night on the river and all the rest?" she pleaded.

"I promise I couldn't forget them to the end of life," he said, squeezing her hand.

"When I come back, Parepa, you must expect to take up a new course of study. I will be your tutor. You must know all about sociology, social science in other words. You will be old enough to understand life's problems better then."

"I'd love it," she said, always eager to learn anything.

"What are you going to do now that you have two extra years on your hands? Wouldn't you like to go away to college?" he asked.

"No, I must stay with Uncle George and Aunt Kate. Poor Uncle is sick, you know," she answered.

"Sick? What's the matter with him?" he asked, astonished.

"Listen," she said, and they heard him coughing, coughing, such a hacking cough.

"Aunt Kate don't want me to tell, but I'll tell you. The doctor is afraid of consumption. He has quit the tea company job and is going to see if a rest won't help him. He may have to have a change of climate. Now don't let on I told you, for he is very sensitive about it."

"I won't, but I'm sorry. Can I do anything to help? You know what I mean, with money, you know. It's expensive, traveling for one's health, you know. I'd love to foot the bills, just as friend to friend."

"No indeed," and she sat up straight as if insulted. "Uncle George hasn't a lot saved up, but we'll manage some way—thank you, though."

Frances said no more, but if Parepa only knew that he reaped his happiness from the good his gift of gold did people. His whole life was dedicated to helping, wherever he could, those less fortunate in this world's goods. He wished from the bottom of his heart he dared to leave a bank account in Parepa's name so large she couldn't spend it in the two years to come, try as she may. He knew better than to even mention such a thing to her. He knew her pride was genuine and money seemed such a weak offering to make her in time of real worry.

"I must go, you little queen," he said, kissing her again. "I'll see you again day after tomorrow. We'll spend all afternoon just roaming in the park. Will that suit you?"

"Couldn't be better," she said, "but don't forget I have a new bicycle now, and we'll want to take some long rides someday. Wasn't that lovely of Uncle and Auntie?"

After he had gone, Parepa sat and pondered. She wondered just what changes might come to her in the next two years.

The next morning, when she awoke, the sunlight was streaming through the open windows into her room. She felt grand! She slipped into a pink morning gown and descended the stairs. The perfume of the roses drew her to the living room. *Oh, how lovely of Mr. Wheeler,* she thought as she buried her face in them. *Everyone is so good to me.* "Daddy Wheeler," she laughed as she looked at the card again. "Don't that sound funny, though?"

Aunt Kate came in smiling. "Well, how does it feel to step back two years of your life, my dear?"

"Glorious! Aunt Kate, and how do you feel the day after?" she asked.

"Good," she said.

"How's Uncle George?" Parepa asked anxiously.

"Coughed a great deal after he went to bed, so is sleeping late this morning."

"Come and sit down by me, Aunt Kate," and she motioned her to a seat beside her. "I want to talk to you about our plans for the future. Do you and Uncle George have any?" she quizzed.

"Nothing settled at all, only I've been thinking we may have to make a change in climate if your Uncle George don't improve faster than he has. I haven't mentioned it to him yet." And for the first time Parepa noticed a worried look on Aunt Kate's face.

"Where had you thought of going?" she asked.

"The doctor mentioned Las Vegas, Nevada. That's a long way. Of course, we'll all three go," and Aunt Kate smoothed her dress nervously.

"I will go if I can be a comfort to you, Auntie, you know that, but if you don't need me, I'd rather stay here and work. You know it takes a lot of money to travel," she spoke as one of experience and not as a girl of sixteen.

"We couldn't go without you, darling. But let's not talk about it now. Let's just hope a few more weeks will show a great improvement," said Aunt Kate.

"Should I talk to Frances about it?" asked Parepa.

"I wouldn't yet, dear. Come, let's have a bite to eat now, and if George feels like it, let's go to the park this afternoon. We'll take a lunch so we won't have to hurry home," and Aunt Kate led the way to the kitchen. They laughed and talked over the party. Joked about Mr. Wheeler and Mrs. Breen. Wished they'd hitch up together. "Wouldn't they make a dashing couple?" laughed Parepa. "She is so beautiful and he so handsome."

Uncle George came into the dining room just in time to eat with them.

"Morning, my beautiful ladies," he said. "Glad to see you both so wide awake this morning."

"And how's our old man himself?" laughed Parepa.

"Fine! Fine! Never been better," he lied.

After breakfast Parepa went for a ride on her new wheel. That afternoon they had a glorious time in the park. Parepa insisted they have their lunch on the grass down by the rustic bridge. Only she knew why she liked it better than any other place in the park. Just as they were ready to seat themselves, who should appear on the bridge but Frances.

At first he did not recognize them. In fact did not think of it being anyone he knew. He had hoped to have that very spot all to himself that evening, and when he noticed figures there, he turned his back and stood looking into the water as if deep in thought. He was health, life, beauty, and to *someone*, love personified. So engrossed in thought was he that he did not notice the sweet, blushing Parepa slip up behind him and cover his eyes with her hands.

"Who's there?" he cried.

"Guess," she laughed.

"No one but you," and he grabbed her hands and squeezed them in his own. "Where did you come from? Out of heaven, I should judge."

"Oh no! Look over there," and she pointed to the others.

"Come, eat with us."

"No thanks. I don't want to impose on these good folks so much," he said, starting to go.

"Come on! Come on!" shouted Uncle George. "You must!"

Parepa led him by the hand and seated him between Aunt Kate and herself.

"This certainly is an unexpected pleasure," he said. "I just strolled out here to drink in some of the beauty going to waste."

They were a jolly quartet, laughing and singing as merrily as if they were all in their teens. Frances carried the lunch basket up the hill and they sat down on a pavilion seat to rest before starting home.

Frances excused himself and went inside, coming out with a waiter carrying four dishes of ice cream. In about an hour a carriage drove up to the pavilion, and Frances informed them he had 'phoned for it when he was in for the cream.

"That's fine of you," said Uncle George. "I'm so tired I wondered how I was going to make it to the car."

"I thought that pull up the hill after lunch was tiresome for you both," he said and hated himself for letting them know he had noticed it.

"Guess we'll have to admit we aren't as young as we use to be, won't we, Mother?" and Uncle George chucked her under the chin.

When they reached home, Frances wouldn't go in, saying he'd see them tomorrow.

"Oh, hire a bicycle and we'll take a spin in the country," begged Parepa.

"That will be great fun," he assured her, and the time was set for six o'clock.

"We'll ride up the river road to Mossville and take breakfast at Crawl's Inn," he suggested. "Daddy Wheeler and I have made it several times this summer. It's just enough sprint to give a fellow a good appetite."

At five-thirty the next morning Parepa donned her new bicycle costume, which Aunt Kate had made of the prettiest dark red serge. A divided skirt and loose jacket worn with a shirtwaist. Her jaunty little cap to match, pulled over her tawny head, jerked a little to one side, seemed to invite the approval or disapproval of those "privileged" (as Aunt Kate considered it) to gaze on her.

Most girl bicyclists wore blue or grey outfits, and Parepa felt quite "set up" in this attractive different suit. She was all animation and delight as she met Frances on the front steps. Uncle George and Aunt Kate, glowing with pride, waved them a long farewell.

Frances wore a cycling suit of gray check knickers, white shirt, and an unmistakably red tie that floated over his shoulder in the breeze. His skullcap fitted tightly to his head. They were both good cyclists. Parepa had learned on Aunt Kate's old one before they had traded it in on a tandem, which she and Uncle George enjoyed together.

The morning air and exercise put color in these children's cheeks and sparkle in their eyes. As they sat at the little breakfast table, each thought the other the most beautiful person in the world. After eating they strolled to the river and stood whispering little nothings in each other's ears until time to start home.

"It's been a wonderful morning, Parepa; just being alone with you. I'll think of it many times when we are so far apart. Darling, I love you more than I ever thought it possible for a boy to love a girl." He grabbed her to him with eagerness which Parepa knew was prompted only by pure unadulterated affection. She turned her sweet face to his and they stood in one long embrace.

Suddenly they heard a snicker—then another—springing apart and looking in the direction from which it came, they saw the beaming, laughing faces of a little girl and boy. One peeping from each side of the big old tree in the shade of which the young lovers were cooing.

"You little rascals," said Frances.

"Te-he-he, we saw you spoonin'. Te-he-he," they laughed.

"Here, take this and go home," said Frances as he put a coin in each chubby hand. They scampered off hand in hand up the sandy path toward the village store.

"Weren't they the cunningest things you ever saw? Dirty faces, bare feet, and all. I wanted to squeeze them both," laughed Parepa. "I just love kids."

Frances's face clouded. He stood quietly watching the ripples from the little pebbles he threw into the water. He said nothing for some time. He seemed deeply in thought. Finally he turned, saying, "I expect we better start home now. I must see Daddy at eleven, and then, you know, we are to have the afternoon in the park."

The trip home was made by a quieter, more subdued couple than had passed that way earlier in the morning. The sun was hot, the road sandy, and they felt dragged out and dirty from a mixture of perspiration and dust. "You must take a few hours' rest now, Parepa," he said, bidding her goodbye. "I'll call for you about four."

*Something is worrying him,* thought Parepa as she went into the house.
