# Chapter VI

August 28th, 1898, at eight o'clock Alice Parepa-Roza Wesley was actually launched on her industrial career in a big city. She was so shy, so quiet, so charmingly sweet and attentive to her duties (which were few) that she won the love and respect of all the other employes of the shop.

Carry Weber and Mattie Swanson were the "old girls" on the job and from them Parepa was to learn all the "in's and out's" of the candy shop business.

"Guess we'll clean the showcases and windows first," said Mattie. "Parepa, you can remove all the candy dishes, empty them and wash them while we clean the glass and put new papers on the bottoms of the showcases and window shelves." She showed Parepa very kindly just where to put the candy removed from dishes and how to clean and dry them. Parepa wondered if all her work would be so easy. Occasionally a customer sauntered in and one of the older girls would wait on them. "Someday I'll sell candy I suppose," she said to herself. "I wonder how long before they'll let me."

"Say, who's the new 'candy kid'?" said a young swain who had been chatting with Carry as she boxed a pound of chocolates for him.

"Hello there, Pretty, where did you come from?" he said speaking unmistakably to Parepa.

"That'll do, that'll do," said Uncle Will who was sitting quietly in his office cage, heretofore unnoticed. "My helpers shall not be addressed in so rude a manner," he said coming forward and the young rascal took his package and went sneaking out of the door. Parepa-Roza blushed a deep red much to the amusement of the other girls.

It was a blessing that Parepa did not realize how very attractive she was. However, Uncle Will was aware of the fact and made up his mind he would protect her from any advances made by the admiring masculine customers.

"Getting tired, Parepa?" he asked as he walked past her.

"Not a bit, Uncle Will, this is fun," she said giving him one of her sweetest smiles without knowing it. Uncle Will knew it though and the danger that smile might lead to if not carefully guarded.

"We will have to change our lunch hours a little, girls," he said addressing Carry and Mattie. "I think I'll give Carry from eleven to twelve, Parepa and I will go from twelve to one and Mattie can take her turn beginning at one."

"Aunt Kate put up a lunch for me, Uncle Will," said Parepa, "she thought best for me not to go to a restaurant alone."

"You won't have to, you'll go with me. Tell Aunt Kate I'll look after your welfare from eight to five," he said rather curtly and at twelve o'clock he brought Parepa's tam o'shanter to her saying: "Come on, dearie, out to lunch with your old uncle." He appraised her luscious beauty and eyed her appreciatively.

After they had gone out the two older girls stared at each other in blank amazement. "I see where our noses are broken," said Mattie, "but isn't she innocence personified? I see where those dimples and dancing eyes will bring the world to her feet, and 'great will be the fall.'"

"Yes," returned Carry, "and Mr. Slay will find his hands full if he tries to ward off the multitude." They both laughed and joked about their new accessory until Mr. Slay and Parepa came in.

Uncle Will had chosen a table in a secluded part of the room in which to eat and took this opportunity to warn Parepa of the dangers of life in a big city.

"You are a beautiful girl and it's hard sometimes for a pretty girl to keep a level head. You can stay in Peoria and work for me just as long as you keep yourself clean and sweet, but if you show signs of recklessness as so many young girls do, I shall have to turn you back to your father's care. We all love you, dearie, and want to see you have the best of times so don't be afraid, no matter who comes up in your life, to come to your old Uncle Will and tell me about it. Never be too modest to bring me the truth. I'll help you over any difficulties you may have. Will you give me your promise, Parepa?" he asked.

"Of course I will, Uncle Will, and thank you very much, but I can't imagine what troubles you think I might have. I have always noticed that when people have trouble they bring it on themselves and I'm not out looking for trouble."

"That's all right and I hope trouble never looks for you. Remember I have your promise," and Uncle Will proudly ushered this young attachment from the room. On entering the street he met an old friend. After greetings were exchanged his friend said, "And where did you get your young companion?"

"My niece from the country," came the short answer.

"Nice and fresh with the dew still sparkling, hey?" was the insinuating reply.

Parepa didn't know just what he meant but was glad when Uncle Will started on.

The afternoon was a time never to be forgotten by Parepa. The "older girls" decided to let her "dress" one of the back showcases by arranging the cheap candies to suit her own taste. They thought it would result in a panic of color without any thought of design, but much to their surprise and Uncle Will's delight the result was a dream in beautiful color arrangement. She laid out a rainbow across the case bottom which slanted slightly toward the front. She did her work deliberately and seemed to be so completely wrapped up in it that she didn't notice the others eyeing her enviously. When it was done she stood off some distance and gazed at it. Clapping her hands, her face radiant as she grabbed Uncle Will by the hand and led him up to see it.

"It's beautiful, Parepa. How did you ever think to make it like that?" he asked.

"Oh, I don't know only I simply love pretty things. I just crave to get my hands on anything beautiful. Sort of possessed, I guess. These candies are so pretty, Uncle Will. I'm so glad I'm here!" she said.

It was drawing near five o'clock now and Uncle Will suggested he stroll along with Parepa as he wanted to talk over some business with Uncle George. This was only a stall but it worked. He really didn't like to have this young girl on the street alone even in the daylight until she was better acquainted with the ways of the city. When they arrived Aunt Kate was waiting on the porch for them. She could hardly wait to hear Parepa's report of the day.

"Oh! Auntie Kate, I'm so happy!" she said, kissing her. "It's such fun!"

Then Uncle Will told about the rainbow in the showcase. "Prettiest piece of work I ever saw," he finished. "I'll have to watch out or some other shop will be trying to take my designer away from me."

"Don't you worry, Uncle Will, I'll never leave you," she laughed.

It was five-thirty now and it was planned to eat at six, rest until seven-thirty, then practice until eight-thirty, to bed at nine, up at six, breakfast at six-thirty, practice until seven-thirty, be at the shop by eight.

"That's a mighty well laid out program," said Uncle Will, "and should be carried out to the letter."

"We make an exception of Friday evening, we take some sort of recreation then. George is always here on Fridays so we chose that day for a good time," said Aunt Kate.

"Say, don't put up a lunch for her Kate, I'll see she gets a lunch with me. She really should go out at noon to get a little air so I told her she and I would get together. I'll pay for the meals."

"Guess I'll go now, seeing George isn't home," and Parepa watched the stoutly built handsome uncle disappear around the corner.

"You are all so good to me," she said, "and I'm going to be just as good to you."

"Of course you are, but don't you think we should get a letter off to your folks tonight?"

Parepa's face clouded a little as she thought of the possibility of her folks objecting to her staying. They went into the house to get supper ready. Parepa was quiet. Auntie suspected the reason. Nevertheless this letter had to be written and it was. Uncle George wrote one and Parepa wrote one. They were placed together in an envelope and Uncle George mailed it.

"I'll be out there again to stay all night about Thursday and this will give them time to make up their minds before I get there," he said.

"And won't I have to go with you?" asked Parepa.

"No, we have timed this thing so an answer can't possibly get back to us before I leave. I told them you'd stay here until I see them on my trip and talk it over then."

"I won't leave anything unsaid that should be said in favor of your staying, you can bet on that," and Uncle George chucked her under the chin.

"Now Parepa you go practice and George and I will do the dishes," said Aunt Kate.

She obeyed. As she was all concentration on her lesson she didn't hear the front door open quietly and someone slip up behind her putting both hands over her eyes and with a rousing kiss on her cheek say, "Guess who."

She knew it was Frances. When he took her hands from over her eyes he gave a ringing laugh that brought Mr. and Mrs. Slay from the kitchen.

Parepa was blushing a deep crimson. After greetings were exchanged Aunt Kate said, "I'm sorry Frances, but we have planned a definite time for Parepa's piano practice and it must not be interfered with. Won't you come into the kitchen with us until she finishes?"

"You bet. I'm sorry I was so rude," he said to Parepa as he passed her.

When the practice was over they all sat on the porch and joked and sang. Finally Parepa ventured to tell the thing she had been wanting to tell Frances all evening.

"Did you know I've got a job?" she said timidly.

"You? A job?" he laughed and pretended to fall off his chair. "What kind of a job did you get?"

"In a candy store, and I just love it," she said excitedly.

"Gee Whiz! I'll know now where to buy my candy."

"How long do you expect to be in Peoria, Frances?" asked Uncle George.

"Only a few weeks yet. We sail again the 25th of September." He changed the subject immediately by suggesting another tennis game for Friday evening. "You are all to be my guests at the Hotel Fey for six o'clock dinner. Remember Friday night at five-thirty I'll be here."

They agreed and he bade them all good night and went skipping across the street to the place where he boarded.

"He's pretty much sophisticated for one so young, isn't he, Kate? Must have had some experiences in his young life. Think of him being host to a dinner party at the Fey. We'll have to brush up on our table manners, won't we?" laughed Uncle George.

"I'm afraid I'll make him ashamed of me. I know so little about eating away from home," said Parepa.

Uncle George spoke up then, "Say little girl, you know you promised to help me pack up the premiums for this trip and don't forget. We'll do that tomorrow night so I can get an early start Thursday morning."

"Alright," she answered, and kept her word.

There was not one thing in that covered tea wagon that would not add a beauty touch, somewhere in a home.

Parepa rose at five the next morning to see Uncle George off. Aunt Kate and she waved a long goodbye. This trip would bring an answer from her parents that would be the turning point of her life. How she prayed that she might be allowed to stay. That night Parepa and Aunt Kate went through the practice hour together and then decided to take a short walk. As they turned the corner someone sprang between them grabbing both by the arm and laughing out, "I caught you this time." It was Frances, of course.

"Where are you headed for?" he asked.

"Just out for a walk around the block," said Aunt Kate. "Where are you going yourself?" she asked.

"Oh, just wherever you go," he said pertinently. "I have to kill time some way. Let's stop in here and have some ice cream," and he swung them into a stylish eating place before they could object.

After they were seated and the order given he turned to Parepa and said, "Well, Peaches and Cream, how did the candy store treat you today?"

"If you are speaking to me, I'm Parepa-Roza and not sliced fruit. The candy store was great. I like it better every day."

Frances gave a jolly laugh and reached over and pinched Parepa's arm. She slapped at him just as she would have big brother at home.

Aunt Kate watched this proceeding and wondered if it wasn't just as well that Frances's stay in the city was limited. "They are getting acquainted so fast," she thought, "but after all young people must know and have young associates and Frances's fun seems only natural for one sixteen."

"Say folks," Frances was saying, "If it rains tomorrow night we'll go to a show at the Grand Opera House after dinner. I've ordered the dinner and the carriage and will be on time so don't forget."

"All right," said Aunt Kate.

After Frances left them on the porch Aunt Kate suggested that they sleep together in Parepa's bed. "It won't be so lonesome," she said.

After the lights were out she cuddled up to Parepa and told her how much she and Uncle George loved her and how they hoped she would always be careful of herself and not get too familiar with strange girls and boys. She told her of some of the pitfalls she must watch for.

Aunt Kate realized better than this young girl ever would that her modest charms, physical and intellectual, made a peculiar appeal to the opposite sex as well as being the envy of her own gender.

Since her stay with them Aunt Kate was often surprised and pleased at the superintelligence unfolding in her. She knew from the quick and precise way she mastered her music lessons there was no doubt that anything she might undertake would be a success.

"You must try to satisfy yourself with your job and music for a few years at least. By that time you'll be experienced enough in life to choose and judge your associates and pleasures. You know we will see to it that you have all the pleasures and companionship a girl your age needs to stimulate them in the meantime," said Aunt Kate giving Parepa a hug and kiss.

"Oh! Aunt Kate you are all so good to me. I wouldn't do one thing to make you sorry for anything."

"Good night, dear, and sweet dreams," said Aunt Kate.

"Nightie night," echoed Parepa and they were soon in dreamland. But before Parepa slept she offered up a special prayer that Mamma and Papa would let her stay here where she was so happy.

The next day was an anxious one for Parepa. She could hardly wait until time to go home and hear Uncle George's message from home.

It was four-thirty now and turning her eyes from the clock they fell directly upon Uncle George.

"Hello, dear, I couldn't wait until you came home. Just had to come for you tonight to let you know you are to stay with us."

"Oh! Oh! Oh!" laughed Parepa as she danced from behind the counter and threw herself in Uncle George's arms, kissing him over and over again. "You good old fellow," she said.

By this time the customers and other clerks stood amazed. Parepa in her excitement hadn't even noticed her audience or given them a thought. When it finally dawned on her that she had made a scene she blushed shyly and crept behind the counter again offering to wait on a young man who had "seen it all."

"Say, sweetie, do you often take a spell like that? I wish I'd been the fellow attacked," he teased.

"That's my good uncle and he brought me some great news. Did you say gumdrops?" she asked changing the subject.

"No, you misunderstood me. I said kisses," he laughed.

"How many?" she blushed deeper.

"All you got. Take them on the installment plan — all that's good for a fellow tonight and so on as long as you have any," the bold thing answered.

"Here's a nickel's worth," she said handing him the sack half full of the cheapest candy kisses in the store.

He tried to joke with her but she turned to an old lady and very sweetly gave her undivided attention to putting up her box of fine candies.

Uncle George stood back in the store and pretended not to notice the above proceedings. Nevertheless he missed none of it and was pleased at the way Parepa handled the situation.

He told Aunt Kate about it after they went home from the store. "Don't think we need to worry much about her not being able to take care of herself," he said.

It was starting to rain now and this was the night Frances was going to take them out to dinner. Parepa was half glad for now she could wear the pretty pink frock and big leghorn hat for which heretofore no opportunity had presented itself.

"I bet Frances will like it," she said as Aunt Kate straightened the ruffles, tied the long pink satin sash in a bow, and put the finishing touches to her toilet.

Parepa had acquired a grace of carriage that was astonishing in so short a time. No one to look at this perfectly formed, correctly dressed, young beauty would have guessed that she was only a few weeks removed from the farm.
