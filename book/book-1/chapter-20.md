# Chapter XX

One evening before sunset Frances rented a small rowboat at the foot of Hayward Street, and they rowed out to the middle of the river. There they lifted the oars and drifted—drifted—drifted where they may, for several hours. The round red sun sank behind the skyline and the moon rose over the eastern horizon, full and beautiful. The stars shone like diamonds. The city's lights danced and sparkled like so many jewels set in black onyx. Once in a while the shrill whistle of a locomotive would cut through the silence which made this evening and location so irresistible to these two young lovers. For lovers they were, and neither tried to argue themselves out of it.

Parepa lay limp and trusting in the arms of the one she loved. She was happy—Oh, so happy! Frances was all in the world she wanted, and the bliss that was hers as she gave herself up completely to his ardent kisses and caresses was the bliss of a clean young lover.

"Parepa, darling, you are all in this world I have to love. I love you so much I am a coward, a coward about telling you some things you must know before I dare to ask you to be mine forever. Forgive me, dearest, for never having told you before. I have wanted to, but I couldn't bring myself to do it." He held her closer, kissing her lips, her hair, her eyelids passionately. "Can you listen to my story now, dear?"

"Oh, Frances, please. I never want to listen to it. I never want to know. All I want is all your love, all of you. I care nothing for your story," and her eyes, turned to his, pleaded for her.

"You will have to know someday, dearest. I am not a brute, and I will not take your innocent love unless you will listen to my story from beginning to end. Are you ready?"

"No, I will never be ready. All I want to know is this. Did you ever kill anybody?" she asked.

"No, oh no. You have the wrong idea."

"Did you ever steal?"

"No, dear, you don't understand."

"Did you ever love a woman before?"

"Oh no, never, and I never will, but let me tell you—"

"Did you ever bear false witness? Now think hard, Frances."

"No, darling, never. You don't understand at all."

"I don't care to understand anything more. Oh, Frances, can't we go on as we are, never giving a thought to your past, whatever it may be? Can't you see it is you—just you—that I love? *Please* promise me you will never refer to this again."

"I wish I could, darling. Oh God, how I wish I could, but it is not only my past but my present and future. For your sake I must tell you. It's here, it's all here," and he pulled from his breast a large gold locket fastened around his neck with a long thin gold chain. It glistened in the moonlight as he dangled it before her. "It was my mother's," he said.

"Put it back, put it right back. Keep your secret, but give me all your love, take mine in return, and I shall be happy. Oh, so happy, Frances."

"I would give anything in the world if I could take your love on those terms, but I can't, I won't!" he said.

"Please! Oh please! Do not mention this again. See, Frances, look, here comes the Bald Eagle. Let's ride the waves," and she clapped her hands in glee.

"Fine!" and he took the oars and rowed near the big excursion boat. Soon they were surging up and down on the waves, as happy as two little children—which they were.

"I've got a wonderful yacht at home. Bet you can't guess what name is on it," he laughed.

"Nope," she said, "what is it?"

"Alice Parepa-Roza," he giggled. "Everyone wants to know what it means," he said.

"Tell me all about it, Frances, and all about all the nice things you have at home. *That's* what *I* like to hear about," and she patted his hand so lovingly he forgot his worries and launched on the subject she had mentioned.

"Really, darling, I guess I never have told you much about my life at home. In the first place, I have so many places to call home I hardly know which is really it. None of them are equal to your home, though, Parepa. Yours is ideal—mine is cold and—empty. Folks call it beautiful—it is—to look at."

"Oh, go on, Frances, tell me all about it!" she cuddled closer to him and turned her face to his for a kiss.

"I know what you want to hear about first—the garden," he said.

"Oh, yes, the garden! Tell me all about it. I didn't know you had one."

"They say my gardens are the most beautiful in England. Daddy Wheeler took great pride in laying out my estate there before I was old enough to know much about it, and now it has reached the peak of beauty. The trees and shrubbery are old enough to present a stately appearance, and the drives are bordered with the richest combination of growing things. There is a large lake surrounded by a wide drive. This is edged with Japanese cherries and in blossom time is a sight never to be forgotten. The rock gardens are the talk of all England. We have the best gardeners to be had. Some of them have been there nearly twenty years. My stables you'd like too. Every steed has his daily shower."

"What do they do with them when you are away?" she asked.

"Oh, they are taken care of just the same. My stable men love those horses as their lives."

"How big is your lot anyway?" she asked.

"You mean *estate*, dear. It's much larger than your beautiful Glen Oak Park. There are many more servants employed to run it than your park."

"Oh, I can't imagine how you have kept so still about it all. Say, where were you born, in England or America?" she asked as if the thought had just struck her.

"In mid-ocean," he replied. "That's part of my story—I."

"Oh, it must be awful late, Frances, let's row to shore at once," she exclaimed, wishing to change the subject. "I was so happy I forgot all about everything else."

"I'm so glad you're happy, darling, and I hope you will be all your life. Dearest, will you promise me one thing—that if our lives should have to be separated, you will let me help you out of any difficulty you may be in? I am *very* wealthy, Parepa, money means nothing to me only as I see it doing some good for some deserving person. I never think of this money as mine. Only mine to use for the good of the people. I have done a great deal for poor children. That is my hobby. I always make my gifts anonymous. I want no credit for anything I do."

"Oh, Frances, I'm so glad to hear you say all these things. I love children, and it's so good of you to help them. I hope I'll have a dozen children someday. I adore twins! I use to match my dolls up and call them twins. I had Ella and Della, and Minnie and Tillie, and—"

"Didn't you have any boys?" asked Frances.

"Oh yes, Freddie and Eddie. They were girl dolls, but I put pants on them and cut their hair and made them into boys. Wasn't that funny?" and she laughed loud.

They were on shore now, and Frances suggested they sit down on a big log and visit some more. After he was seated he took her in his arms and said, "Parepa, you are the only little child I want. I want you so badly to be mine—all mine as long as I live. Could you give yourself to me, darling, if you knew I could never be the father of your children?"

Parepa was startled—she had never thought of him as the father of her children. All she had cared for was his love—his wonderful love.

"Yes, Frances, dear. It's you, just you, I want. Your love will satisfy me forever. I am yours—yours. Oh Frances, I'm so happy," and she became limp in his arms.

He covered her with kisses, hugged her to him closer and closer until she felt the pressure of the round hard locket on his breast.

"I am all yours, and you are all mine now and forever, no matter what happens," he said. "Is that right, Parepa?"

"Right!" she said, and then, as if struck by a sudden impulse, she sprang from his arms and cried, "Catch me if you can," she ran along the beach like a sprite, laughing, waving her arms, and turning as she ran. Frances ran after her, catching the spirit of the game. Finally he caught her and carried her a few steps toward the river, pretending he would throw her in.

"Let's go home and be good," cried Parepa. "Uncle George will be walking the streets for us."

They walked home silently, arm in arm, so happy each with the other that when the parting time came, they vowed again their love and promise.

"You are all mine," said Frances.

"And you are all mine," said Parepa.

"Good night."
