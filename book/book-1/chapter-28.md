# Chapter XXVIII

Joseph was silent for some time. Finally he said tenderly, "You won't think I'm trying to change your mind if I tell you I think you should listen to him. No doubt he is right, you should know. No real man would ask a girl that he really and truly loved to marry him without telling her all about his past and his plans for the future. Perhaps it is something that may affect your happiness in later life."

"You can't judge a book by its cover. Nor even your very best lover," said Joseph. "I must insist in all fairness to yourself and—myself—that you hear his story. Will you promise?" He took both her hands into his. "For my sake—and his sake and your own sweet sake, to listen?" drawing her closer to him, he kissed her.

She did not return his kiss, nor did she promise. "What I wanted most to talk to you about tonight was this," and she sat up straight before him. "You know Frances is immensely wealthy. He thinks I can be a great help to him in spending. I think it will be great sport myself."

"Yes," said Joseph, "I would think so, but I never heard tell of such a thing. Where did he get the big idea?"

"I think from his mother before she died," answered Parepa. "I've been thinking maybe we could help you out with college funds. You would have more time for study, you know, if you didn't have to work your way through."

"That's mighty kind of you, Angel, but you are mistaken if you think I'm asking help from anyone, much less the husband of the only girl I ever cared for. You must remember I'm suffering every minute I'm with you, darling. I never could stand it to feel I was benefited by your husband. I know you can't understand. You are so good, and no doubt this lover of yours is too, but I only wish you belonged to me as you do to him."

"Now, Joseph, someday you will find someone that will make you happier than I could. Anyway, let's not talk any longer. I'm sorry if I've made you feel badly, I only wanted to be kind," and she rose to leave, turning to him she said, "You don't care if I ask you to leave me alone when we get to Hayward Street, do you, Joseph? I want to go home alone."

"Certainly not, if you insist, but you are a funny girl," he said. Frances heard her say, "I know I am, Joe," then they disappeared down the walk in the park that leads to Perry Street.

Parepa hurried down the five blocks of Hayward Street to the river. She was lonesome for the memories of the night she had spent two years ago with Frances on the river. She was a little chilly now and, throwing her long blue cape around her shoulders, she crouched down beside the big log on which she was sure she and Frances had sat in the light of the moon and stars that night of mad love which Parepa held dearest of all.

It all came back to her now. The boat ride, the moon, the songs they sang, the love pledges, this log on which they sat when first she saw the mysterious locket. "Frances! Oh, Frances!" she cried in a low murmur, "I love you, I love you."

Surely she heard someone coming. Yes, she could see the form of a man coming closer and closer. "Was it?—Could it be—Frances?"
