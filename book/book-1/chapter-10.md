# Chapter X

"You know the man that asked me to sing in the junior choir Sunday? Well, he came into the store and said he would send his son for me Thursday evening," said Parepa.

"That was kind of him I'm sure," Aunt Kate answered. "I think I'll go over too. I'm real interested in getting you started right. You have the making of a beautiful contralto voice and I'm going to see that you are placed where it will receive the most benefit."

"I'm glad you are going. I was dreading it a little," and Parepa leaned over and placed a loud smack on Aunt Kate's cheek.

They did up the few dishes and Aunt Kate suggested they sing awhile.

"Let's sing some of the songs I used to sing in the timber when I went for the cows," said Parepa. "I liked to hear my echo."

"What were they?" quizzed Aunt Kate.

"Oh, 'My Country 'Tis of Thee,' 'Almost Persuaded,' 'Star Spangled Banner,' and I used to like to sing best of all 'In the Green Pastures and by the Still Waters.'"

"All right that will be heaps of fun. You just imagine you are down in the field now."

"And barefooted," laughed the young girl. "I use to sing 'Pull for the Shore' when I waded through the creeks. The last one I learned was 'Just One Girl.'"

Aunt Kate took her place at the piano and they sang one song after another until both their throats were tired.

"That's the most fun I've had in a long time," said Aunt Kate. "Wish Uncle George could have heard us. Parepa, do you know you are going to be a great singer someday?"

"I always thought I wanted to be an elocutionist, I always took the prizes at the country contests," she replied.

"Oh, speak some of your pieces now," begged Aunt Kate. "I didn't know you could do anything like that."

Parepa launched out with "How Sockery Set a Hen" followed by "The Inventor's Wife," "The Stolen Watermelon," and then to Aunt Kate's surprise she gave a most wonderful presentation of "Flying Jim's Last Leap." This dramatic reading brought tears to Aunt Kate's eyes. The hungry beggar scene, the fight between Little Flossie's father and the tramp, the fire, the saving of Little Flossie's life by "Flying Jim," the tramp and the death scene of Flying Jim as Flossie's father begged his forgiveness were portraited so clearly by this beautiful young girl that Aunt Kate knew at once that Parepa was right. She should be a public speaker.

"I love to speak and the more people there are to listen the better I speak."

"You couldn't have been any better if a thousand had been listening," said Aunt Kate. "We'll have another surprise for Uncle George when he gets home."

They went to bed early. Aunt Kate thanked God that night for this wonderful child entrusted to her and begged him for guidance in directing her rightly.

When Thursday evening came Charles Blough called for them to go to choir practice. He was a smart, good looking young chap, full of fun and as usual was the hardest one in the bunch for his father to hold the attention of.

The professor greeted Aunt Kate, and while Parepa was being introduced to the others, she and he discussed Parepa's possibilities.

"You could be a great help to me, Mrs. Slay, if you could come with Parepa every night," he said.

"I'd love to," she said and kept her word.

The next day Aunt Kate called her brother-in-law, Mr. Wm Slay, to her home for a private talk about Parepa's future. "If she takes up dramatics she will necessarily have to have at least two hours more a day," said Aunt Kate. "You know whether or not you can spare her from the candy shop."

"She has certainly made good there," he said. "But I will try to make arrangement whereby she can have an extra hour in the morning and get off earlier in evening. I had hoped to raise her wages soon but can't if I give her more time."

"No, we wouldn't ask you to do that. We don't charge her any board nor for the lessons I give her, but the elocution lessons as she calls them will be high as we want her to have the best teacher possible."

"Mrs. Rose Mendenhall Breen is the best private instructor in the state," said Uncle Will. "If I were you I'd go and see her at once."

On Saturday morning after Parepa had gone to the shop Uncle George and Aunt Kate started out to find a suitable teacher for Parepa. They found Mrs. Breen much interested in taking her but the lessons would have to be given twice a week in one hour periods. They were to cost two dollars per lesson. She was to be given a complete course which would require a great deal of home study. They went home to talk it over with Parepa before deciding.

At supper they told her they had a big surprise for her. She must guess what it is.

"Another letter from Frances?"

"No."

"Some beads?"

"No, no, no. You'll never guess it."

"What in the world can it be, Uncle George?"

"What would you say if I'd tell you we have found an elocution teacher for you?"

"Oh! Oh!" she said jumping up and clapping her hands. "Really! Where is she?"

"Well, first you will have to decide whether or not you want to pay the price. It comes pretty high, Parepa, and Aunt Kate and I can't afford to pay for the lessons much as we would like to."

"I'm afraid I won't have time to study, with my music."

"Your Uncle Will and I have settled the time question," spoke up Aunt Kate. "You can take your lessons from seven-forty-five to eight forty-five two mornings a week. It is about a ten minutes' walk to the shop from her studio. It will cost you four dollars a week. That will only leave you one dollar to yourself."

"Do you think I could buy my clothes on that?" she said wistfully.

"No, but if you will be satisfied to wear what I can make over for you I believe we can make a go of it," Aunt Kate replied.

"I'd like too much to try anyway," said the delighted girl. "Maybe someday I can pay you good folks back for all you are doing for me. Oh, I'm so happy!" and she kissed one and then the other until there was no doubt of her love for them.

The only break in the above schedule came on lesson days and choir practice. It was a busy happy girl that hurried from one duty to another always finding time to smile and brighten those about her with her ready wit which was fast manifesting itself as she became better acquainted.

"It's worth the price of the lessons just to have her around," Mrs. Breen confided to Mrs. Slay when she had called to inquire about her progress.

"She is a natural actress and no doubt about that."
