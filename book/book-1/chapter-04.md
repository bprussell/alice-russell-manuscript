# Chapter IV

At 5:30 she went to the bed and stood looking down at Parepa for some time. "Our Maude would have been sixteen now," she said half aloud and half to herself, then she bent over and kissed the warm cheek of the "sleeping beauty."

Parepa sat up sleepily rubbing her eyes with both fists as a two year old would do. "Did I go to sleep?" she asked.

"Yes, dear, and Uncle George and I have the picnic lunch all packed. I'm glad you had such a fine rest. You'll beat us all at tennis for that, I suppose," she said. "Now come into the bathroom and take a plunge. I've drawn your water and then I'll comb your hair and help you dress. Which dress do you think would be the best to wear tonight, Parepa?" Aunt Kate asked just to see if the girl had any idea of the propriety of dress.

"I'd like to wear the blue one and the tam o'shanter if you will let me," she said to Aunt Kate's surprise. She thought the pink or white would probably be the silly choice of this young inexperienced country girl.

"That's just exactly what I hoped you'd choose," she answered. "And the blue ribbon to match."

It was a proud pair that descended the stairs some minutes later.

"You better sit on the porch with me and read a little till Frances comes over, Parepa, and then we'll go," said Uncle George wishing lots of people would pass and gaze on their handiwork of the day.

She placed herself proudly, book in hand, in a large chair on the porch but if one had observed her closely they would have noticed that her eyes cast toward the big house on the corner across the street. She knew she was most as "pretty" as any of the children her age that passed the house and she was really anxious for Frances to see her before she got "mussed up." She twisted her handkerchief in her fingers thoughtlessly. Finally she saw him coming. What was that he had in his hand? He had on a blue suit, too, and a cap something like hers!

"Hello, Mr. Slay, Hello Parepa-Roza. Gee! Whiz! but you're pretty," he said as he pulled her book away from her. "Stand up, and let's see how big you are."

Parepa obeyed and the frank young boy backed up to her and said, "Look here, Mr. Slay, who's the tallest?" Parepa blushed a deep red but it had no effect on our hero.

"Oh, you got her beat an inch in height, but I bet she weights the most," said Uncle George.

"I bet I can lift you," declared the progressive Frances and straightway proceeded to prove it.

"Show him you can lift him too, Parepa," said Uncle George, and there was nothing else for the embarrassed girl to do but "lift."

Aunt Kate appeared at the door and suggested they all take what there was to carry and start for the streetcar which was to take them to the park. Each carried a racket and parcel. Parepa was awkward about it so Frances took her racket and walked alongside of her keeping up a continual string of questions and chatter.

Uncle George and Aunt Kate were amused and happy. "Couldn't have found a better companion for her. He'll soon take off the rough edges," said Uncle George. He sat in the seat behind them on the streetcar and noted their young smiling faces as Frances talked a stream because he wanted to and Parepa answered his questions because she had to. She was beginning to limber up some though, and he could see her shy glances toward her boy friend, the dimples playing a great part in her attractiveness. "Hanged if she ain't the prettiest thing I ever saw," he thought. "We'll keep her alright if John and Eliza can be convenienced."

Aunt Kate sat with her eyes riveted on the picture in the seat in front of them. She too was dreaming—"Our girl! Our Maude. Parepa *must* stay and take her place."

Now Parepa was volunteering a whole sentence. Not in answer to a question but was actually making a statement telling Frances something she wanted him to know. Her face was all animation and she was motioning to make it more impressive. He was laughing with her and when she had finished they both gave vent to a loud giggle.

By this time all eyes in the car were upon them but they didn't seem to know that even Uncle and Auntie were watching, so wrapped were they in their own enjoyment.

By the time they reached their destination these children were as lively and alert as crickets. Parepa seemed to have lost all her restraint after the picnic lunch was over and gave herself up to the game they were teaching her. It was great fun and she proved herself an apt scholar.

"We'll come over real often, so you won't forget all you've learned," said Uncle George as they waited for the streetcar to take them home.

"You see I'll only be here two weeks unless I get a job, and maybe if I do Papa and Mamma won't let me stay," said the girl thoughtfully. "Let's look for a job tomorrow."

"A job! You a job!" laughed Frances. "Gee Whiz, what could you do?" and he roared with laughter. "There's no cows to milk in Peoria."

Parepa blushed again but she realized her inability to do much else but milk cows.

"Of course I'll have to start at the bottom of whatever I do, but I can learn most anything I believe."

The car came now and after they were seated the conversation went back to "her job." Frances couldn't imagine her working at anything. He liked her extremely and wanted her to "play with" so to speak.

This was the first time in the boy's life he was alone to make his own friends and to do as he wanted with his time. "He must be in by ten o'clock" was his guardian's strict orders to the old people with whom he was trusted. He seldom referred to his past and when he did would halt his conversation as if he had told more than he intended to. They learned this much—that he had crossed the ocean eight times. How much time was spent on either side could only be guessed at. That he had never seen his father or mother was another fact secured on one occasion. "My father died more than eight months before I was born and my mother three minutes before," he had said then shut up like a clam.

The next day Parepa was in deep thought. She wondered how anyone could ever live without a father and mother. Her sympathies were aroused for this orphan boy and she determined to do all she could to make him happy. "He must have a big brother" she said to Aunt Kate, "because he said that first night that Clem scrubbed his white ponies every day."

Aunt Kate explained to her that the "Clem" spoken of was probably the stable man who looked after the horses.

"And Aunt Kate, what country do pedigreed horses come from? He said his were all pedigreed," asked Parepa not caring to expose her ignorance to him on that subject again.

Aunt Kate explained as best she could and the subject was changed.

"I think I ought to be getting a job tomorrow, you see Papa won't let me stay I know if I don't already have my job when we write to ask him."

"Your Uncle George is going to see about one for you. He thinks Uncle Will will be able to take you into his candy store. Would you like that?" asked Aunt Kate smiling. "They sell candy, ice cream, and sodas there."

"Oh! do you really think I could learn to do that?" she asked clapping her hands for joy. "I'd love it. The candies are so pretty. Aunt Kate why do you suppose I always love pretty things? If anything is pretty that's all I care."

"I don't know my child but it's not a bad failing. Uncle George thought your father might not object if you are working with relatives. Uncle Will will be good to you and see that you are taught the right way to do things."

The only job Parepa had even hoped to land was in someone's household and now just think—a candy store job! Wonderful! The "pretty" candies in the windows had always attracted Parepa's attention. Not that she cared to eat them—they were too "pretty" to eat.

The next day it was settled. Uncle Will could pay her what Parepa thought an enormous wage. Five dollars for six days per week omitting Sundays as he knew she or her folks would never consent to working on Sunday. She was to be at the store by eight in the morning and go home at five in the evening.

Aunt Kate had something to do with the arrangement of time because she was planning a regular course of music lessons for Parepa which would take two full hours' practice daily.

"You can just as well make use of the time between six-thirty and seven-thirty in the morning and seven-thirty and eight-thirty in the evening," Aunt Kate told her, "And I'll teach you and board you free."

Parepa was so happy she hardly knew what to do. "A job and music lessons both at once," she clapped her hands and then rushed to Aunt Kate throwing herself in her arms and kissing her. "You and Uncle George are so good to me, but what can I do for you?" she asked looking troubled.

"Just be a *good*, *good* girl always and we shall feel as if we had been more than repaid," and Aunt Kate patted her tawny head lovingly.

This girl had everything to learn. Her simple trust in people, her untarnished character, her winsome ways and sweet disposition together with a quick alert mind had completely won the hearts of her middle aged Uncle and Aunt. She was so unspoiled in every way and was beginning to disclose a very witty and jovial temperament.
