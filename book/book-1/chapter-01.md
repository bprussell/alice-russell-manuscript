# Chapter I

"Alice Parepa-Roza, where have you been and what have you been doing?" said John Wesley, the stern father of the house of Wesley.

"Down in the pasture," returned the tawny haired, freckled-faced, dimple cheeked, ten-year old, in question.

"What do you mean by staying out in the pasture 'till this time of night? We've had supper two hours ago and you don't get any. I asked you what were you doing?"

"I was watching the clouds run through the sky until dark. Oh Papa, I just couldn't come home, and then I watched the stars and moon. Mother said if I did all my work up nice I could go visiting the Burnett girls or whatever I wanted to do this afternoon. I took the old shawl and went way down by the creek where I could be all alone and laid down on my back and had the *most* fun in the world just watching the clouds chase round. Oh, Papa, once there was a *big* drove of cattle that just *tore* 'cross the sky and finally ran into a *big* woods. Then I saw the most beautiful castle. There were rich ladies and children in the yards and I saw one little girl run after her dog clear down a *big* hill and, Oh Papa, I just wish you could have seen the *big* battle. The smoke just rolled up and the soldiers fell and one of the *big* strong ones I called you, and when it got dark and the little stars began to peep—"

"There now, that's enough. Go right to bed and never let me hear of such foolishness again."

Parepa-Roza some way couldn't make herself feel as if she were being punished because the memory of having spent the afternoon "just as she wished" was recompense enough for the supper she missed.

This child liked to be alone with her thoughts and would work hard and long if only she were promised a few hours to spend "as she liked." She always "liked" to be alone. Never once would she choose to visit her playmates if she had her way.

"I can't understand the child," her kind mother would say, and her father would add, "she's an odd one. Smart as a whip but so different from the others."

She was a beautiful child despite her freckles, chubby form, and curly reddish hair.

It was the custom of her parents to go each Saturday afternoon to Chillicothe, a small town some miles distant, to do the family trading and "take in the produce of the farm to exchange for necessities." The rule was that one of the six children of the household was to go with them, by turns, each week.

Parepa-Roza seldom cared to go but would give her "turn" to an older sister, whose hobby was going to town, whom she liked to get off the place thus assuring her a better chance to do the things she could "put over" better when this watchful bossy "big sister" was out of hearing and seeing distance.

Many times Parepa-Roza would watch until the old "rig" drawn by two old gray horses was well out of sight over the "big hill" before she would venture to try out some coveted "stunt" which had been brewing in her mind since the last Saturday.

One day it was to climb high up a tall maple tree and out on a slender limb that would swing and dip and sway in the breeze. She imagined herself a bird, maybe an eagle, or maybe an oriole. Anyway she was *always* something that she wasn't. "Oh, if I were only a boy. God made a great mistake," she would say. Another time it was the big swing made of hay rope that hung from the tops of two maples. If one dared try they could swing so high they could see clear over the "big hill". "One" did try real often when the folks had gone to town. Though the rule was no one was to swing past the fence.

Then that old trunk in the attic that Dad had before he was married. What a store of things there was in that to be investigated. Parepa knew some of them: the hardtack like Papa had to eat when in the Civil War, one of Grandma's dresses with fringe on it, an old bracelet, some red and silver glass vases Mamma's schoolchildren had given her, and Oh, Papa's and Mamma's love letters, and so many things that she had seen but never really *felt* with her own hands. She knew the key was kept in the old Seth Thomas Clock which stood on the shelf in the dining room, where no one was supposed to even look for anything.

She never took anyone into her confidence. Always choosing to be alone she was exceptionally fortunate in not being caught for she planned well and took no risks.

She had few real chums at school for she held strictly to the rule of "Friendly with all, but familiar with few." Everybody liked her and would gladly have been a pal, if not her slave, had they gotten any encouragement. She lived a distance of a mile from school and so fond was she of her own company that when she had an especially interesting subject to work out would miss a spelling word or two so she would be kept after school until the other children were well on their way home.

It was not always mischief that was on this child's mind. Often questions arose that were almost incredible for such a young mind. She invariably chose to figure the answers out for herself.

She was a very attentive churchgoer, never missing a meeting 'though she with the other five Wesleys usually had to walk a mile each way unless some kind neighbor offered them a ride in their surrey, as was often the case.

They liked to question this young girl for when she answered, as one neighbor said of her, "she always says something."

She grew finally to a beautiful girl of fourteen. When she finished grade school the question arose in the family just what was best to do with her. There were two sisters and one brother older and one brother and sister younger than she. The family finances, which were never quite equal to the demand, were insufficient to send her to high school. Anyway she didn't care to go to high school, she wanted to earn while she learned.

Finally one day in August, the 17th. to be exact, an Uncle from Peoria came on his regular two weeks' trip. He drove a team to a tea wagon which it was his business to drive through the country taking orders and delivering teas, coffees, spices, soaps, and sugar to the farm and small town people. He always stayed overnight at brother John's. When he learned that it was Parepa-Roza's birthday he was bound she was to go home with him for a vacation. After considerable discussion, pro and con, it was decided to let her go and stay two weeks which was the time for his regular return trip.

Such a hurrying around as there was; washing out and pressing this dress and that, hair ribbons, even big sister donated the use of some of her "nice things" for the trip. Though there was several years' difference in their ages Parepa had acquired the height and weight of Lavenia and could easily wear her clothes.

Early the next morning Uncle George was "hitched up" and after an extra amount of advice and warnings, say nothing of the kisses and goodbyes, the two of them were off for Peoria. As far back as Parepa could see she waved her hand, then down on the "other side" of the "big hill" they went out of sight to the home folks.

To them, Parepa was going on her vacation, the first one of John Wesley's children to ever be away from home without their folks; to Parepa she was going for a job. It had been in her mind for weeks but just how she was going to manage the "get away" had been her only worry. Now it had come about in such a natural way that Parepa pinched herself to be sure it was really she that was sitting upright on the hard rough seat of the covered tea wagon.

Uncle George tried to visit with her to keep her from getting lonesome. She was quiet but he was mistaken if he thought for one minute she was lonesome. She was real busy trying to plan the best way to broach the subject that was uppermost in her mind to Uncle George. Finally he made it easy for her by saying, "Do you know Parepa-Roza, your Auntie and I would just like to have a little girl like you to live with us all the time. Don't see why John needs so many kids and I just think we'll keep you in Peoria." Much to his surprise she clapped her hands and said, "Oh, Uncle George, would you really and truly keep me? I'll be so good if you only will."

Uncle George was so surprised he hardly knew what to say. Finally he said, "Of course, your folks will have to agree to it." Parepa's face fell. She knew her folks would never agree to it. "And if I stay can I work?"

"Work?" said Uncle George, "You work? You're not sixteen yet. The law wouldn't let you work."

"But, I'm big enough and everyone thinks I'm sixteen or seventeen," she said slowly. "Oh, Uncle George, can't you understand. I do so want to do something; to earn something and to learn something. I just can't stay home all my life. They don't need four girls there and I'm different from the others. I could just as well be earning something for my family."

"You're pretty young, my girl, but I'll see what can be done about it."

This was enough of a promise for now. Parepa-Roza could wait now that the subject was broached. But all the way to the city she kept her thoughts on her "job."

Finally Uncle George broke in with, "Now, just what do you think you could do in a big city?"

"I could learn to do most anything if I got paid for it." And Parepa-Roza's thoughts went back to all the pretty things she had wanted in her young life but there was only funds for the bare necessities and no extras for anyone.

Uncle George's mind was busy too. "Gosh, she's a pippin', having all this tucked away in her head and never peeping a word of it to anybody. Nice little thing too, wouldn't Kate be tickled to death to have her stay with us. I can just see her now dolling her up in some of her clothes made over to fit her. Kate's handy that way."

For a long way they rode in silence. Finally he drew up the reins at a farmhouse where he always fed his horses and got his dinner with the hospitable Jacksons.

Mrs Jackson came out to the wagon for some coffee which she declared they were out of and on seeing Parepa sitting on the seat said, "Well, where did you get the girl? Did someone trade her in on coffee and spices?"

"No, that's my girl," said Uncle George.

Parepa didn't correct him for she had begun to imagine herself as partly Uncle George's girl anyway and was so hungry for some dinner and a rest from riding that the small matter of who she belonged to was nothing in comparison.

Mrs Jackson called to her seventeen year old daughter to show Parepa where she could "wash up" and at once they were chattering away like they had always known each other. At the dinner table Mrs Jackson said, "I should judge your girl and our Ruth are about the same age, Ruth's sixteen."

"Just about," said Uncle George and changed the subject. Score one, thought Parepa-Roza. "From now on I'm sixteen."

The meal progressed with the usual bantering and jokes between Uncle George and Mrs Jackson. Parepa-Roza was quiet. She had "reclined within herself" as big brother use to say when she closed her mouth and ears to those around her. After Mrs Jackson had traded in her "tea tickets" for a kettle and a tin cup the two mounted the seat and were off on their last stretch homeward.
