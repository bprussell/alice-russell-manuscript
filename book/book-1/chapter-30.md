# Chapter XXX

Frances called Parepa by 'phone the next forenoon and arranged for a trip to the park. They went directly to the slope of the hill where they had sat together two years ago.

"There it is! There's the place!" cried Parepa almost running to it and throwing her cape on the grass she turned to Frances and said, "Welcome Home my dear one, thou hast come at last!" in a most dramatic way.

Frances laughed as they sat down on the soft grass in the shade of the large elm tree. For a long time they sat in silent understanding and peace.

"Would you like to see what's in the locket now, darling?" and pulling it from his breast dangled it before her eyes.

She took it in her hands warm from the heat of his flesh as she said, "Not just now Frances, I want to ask you a few questions, first. Why, do you insist upon my knowing?" and she turned her honest blue eyes to his.

"If I was to tell you you could not understand. You must see for yourself," and taking the locket from her he placed the key into the lock and turned it once forward twice backward and five clicks forward. It sprang open and Frances said, "We'll look at the photos first." He laid them in a row before her.

"Oh!" she cried. "They all have on the locket." As she picked each one up and scrutinized it carefully she commented on their features, attire, and hairdress. Growing serious and laughing heartily at times. She asked many questions about them as Frances introduced each one. "I love this one, Frances," she said as she raised it to her lips and kissed it. "Because it's your Mother and because she has a quality expressed in her features that is not so distinct in the others."

"What do you mean, darling? What is it you see there so plainly?" he almost shouted. So happy that she too discovered it.

"I think I'd call it determination or—or—belief in her own convictions," she hesitated as she watched her lover's face color. "Courage," she cried, "That's the word."

"That's it! That's it exactly," he cried. "And to think that you could find that trait. Parepa, you are a wonder."

After a long while she replaced each one on their side of the locket and clasped the glass over them. "Now what's on the other side?" she asked as if she might have been a little child interested in a scrapbook.

"That's it—my secret," he told her and she noticed he was trembling in voice and body. "Letters written by my mother to her unborn son. She wrote them from the time she knew I was to be, up to a week before her death. They are not all here, of course. So thoughtful was she of me and my future that she wrote her wishes to her old nurse who was to be my nurse too, and to Daddy Wheeler who she chose as my guardian. Everything concerning my future was planned with only one idea in view," he hesitated, made as to lift one of the letters from its hiding place when he stopped. "Do you want to read them darling?" he asked trembling.

"Do you want me to? I'll do whatever you say," and reaching over while saying it she closed the locket and taking the key from the lock snapped it locked. Replacing it in its familiar place she said, "Tell it to me, dearest, if you love me. Tell it to me with your own lips. I'd rather know it that way."

It was growing dusk now and throwing her long cape over both their shoulders, cuddling up close in his arms with her head on his shoulder she said, "Do—do you love me?"

He took her chin in his hand and turning her face to his he said, "Look in my eyes, dearest—what do you see?"

She could not answer for they sat in a tense embrace. Then as if suddenly reminded of his duty he said, "Parepa, my mother was a brave woman, much braver than her child. She knew the shame her ancestors as well as she herself had brought upon the name of Emmerson and society. Her mother had tried to correct it and resist the temptations that lead to disgrace. She had not the strength of character to overcome her weakness. Then my mother fell in love with Daddy Wheeler—"

"Oh, and is he your father?" exclaimed Parepa almost happily.

"No, I'm sorry to say he was not. Mr. Wheeler was ten years older than my mother and for that reason he would not confess the true love he had for her. As his love for her grew he decided to go to Germany and study music. While he was gone his father, who was my mother's guardian, placed my mother in a finishing school in the west.

"She imagined herself in love with a dashing young fellow handsome in face and manner. They sinned—my mother had forgotten her vow that she never would fall as her mothers before her had done. Such was the weakness of the flesh. The sins of the parents visit upon their children in this way. When my mother found her weakness and realized how impossible it was when born in the blood to overcome it she determined to end her disgraceful line, by bringing into the world a son. All the time she carried me near her heart she thought of me only as her son. She wrote to me and talked of me to her intimates (who were few) planning for her son a life of goodness, helpfulness, and purity. She seemed to realize from the first that it would be impossible for us both to survive and she bravely reconciled herself to the fact. Her family for years had accumulated vast fortunes in turpentine. It was her wish that my identity be lost but the fortune kept intact by my guardian that I might when old enough use it where it would do humanity the most good. This was to be my mission in life.

"All of the mothers whose faces you have seen tonight were kind and loving. Their sexual weakness being their only fault. It ruined the lives and name of my family. From the minute I was born every wish of my mother's has been carried out. My name which she gave me is Frances LaVern Emmerson. I have been raised a real fellow from the start. All the physical exercise for the proper development of a strong well formed boy was carefully adhered to. I grew to be an excellent sportsman, skilled in every form of athletic feats. I have won medals and many loving cups for swimming, tennis, bowling, vaulting, running, riding, driving, rowing, jumping, and discus throwing. I love my music. It is the most comforting accomplishment I have. I can always find pleasure in good books. No boy ever grew to manhood more carefully raised than I. From the time I was old enough to understand what a mother was I have missed her.

"She left letters for me to open on my birthday from the sixth one up to the fourteenth. Each one was written to interest a boy of that age. I was given the locket to wear on my fourteenth birthday. It has been a part of me ever since. The 'secret' was in it. Parepa, dearest, have you guessed it? Can you believe me?" and he whispered the truth in her ear. "Darling, I'm a Girl."

Up to this moment the girl had sat quietly, his arms around her, cheek against cheek as if afraid to stir for fear he might not go on with his story. Now she gave a start and a little cry of surprise. Then as if her whole being was enveloped in that most precious of all gifts—sympathy—she turned her lips to his and throwing her arms around his neck kissed him tenderly. "I love you—you are mine—mine forever in spite of fate."

Frances did not speak, he could not speak. He hated himself, hated all his blood, hated life, for the first time in his life he knew the true meaning of hate. But—there was one thing he did not hate. That was Parepa. Would he, should he keep on loving this human thing in his arms? Was it right? Was it right?

"Oh God help us! Help us to know and understand what is right that we should do," he cried out in despair.

"Let me answer that prayer, Frances, dear," said "Bethany's Angel." "Yes, for my sake—for your sake—and for his sake keep on loving me."

Then straightening up and facing him kindly she seemed to have survived the shock completely. "Do you remember, that nearly one thousand years ago, a Persian poet, Omar Khayyam wrote this poem:

> "Ah, Love could you and I with fate conspire,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To grasp this sorry scheme of things entire;  
> Would we not shatter it to bits and then,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Remould it, nearer to the heart's desire?"

"You, Frances, was your mother's love. She and fate conspired. They remoulded you 'nearer to the heart's desire.'"

"Yes, a real conspiracy in which I had no part," said Frances sadly. "In her letters to the nurse and Mr. Wheeler she requested them to always call her child a boy from the minute they saw him. Never even though he be born a girl allow themselves to think of him as such. He shall be a boy even against fate itself. He shall break this line of weakness and shame. He shall be a help to humanity, he shall relieve the suffering, he shall help the poor. He shall be a credit to the disgraced name of Emmerson."

Thus it was that from birth this child of yesterday was "shattered to bits" and "remoulded." He was spoken of as a boy, was reared as a boy, he played as a boy, was educated as a boy, he worked as a boy, he sang as a boy, and now he loved as a man.

"I did not know until I was fourteen years old that I was born a girl. The nurse and Mr. Wheeler were always so kind to me. I have never wanted to be a girl. Have never been suspected of being one or thought of myself as one. Having been raised a boy may or may not have been responsible for the refusal of nature to take its course, and at seventeen years of age it was necessary for me to submit to an operation ridding my body of female organs which were peculiarly formed and could not function. For three months I dressed as a girl at that time. My name being either girl or boy I was not suspicious at the hospital. It was while I was still recovering from this operation that I came to Peoria. After this operation my voice changed and became more masculine. After a year's shaving and the generous use of hair grower I had quite a growth of whiskers. I am masculine in every thought and action, dearest. I'm sure you understand now why I can never be the father of your children."

Parepa sat again as if petrified listening to this strange confession. Then turning her face to his and searching his eyes for a minute she said, "Are Mr. Wheeler, yourself, and I the only ones living who know your story, Frances?"

"Yes," he answered, "we are. Mr Wheeler's father knew and my nurse knew but they both died with their lips sealed."

"Then I shall never tell, never even unto death."

"Do you remember when you and I were sitting in the boat on the river two years ago you said that when you were a little girl you made twin boy dolls by cutting off the girl dolls' hair and putting pants on them? That's what they did to me. That's the kind of a boy I am," he said sadly.

She sat silently thinking.

He interrupted her thoughts by adding, "That's not the kind of a husband you want, my dear, nor the kind you shall have. I have fully made up my mind to leave Peoria tomorrow never to see you again." She clutched at his arm as if already trying to hold him, but said nothing. "It's best, you know darling, best for both of us. Someday you will be a proud mother, a real one, you know. I hope there will be lots of boys among them. Don't you, dear?" he asked hoping to hear her answer.

No answer came.

"When you told me that in your childish dream moments when you had everything beautiful, every wish granted, but could not remember anything about your husband I felt encouraged to think you might be happy with me just as a companion for life, but now I know it would never do. Promise me you'll transfer all your love for me, to the one who has a normal natural love for you—to Joseph."

She sat erect and faced him. A new light in her eyes, a new expression on her face. "No, not Joseph. It must be someone who knows nothing of this—of you. It's been a hard fight—I have won!" she said. "I shall begin all over again, but I shall never sing again. I place in your keeping my voice which only the inspiration of your love made possible. Take it, it is yours. From this on I shall be true to myself and to the God that gave me life. I shall never worship the imaginary again. I shall be a true woman in all ways—My Dreams are Over. We should each be richer for having had this experience. What are your plans for the future, Frances?" she asked.

"I have but two wishes concerning you," he answered. "One is that you be sure the man you marry is one whose blood you will be proud to have flow in your children's veins. The other is this: If ever there comes a time in your life, either early or late, that you want me, or that I can help you in any way you can always reach me by addressing me at Halifax, Nova Scotia. It will be forwarded to me wherever I am. Should you have a longing, whether next year or fifty years from now, to be my companion, remember your home in my heart will be vacant waiting for you to come live in it. I shall never love another woman in my life."

The next day found two young people making a new start in life. Each going their separate ways; for better or for worse.
