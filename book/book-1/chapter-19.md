# Chapter XIX

"First, have you noticed any change in Uncle George lately? I mean, have you noticed him coughing?"

"No!" exclaimed the surprised girl. She had not expected Uncle George's health to be the important subject of this confidential talk. She recovered her surprise and said, "Is he really coughing much, Aunt Kate?"

"Yes, dear, he is. He has tried to hide it. He has been doctoring for several months, and now the doctor thinks it advisable for him to give up his work and devote all his time to getting well. It may be advisable for him to change climate."

"Why, Aunt Kate! I never dreamed of such a thing. How could I have been so blind!" she cried.

"He has been very careful that you should not suspect. This will be his last trip in the tea wagon."

Parepa felt herself a heartless creature never to have noticed when her benefactors were in distress.

"He didn't want one little worry to interfere with your study. He loves you, Parepa—loves you as he would his own child, and so do I."

Parepa threw her arms around Aunt Kate and burst into a torrent of tears. "I've been so ungrateful!" she cried. "I'll not take another bit of help from you again. From this on I am going to be the wage earner in this family, and you and Uncle George are going to have a long rest and get well."

"There, there, child, I'm afraid I have frightened you. All he needs is time to cure him. You will be home all through the month of August, and I have been wondering if we couldn't take a trip somewhere," Aunt Kate said, "in celebration of your eighteenth birthday."

"Now, Aunt Kate, don't do all your planning for my pleasure. Frances is leaving for England in September, and I want to enjoy August with him. You know, Aunt Kate, he and I have the best times together. Like tonight—he was such good company. Isn't it strange that he and I ever met? It just happened, you know, that he boarded across the street."

"He said tonight that he has never found a friend he liked as well as he does me, and I know I'll never find one I'll like as well as I do him. You just happened to ask him over to supper that night, you know, because you felt sorry for him."

"Yes," smiled Aunt Kate, "he helped me home from the grocery store with my packages one day, and I became interested in him. He was so mannerly and attractive. After that I always took pains to talk with him when I met him on the street. That's how strangely things come about in this world. Did he ever tell you anything about himself, Parepa?"

"No, Aunt Kate, but I believe he wants to, and I think I'll let him someday. Every time he hints about himself, I change the subject. Some way I don't want to know. It might make a difference, you know."

"Yes, dear, it might, but if it will, the sooner you know it the better. There is undoubtedly a mystery somewhere. Neither he nor Mr. Wheeler tell much of their past. Do you know how old he is, Parepa?"

"No, Auntie, I don't. He never says, and I never think to ask him. I will, though, someday."

"Now, let's go to bed, dearie, and I hope you will dream sweet dreams. Good night," and with a tender kiss she left her.

That night Parepa had lots to think about. Her love for Frances was balanced by her anxiety for Uncle George. *I must help them all I can,* she thought. *They have spent lots of money on me, and I must pay it back. I can do it only by giving entertainments. That's what I'll do,* she decided. *I'll begin after Frances goes away in September. Mrs. Breen will help me get the engagements, I know.* This settled in her mind, she fell asleep.

The month of July passed with only a few evenings with Frances. He and Mr. Wheeler had business out of town and were gone several days at a time.

When he was in Peoria, he was more than attentive to Parepa.
