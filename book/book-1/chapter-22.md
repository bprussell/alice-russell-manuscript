# Chapter XXII

That afternoon found two young people sitting on a slope of the hill in a secluded spot of the park, thoroughly oblivious of the sighing of the breeze, the song of the birds, the beauty of the flowers, the trickling of the waters, or the distant strains of music wafting over the hill. These two lovers were alone. It was heavenly to be together; neither could ask more. They dreamed silently as if words were frail instruments of expression. Why bother with them? Just to sit with hands clasped, heads together, hearts beating as one. Why couldn't it always be this? The shadows were falling now, and the darkness seemed to beckon confidence.

"Parepa, dear, won't you listen to my story now? I *must* tell you for your sake and my sake. Come, darling, won't you listen?" His voice was trembling.

"Please, oh please," she said, "Frances my lover, I am so happy why let your secret worry you? It doesn't worry me. Let's both forget there is a secret," and she pulled his face down to hers and pressed her lips to his.

Again he pulled the large flat locket from his breast and begged, "Darling, I can never let you kiss me like that again until you know all there is to know—then you shall be the judge. It's because I love you so much, dearest, that I insist on you hearing me through. You are afraid to hear and I am afraid to have you hear, but nevertheless you must, or we must part forever."

"Oh! Frances, we must go home. I'm getting so sick, *so sick*," she cried. "I feel as if the very life has gone out of me. I don't know what is the matter."

"Lean on me, dear, we'll soon be to the car track and you will feel better when you get home."

They were silent on the car though Frances was deeply concerned about the pale, trembling, young girl at his side. When they left the car he asked, "Is it because I grieved you, darling?"

"Yes," she answered, "I think it is."

"But I can't go on taking your sweet kisses and your unselfish love without feeling like a thief. I *am* a thief, I *am* a thief to have hoped we would ever be more than friends. I have stolen the most precious thing a girl has to give—her love. Oh, dearest, forgive me! Forgive me. I beg of you forgive me." His whole being surged with anguish.

They were home now and Frances went into the house with her and called Aunt Kate who was preparing for bed. Parepa lay limp on the couch. Frances had wet his handkerchief and placed it on her head and was kneeling beside her when Aunt Kate came in.

"She took sick in the park. I think she feels better now though. Don't you Parepa?" he asked.

"Yes, I'll be all right. I guess I'm tired out," and she closed her eyes. "All I want is to sleep and sleep and dream and dream," she drawled. Then she lay still in a peaceful slumber.

Aunt Kate removed her slippers and loosened her clothing. "She will be all right, there I think," she said.

"Had we better call a doctor?" he asked.

"Really I think the child has had too much excitement this last month and she has just collapsed from it. Her pulse is a little fast, but I'm sure she will be all right by morning."

"I'll go then, but I'll call you up at eight in the morning and hear how she's feeling."

Parepa slept peacefully all night. Aunt Kate made a bed on the floor beside her and got some rest.

Frances didn't sleep or even try to. He wished he could sail out of her life forever. How did he ever allow himself to become so attached to any girl? In all his life he had managed to side step the advances of the many fair maidens of his own class as well as the well directed management of the mothers. He was termed as "a little odd" but desirable in society. He detested public demonstrations and avoided attention at all times.

Mr. Wheeler was his good counselor always. No one knew the amount of his wealth. It was a great subject of discussion. So well did Mr. Wheeler manage his affairs, both financially and personally, that no one place could claim him as a resident or be sure it boasted the habitation of his bankroll for any length of time. If the least familiarity began to exist in one location they would move on to another. His education had been gathered in many different countries. He hoped he would "finish" in America. He lay awake now wondering what was the best thing to do concerning this wonderful young girl whose trust he knew he shouldn't encourage longer.

Such were the pangs of fate. The sins of the parents visited upon their children. He hated money. It could not buy what he would have given it all to own. The study of the science of life had brought home to him many facts that could not be denied. He had long since determined never to propagate his kind. The time had come for his chain to be broken.

It was his mother's wish, too. Five generations of mothers had given their lives to their newborn, or in his case unborn babies. It must stop—and it would stop. There would be no more lives lost—with him (the only living being of six generations) this thing would stop. No powers could be brought to bear to make his resolution waver. Even while these decisions were being so forcibly expressed he was turning over in the secret places of his brain the possibility of Parepa actually being willing to be his life's companion even though she knew there would be no family increase. Perhaps she would be satisfied to help him spend his tremendous wealth in doing for the children born into this world unwelcome and uncared for. Perhaps they could study together and plan a perfectly legitimate compact satisfactory to both.

That their love was genuine there wasn't a doubt but—Parepa didn't know—how could she understand? There never was another human born into the world with their future hung round their neck by a golden chain, and no—his was—his was a singular life. She and I could "go about doing good" the rest of our lives together. I could satisfy her love for everything that's beautiful. She should have the most beautiful home and gardens in the world. We could be so happy together, but—she must know first. Thus, he argued with himself until morning.

Mr. Wheeler came into his room to plan the day's program, and noticed a tired worn look on young Frances's face.

"What's the matter, young'un?—lovesick?" he laughed.

"I'm afraid you are right, Dad. I know you have diagnosed my case correctly. You are the only physician in the world capable of prescribing a remedy so out with it, old man. I need help immediately."

"Tell me just where you hurt worse," teased Mr. Wheeler.

"Right here! The pain is all here," he said placing his hand over his heart. "And it's a terrible one!"

"Now, my lad, get dressed and we'll have breakfast. Then we are going to go into my room and have a long, long talk. It's time we have a conference such as we have not had for years."

Before going to Mr. Wheeler's private rooms, Frances called Mrs. Slay by 'phone and learned that Parepa was much better and had gone for a walk. "She and Mrs. Breen are spending the afternoon and evening together," Aunt Kate said.

Mr. Wheeler was sitting tipped back in his chair looking out of his window in a deep study. Frances did not disturb him at first, for he thought he was probably trying to figure out a most effective prescription for him.

Finally the elder man faced Frances squarely. "My child, I know how you are suffering. I suffered the same way when my love for your mother burned my heart to a crisp. No other love has even attempted to pierce its charred crust. Your love is repaid by the love of your sweetheart. Mine never was until death was ready to claim her. Then she sent for me to come to her bedside. She gave me all her love wrapped up in her unborn babe. You, Frances, you. She knew she must die as her foremothers had died. She prepared for this death many months before by writing with a fearless heart the message to you, her old nurse, and to me. We have carried out her every wish, you and I together. Now we are confronting an entirely new problem for which she omitted the solution.

"Tell me, Frances, tell me truly do you really want to tell Parepa your story?"

"Oh, yes, I do so much, but she will not let me. Last night when I told her I could not take any more of her kisses until she had heard my story she took sick and I really thought she was going to faint. She insists on not hearing it. Her very kiss fills me with such passion I cannot describe the desire that thrills my whole being," and he paced the floor in front of the older man.

Finally he stopped close to his side, "Dad, do you suppose Parepa could be happy with me even though my blood did not flow in her children's veins?"

The old man looked into Frances's eyes and said, "My dear child, my blood does not flow in your veins and I could not be happier than to live with you and for you always. No child of my own could have given me more comfort. I shouldn't worry about telling her yet, if I were you. She is too young to understand. Enjoy each other while you are here and when you come back two years from now, will be time enough. Maybe you will both have changed your minds by that time," and Mr. Wheeler rose and patted the lad on the back.

Frances turned and faced him, "Did you change your mind about my mother?"

"No, but run along now and dress for tennis. We'll have a good time today," and he waved Frances out of his room.

There were only two days left before they would leave Peoria. Tomorrow he must see Parepa but today he must make Daddy happy. They played awhile and then decided to go on a boat excursion. They succeeded in making themselves happy by succeeding in making themselves think they had succeeded in making the other happy. Frances had never known the love of an own father but he was given the fineness and beauty and companionship of a real daddy and did not miss that indescribable sector from life itself.

Daddy Wheeler was his pal. He was not a spectacular daddy—just an everyday clean, strong, gentle and noble daddy. Setting the best example of uprightness and sincerity for this young life left to his care through the workings of fate.
