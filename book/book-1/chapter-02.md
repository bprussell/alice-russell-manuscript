# Chapter II

Neither said a word for sometime. Uncle George finally broke the silence by saying, "So you are Ruth's age, hey? about sixteen."

"Yes," spoke up Parepa-Roza quickly, "and from now on don't you ever forget it."

"I won't," promised Uncle George faithfully and he never did.

At each place they stopped to sell their wares Parepa-Roza tried not to be noticed as she didn't crave attention, but Uncle George just couldn't keep from introducing his (sixteen year old) niece to his customers. They all had the usual complimentary things to say about her but to Alice Parepa-Roza they meant nothing and it bored her to have to listen and "smile back" to such ordinary talk.

The only thing about Uncle George's business that really interested Parepa-Roza was watching the customers "pick out" their premiums for their "tea tickets."

After they had left the last place on the route she said, "Uncle George, do you know not one of those women took a pretty thing. They all chose ugly old kettles, wash-pans, or mop-sticks? Why don't you suppose they take pretty things when they are getting them for nothing? Just think how pretty Mrs Jackson's shelf would look with Ruth's picture in one of those pretty celluloid frames and her dinner table would have been so beautiful with a bouquet of those petunias in one of these pretty vases. I could see that third one back really wanted that pretty matting rug for her tickets and I felt so sorry for her when she put it back and took the homeliest thing you had, that horrid old spittoon for her husband to spit in and I'll bet she will be the one that will have to empty it too. Uncle George, I want you to promise me something. Will you?"

"Now what are you up to? I've already added two years to your age, isn't that enough for one day?"

"Oh, it's nothing like that Uncle George, it's just this. Won't you please leave all the ugly premiums home next time so those nice folks will have a good excuse to pick something pretty? Do please, just once."

"Oh, that's just like you, always thinking out a way to get what you want without letting on. But I'll try it just once and see how it works. They really should have some things they don't need I suppose."

"Yes, but they *do* need them Uncle George. They need them *so* badly but they don't know it. Maybe you will be the means of finding the "missing link" in their chain of happiness (a phrase she had gathered from a book). Who knows?"

They rode on in silence again for sometime. Parepa-Roza picturing little dots of beauty here and there in the many homes they had visited and Uncle George wondering how in the world he had made such a rash promise. Already he could hear the complaint of his customers at not finding the useful premiums they expected to get. Well, he'd slip in a few any way.

Parepa-Roza broke the silence, "And Uncle George, you'll let me help pack in the pretty things won't you? Let's see, we'll put in lots of those beads, they are so pretty. Do you care if I wear a string of them till we get to your house? I love those pink ones."

"Sure, put 'em on," and he reached back in a box and drew forth a long strand of the cheapest glass beads one could imagine.

"Oh, aren't they beautiful? And, Uncle George, we'll put in a lot of those pretty pictures for the walls and curtains, and sofa pillows, and vases! Lots of vases for flowers and those dear little lace collars to make their dresses pretty and where you've got a spittoon let's put those comb and brush sets for ladies, and, OH! what's that, Uncle George?" and Parepa-Roza most fell off the seat she was so delighted with what met her eyes.

"Oh, that's Glen Oak Park," said Uncle George in a most casual way.

"Oh!" gasped Parepa-Roza leaning forward on the seat, "Isn't it pretty? Do you suppose Heaven is anything like that?"

"Don't know, never been there." and Uncle George wondered what the next question would be. There was no more. Parepa-Roza was so wrapped up in her thoughts that she was most thrown from the seat again when Uncle George pulled up the reins and the horses stopped short.

"Well, here we are Parepa-Roza" he said.

"I'm not Pepper-Rosie, I'm Alice Parepa-Roza, sixteen years old *and don't you forget it.*"

Then the door opened and out came Aunt Kate; pretty, sweet, and gushing.

First she kissed Uncle George, a big smack and then gathered her young visitor, as best she could, into her arms.

"Oh, it's Alice Parepa-Roza!" she exclaimed. "How glad I am you've come, and what a nice big girl you've grown to be."

"Yes," said Uncle George, "pretty good size for a sixteen year old, eh, Kate?"

Parepa-Roza could have kissed him for that.

"Come right on in and make yourself at home. Expect you'd like to take a bath and freshen yourself up a bit before supper, wouldn't you?" smiled Aunt Kate.

"I do feel dirty" ventured the bewildered Alice Parepa-Roza. She had heard of bath rooms and bath tubs but had never seen one. Never-the-less she was soon to be introduced to one in full action. Aunt Kate drew the water for the bath, got out pretty bath towels, sweet smelling soap, and then turning to the tired, half scared girl said, "This is the toilet, I'll show you how to use it, and this is the lavatory which we use to wash our hands and face in. Now just undress and I'll be unpacking and hanging up your clothes while you bathe. I'll leave the door open a crack so you won't be lonesome."

Parepa-Roza climbed over the edge of the tub and slipped down into the water a little cautiously, but was soon enjoying the refreshing thrill as well as the smell of the pretty soap. She hoped Aunt Kate wouldn't peek in at her. She would have more fun if the door was closed.

Finally Aunt Kate called, "Are you through, dear?"

"I'm just getting out." Splash she slipped and went down ker-bump to the bottom of the tub, splashing the walls and floor of Aunt Kate's pretty bath room.

Aunt Kate rushed in laughing and helped the very much mortified and subdued girl to the floor, wrapped her in a big bath towel and rubbed her dry. Then she combed her pretty wavy hair making a braid part way down and terminating it with a curl. "What a beautiful child you are, my dear. I wish you were our girl. Our Maude would have been sixteen now." Then she slipped a clean, stiffly starched gingham dress over the clean stiffly starched petticoat and with a kiss on one rosy cheek said, "Now come on downstairs and I'll let you help me get supper."

"I'd love to" ventured our heroine and as she passed the long mirror in the hall she hardly knew herself. "I really am pretty," she said to herself, "Funny I never noticed it before."

"Guess I'll let you fix the salad," said Aunt Kate, smiling.

"You'll have to show me how but I'll like doing it." She remembered seeing a salad at the Church supper once and it looked pretty but she didn't risk eating it for fear she wouldn't handle it just right. Now she was actually going to fix one.

"All right," said Aunt Kate, "we'll make the one your Uncle George likes best. It won't take long. First, you can cut up these two apples into small pieces, wash them first; then cut this celery in just as small pieces. You see I've had it washed and in this clean bag on the ice so it will be nice and crisp. Open this little can of tuna fish, salt, and mix them all together by putting them into this white bowl and tossing about with this wooden fork. Then mix in this salad dressing. After that is done you can put a lettuce leaf on each salad plate and put some salad on the leaf. I always put some of the dressing on the table in this mayonnaise bowl so if anyone cares for more they can help themselves."

Through all this long speech Parepa-Roza stood spell bound. Such a pretty kitchen, such beautiful dishes! She didn't think she would ever get tired washing them. If mamma only had pretty things. And the curtains! Think of it, thin white curtains trimmed with a blue border — in the *kitchen*, mind you.

Finally Aunt Kate broke the spell by handing her the paring knife (she had already washed the apples).

"Oh yes," said Parepa-Roza coming to, "I guess I forgot what came first."

Aunt Kate was a shrewd woman and knew when to keep her eyes as well as her mouth shut. She slipped into the dining room and began setting the table. This time for four.

Parepa labored on, for it was real work, this being left with the "ingredients" to combine her first salad and Aunt Kate away in another room. She wished she would come near once in a while just so she could ask her if she was doing it right. She was plucky enough though and finished the job completely.

When she was putting the lettuce on the plates she noticed there were four and wondered if Aunt Kate hadn't made a mistake. She wondered too, where Aunt Kate was, she hadn't heard a sound for a long time.

Finally she came in the front door, calling out "Did my little girl think I'd left her?"

"Oh, what a pretty salad! Won't Uncle George and Frances be surprised when I tell them you made it? Now how would you like to run out in the garden and pick a few flowers for the center of our dining table?"

"Oh! that would be fun," but Parepa-Roza wondered why Aunt Kate had such lovely flowers in the *back* yard. Why not in the front yard like the "Burnett girls" had theirs. I wonder who Frances is, she thought, as she picked far too many flowers for the small center bowl. She's probably a neighbor's girl she thought. I hope she's nice. She didn't need to wonder long for when she went in Aunt Kate told her they were going to have another guest for supper too, a strange lad that had been visiting some old people across the way for a month. She felt sorry for him because there were so few children his age to play with in the neighborhood. "They don't tell anything about him only that his name is Frances," said Aunt Kate, "So you must be careful not to ask him any personal questions. Seems that his guardian is a friend of these old folks and he's to stay here until college this fall. He's sixteen too, I think. He has always had private teachers and so finished high school work some time ago. Now, remember not to ask him where his folks are or anything like that. I doubt if these old folks he is staying with know themselves. I have it figured out that they are getting well paid for keeping him in a "quiet" place until his college work begins. The old lady did tell me this much, that he had received an awful shock at one time and his physician ordered him kept quiet for several months. Don't think she knows what the shock was herself. Anyway he's real polite and nice and I know you'll like him."

Aunt Kate was taking a great deal for granted. Parepa's appetite for salad was spoiled right then and as she noticed the pretty napkins and two spoons and a different kind of fork at each place she knew that misery through the meal was ahead.

Aunt Kate was so wise — she surmised what was in Parepa-Roza's mind and said, "Did you taste your salad?"

"No, I didn't," she answered.

"Well, let's taste it," and she dished up two small plates of it. Then getting two salad forks handed one to Parepa-Roza. "The best I ever ate" she said. "You've got yourself into a job now. Uncle George will want you to make all the salads while you're here."

"See if I put on a spoon at each place for the tea, and one for the dessert — I mean the peaches."

"All right. Then I think I hear Uncle George talking to Frances now. We'll take up the supper and call them in."

Parepa-Roza tried so hard to help but her fingers were all thumbs. Finally all was ready and Parepa-Roza was not too fussed to appreciate the beauty of Aunt Kate's well appointed table. The china, silver, crystal, and flowers combined to make it a perfect picture. She heard them coming in now and what would she do? There was no pantry door to hide behind in Aunt Kate's house, as she was used to doing at home when she wanted to be unnoticed and especially un-introduced to strangers.

"This is our niece, Alice Parepa-Roza, Frances. She is going to visit us a while and I hope you two will have lots of good times together."

"I'm sure we will," said Frances and Parepa-Roza smiled back at him without half trying. "Do you play tennis?"

"No, I don't, but I'd love to learn," and she wondered how she happened to make such a long sentence to a stranger that she had never seen before.

"What say we all go over after supper and teach her a bit about the game, wifie?" spoke up Uncle George. Then on second thought he added, "I expect she is tired though, after that long jaunt in my old tea wagon."

Parepa-Roza could have squeezed him for that, seemed as if he always said the right thing at the right time.

"Let's go tomorrow evening then," said Frances not willing to drop the subject.

"Yes," said Aunt Kate, "we'll take a picnic lunch and eat out there. Would you like that Parepa?"

"Oh, yes, I've always wished I could see a picnic — or be at one." She hesitated, knowing she had said the wrong thing. Her cheeks turned a deep crimson and she could feel herself slipping. The rest of them kept up a round of chatter but our heroine didn't risk another sentence during the meal. She was actually sweating now and Uncle George knew she was laboring harder to get done with that meal than she ever did milking the five cows which was her usual allotment each morning and evening.

She will never know how she got through that meal. It was all a blur to her, but when Aunt Kate said as they rose from the table, "Parepa-Roza and I will do up the dishes and then we'll be out on the porch with you," she thought that for a few moments at least she would be away from the eyes and ears of this strange boy. No such luck. "Let me help you Mrs Slay? I can wash dishes as well as a woman so let me prove it?" said the unsuspecting Frances as he gathered his hands full of dirty dishes. "Right this way my ladies" and he bowed a low bow into the kitchen.

"Oh! do the dish washers get to finish up the salads and stuff," he said as his eyes fell on the remainder of "Parepa's first." "Really I thought that was the best salad I ever ate. How did you make it Mrs Slay?" Parepa wished she wasn't there to hear Aunt Kate tell him she made it, but she was and when he grabbed her two hands in his and swung her around saying, "Hurrah for the champion salad maker" she felt her awkwardness more than ever.

"Now you two can wipe and I'll wash," said Aunt Kate as she handed each a tea towel. Uncle George puffed at his cigar amused at the girl's discomfort. Parepa was really beginning to feel a little more at home now. Something in the "feel" of the tea towel was familiar to her and she ventured to say, "I better wipe the tumblers, you might break them" to Frances.

"Tumblers? What's tumblers?" asked Frances.

"What you drink out of, of course," and she felt quite smart at being able to tell him something.

"Oh, in our English those things are the crystals. No wonder I didn't know what you were talking about, and why do you think I might break them?" and he proceeded to show her that he could wipe them as well as she when crash it went to the floor and broke in a hundred pieces.

"Smarty, smarty," said Parepa just as she would have said to her own brother. "You see you're not so smart as you think you are. Breaking Aunt Kate's CRYSTAL."

"I'm so sorry," he said picking up the pieces. "Is it an open stock pattern, Mrs Slay?"

I wonder what he's talking about, "open stock pattern" thought Parepa as Aunt Kate was telling him where she bought them and all about the name of the pattern, etc.

"I'll get you one tomorrow to replace it," he said, "so your set won't be broken."

"I wonder why the whole set will break just because one did," she pondered as she put the last one carefully on the table. Maybe I'll learn if I stay here long enough. They went out to sit on the porch now and Parepa liked to hear the sound of the horses' feet clap, clap on the pavement and see all the fine ladies and girls sit primly in their surreys with their nicely ruffled and lace trimmed dresses arranged "just so." The fringe trimming around the edge of the surrey tops took her eye, too. It was all so pretty, this parade of fine horses and carriages in the twilight. She wished the folks could see it.

"Penny for your thoughts," said Frances as he gave her a poke in the back. "Wake up and talk to the people."

"Isn't that a pretty horse," she said and then went to pondering again.

"Say, what's-your-name, if you like pretty horses I just wish you could see some of mine. My favorite saddle pony is black as coal. I have a team of pure white ones that Clem scrubs everyday. I'll show you their pictures some day. I'll be glad when I get home and can ride and drive them again. You see, I haven't seen them for nearly two years. I've been over the ocean that long. I bet they will know me though."

"I bet they can't pull as much as old Coaly and Scamper," she ventured. "They can out pull any horses around our place."

"Maybe, but I bet mine's the prettiest. What's more, they are pedigreed."

"Are they?" said Parepa, not willing to let on that she didn't know what the word meant. "I've heard of that kind."

Aunt Kate suggested they go in and have some music.

Parepa was crazy about music although she didn't know one note from another. She possessed a beautiful voice and could readily carry any tune she had once heard.

Uncle George had heard her singing while she was milking and had told Aunt Kate about it. She being an ex-music teacher and popular vocalist was anxious to find out what the possibilities were for a musical genius.

"What do you want to sing kids?" said Uncle George, "just name it and you can have it."

"Do you know 'Sweet Adaline'," said Frances laughing.

"Sure, come right up and let her roar. Come on Parepa-Roza we need you too to make up this quartet."

"I'd rather listen," she said and no amount of coaxing could move her. That she was spell bound by the singing of the others was evident, and they sang and sang sometimes one at a time, two at a time, or three at a time. Parepa made up her mind right then that she would learn to sing just like Aunt Kate. She thought she never saw such a pretty boy, too. She could look at him now while he was watching his music. Pretty black curly hair, dark eyes, and olive skin, tall, not too slender and so graceful. Too many dimples she thought.

"That's enough now," said Aunt Kate as she whirled around on the piano stool.

"Yes, and I must be going home," Frances announced, "it's ten o'clock." He bade all a good night and said to Parepa, "Remember you get your first lesson in tennis tomorrow evening."

"Yes," she answered, "but I haven't the least idea how it's played."

"She'll never say that after tomorrow night, will she Mrs Slay?" he answered her.

"I expect she'll be beating us all!" said Uncle George. After he left Uncle George couldn't help teasing Parepa a little so he said, "Pretty nice little fellow, eh, Parepa?"

"Suppose so," said Parepa and gave a big gape signifying that she was sleepy.

"Kate, better put our little girl to bed, she's had a hard day of it."

"Come along then," said Aunt Kate but Uncle George held his hand out and caught Parepa as she was going to the stairs. "Have to kiss your old Uncle good night my girl" he said as he gave her a rousing smack.

Parepa was so mortified she blushed all over but managed to mount the steps by Aunt Kate's side.

"We'll undress in the bath room where you can cool your face with a wash cloth wet in the lavatory. Put on your nightie and come into this bed room" said Aunt Kate pointing to such a clean and pretty room. "I'll turn the covers down for you."

Aunt Kate watched the beautiful young girl approach in her long nightie which had been made by bleaching and piecing flour sacks together as were all the night gowns in the Wesley family.

Parepa kneeled down beside her bed and reverently whispered her prayers. Aunt Kate wished Uncle George could see this pretty picture. It remained clear in her mind to the last day of her life.

When Parepa arose she kissed Aunt Kate and told her how nice she was. "I'm tired as can be," she confided, "but it's been such a big big day."

"Now you must sleep as long as you can in the morning because you are all worn out from your long ride, and you know we are going to have lots of fun tomorrow."

After the light was snapped out and Parepa left alone she reviewed the day's happenings. I didn't suppose so much could happen in so short a time. I must ask Aunt Kate tomorrow what country pedigreed horses are from and what they look like. Hope Uncle George won't kiss me tomorrow night. Wonder if I'll make the salad to--m--o--rr--ow but the child was asleep, not to awaken until the sun was high in the sky. She couldn't believe she had slept so late.

Aunt Kate heard her and came running upstairs calling out, "Sleepy head, sleepy head," and as she entered the room Parepa noticed she was dressed to go out.

"How's our girl this morning?" she asked.

"Fine, but how late is it Aunt Kate?"

"Nine-thirty, dear, I'm glad you slept so well."

Parepa couldn't remember of ever having slept so late.
