# Chapter XVII

Parepa spent the afternoon and evening resting. Reaction came in the form of tired mind.

"I just want to be left alone to sleep and rest," she told Aunt Kate. This wise woman understood and kissing her warm cheek closed her bedroom door and crept quietly down the steps to her husband who was sitting on the sofa waiting for her to come to him. There seemed to be a completed understanding between these two people. One always knew just what the other expected of them and always did it! Just then Uncle George wanted his wife to kiss him and she did. Then she slipped down beside him real close and his arm closed around her. Here they were again, nothing to say, just happy in each other's company. Once in a while Uncle George's hand would caress Aunt Kate's hair. Once in a while Aunt Kate would squeeze Uncle George's hand. And once in a while two pair of eyes would meet and speak for two tongues which were numb. Such was the mutual understanding of this devoted couple.

Monday morning found Parepa up and coming as usual. She went through her lessons as earnestly as if they were her first ones. Success did not mean the end for her—only the beginning.

That noon when Uncle Will and she were seated at lunch he became confidential. "We are all so proud of you, Parepa," he said. "And you refusing to go on the stage gives me more pleasure than your success. That showed the real metal in you."

"Thank you, Uncle Will, but I did only what seemed to me the right thing to do. That kind of stage life don't appeal to me in the least."

"I'm glad of that," he said. "But there is something else I want to talk to you about. Who is this young Emmerson chap I've been hearing about? Are you sure he is good company for you? A fine looking chap I'll agree, but what do you know of his past? I'm surprised at George and Kate encouraging his attentions knowing so little about him."

"Oh, Uncle Will," laughed Parepa, "you are borrowing trouble now. Frances and I are the best of friends but that is all. I like him immensely and I hope he likes me as well, but neither of us are simple minded enough to think of anything but friendship. I wish you knew him as well as I think I do. You couldn't help liking him."

"I hope he is all you think him to be, but I do think you should know all about his past. You may suddenly find yourself in love with him and then what? You will soon be eighteen and a woman grown. Old enough to decide matters for yourself. I hope you don't think me meddling when I insist on your being familiar with his family history before you let yourself in for a life of repentance."

"Oh, Uncle Will, please don't worry about me. I'm not in the least concerned about matrimony," she said emphatically.

"You shouldn't ignore the attentions of our home town boys. You refused to go again with Jerry Williams who is from one of Peoria's best families. There are no skeletons hid in their closets," he boasted.

"I agree with you, Uncle Will, there are none hid but plenty at large. I have my own reasons for not caring for his company."

It was time to return now and as they neared the shop Parepa said, "Please, don't think me ungrateful, Uncle Will, but I believe I am a better reader of human nature than you are. Thanks just the same for your kind interest in me."

"That's all right, and don't forget that you promised me two years ago that you would come to me if you were in distress for any reason."

"I'll keep my promise, Uncle Will. You can depend on that."

As they entered the store Frances came toward them smiling.

"Uncle Will, meet my friend, Frances Emmerson," and Parepa blushed a tell tale pink which always made her irresistible.

"Glad to meet you," they said in unison.

"Fine day," said Uncle Will.

"Dandy," said Frances. "Just stopped in to see if Parepa would like to try a steed this evening. She's been telling me what a whiz she is in the saddle."

"She's great!" said Uncle Will. "But promise me the steed is a safe one. You know she is a precious thing to all of us."

"And to me too—I promise," he said and when arrangements were completed he left.

"Isn't he great, Uncle Will?" asked Parepa, her eyes still sparkling.

"I guess so, but be careful my girlie. All is not gold that glitters." And he went into his office.

"Poor Uncle Will," thought Parepa, "he means all right."

That evening proved to be one never to be forgotten by either Parepa or Frances.
