# Chapter XII

Now ensued what proved to be the most irresponsible, delightful, entirely healthful, and enchanting year or two of Parepa's life. It was a free, eager, venturesome, joyous life altogether and helped in the development of the normal happy girl.

There were many skating and coasting parties in the wintertime. These Parepa-Roza enjoyed to the limit. Often some courageous young gallant would grab her mittened hand and careen with her over the slick cold ice. Just as often some lovesick swain encircled her heavily coated waist with his heavily coated arms as they coasted down the long, long hills on their big sleds. It was a long and jolly ride from the top of Spring Street Hill to the Monroe Street car tracks. Sometimes they risked the more curved, more exciting descent of old Seventh Street Hill.

Parepa-Roza was truly intoxicated with the joy and laughter created by the very spirit and action these sports demanded. One had to keep on the move or freeze to death. She loved the loud high-pitched yells of the merry coasters filling the chilly air as only irresponsible youth can.

There was always a noticeable rush and scuffle among the boys to "beat" sitting next back of Parepa-Roza on the long sleds. She had no favorites among them. They were all alike—only Frances was different. Sometimes she thought they held her on the sled a little tighter than was necessary, but she didn't mind—so much—being squeezed—a little. There is safety in numbers, you know, and there were always numbers.

In the summer months there was such a variety of sports into which Parepa plunged with all her young strength and courage. Swimming lessons at old Central Park on "Ladies' day." Aunt Kate made her suit of blue serge. It would look like a winter overcoat compared with the modern and more practical suits of today. How she loved the water! Then there were boat excursions, both daylight and moonlight. How she enjoyed dancing on the boat. There was always a "bunch" of them that gathered on the deck and sang songs in the moonlight.

She had become an expert tennis player now and challenged the best players in town. Sunday afternoon seldom passed without a few hours in Glen Oak. The band concerts were a sort of stimulating pleasure to the Slays. Uncle George and Aunt Kate would sit in seats on the pavilion porch facing the music, while Parepa-Roza would stroll arm in arm with some girl friend over the grounds. When they returned to the pavilion, however, their number was usually increased to four, and the addition was of the opposite sex. Rides on the lake in small rowboats were a part of the afternoon's enjoyment, too. Of course, the little animals in the wire pens must be fed—Parepa always carried nuts for them.

Aunt Kate and Parepa strolled to the conservatory each visit to make the acquaintance of any new plant or to check up on the progress of older acquaintances. So delighted were they with this study that Uncle George thought they were more devoted to it than to their music.

Sometimes after an especially happy day with young friends, Parepa would lay awake and think over the little intimacies that made it memorable. The romances she dreamed were singularly clean. Of sex she knew little and cared less. It took many years and much living to undo her early impressions as to the relationships of boys and girls.

Aunt Kate studied Parepa carefully. *She has the play spirit, but she thoroughly loves her work,* she thought. Great-hearted, generous, wise Aunt Kate. How kind she was, how stimulating, how interested in every step of Parepa's progress.
