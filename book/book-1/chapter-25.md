# Chapter XXV

Days, weeks, months and to Parepa ages went rolling away in the two years that followed. It was a hard two years for Parepa as Uncle George's death left Aunt Kate in a broken condition of health. It had been necessary for them to sell the home which they so loved in order to keep Uncle George in the hospital and continue the services of the best Doctors. They moved into a little cottage of four rooms near the Bethany Baptist church which cornered on Hayward and Madison Streets.

Parepa became much interested in this Church and the Sunday School work to say nothing of the young man Joseph Donald by name who taught a class of boys just opposite her class of girls. Oh, of course, Parepa wouldn't have admitted she was interested in this smart industrious young fellow any farther than their class interests threw them together but never-the-less there was a little "hankerin" for each other's company just the same. The young man was earning his way through Bradley college and had won several scholarships that made him all the more determined to succeed even though the profession he had chosen for himself would cost him most of his time and all his earnings.

How he wished he could afford to take Parepa out and show her a good time—just once—but no, he needed every penny if he was to succeed. He had a fair voice and they both sang in the choir. After evening services he would stroll home with her, the half block, and then sometimes would "come in a minute" and sometimes he would "just sit on the porch a minute." Parepa admired this young fellow so much. He had such a strong purpose in life and such a strong will to carry out that purpose.

He could not even think of marriage, so dispensed it from his mind, or tried to. Never-the-less there were tell tale glances passed between these two which the "church people" diagnosed as "love messages." Neither the young girl or man bothered to define them.

Reverend Reynolds, Pastor, as well as the older members of the church fell to calling Parepa-Roza "Bethany's Angel" in respect for her sweet voice and helpfulness in the church.

Aunt Kate had fallen heir to the dreaded malady which claimed her husband and she begged for the day to come when she might join him. Parepa took every care of her Aunt refusing the aid of a nurse as she knew the limit of the Slay finances. Parepa had saved a small amount from the recitals and Uncle George's insurance helped some.

Now the time had come for Frances to return. Parepa had not told him of their change of address but had let the Post Office Clerk hunt her out and forward his letters to her. She didn't want him to know how badly they had needed money.

Frances's letters were so filled with the joy of their meeting again and Parepa and Aunt Kate could hardly wait. Only a few days now and he would be here.

On Sunday mornings a kind neighbor stayed with Aunt Kate while Parepa went to Sunday School and Church services. She went to the church early in order to put some drawings on her blackboard. As usual Joseph was there too. He had some maps to draw on his blackboard. Their conversation floated from one side of the room to the other. Their maps didn't receive much personal thought as each of them sketched line after line between sentences and glances.

Neither saw the figure of a handsome young man enter and stand watching the two through the crack in the door. The conversation between the two was going something like this:

Joseph—"It's awful to be poor isn't it 'Pep'" (his pet name for her).

Parepa—"No one knows that better than I do. How you coming with your college work?"

Joseph—"Slow—no money—no progress you know."

Parepa—"Don't I know though. I'm looking for a dear friend of mine about tomorrow that is so rich he don't know what to do with it all. He aims to do a lot of good with it though. Just go about helping wherever he finds a need for it. Wouldn't that be a grand life though?"

Joseph—"Yes, but I'd rather be a Doctor."

Parepa—"I'm going to tell you something Joe." Drawing the last line on her board and walking over to his side of the room. "This fellow wants me to help him spend it."

Joseph—"Parepa! You don't mean to tell me this chap has proposed to you?"

Parepa—"I mean just that."

Joseph—"And what are you going to tell him?" (coming closer to her his eyes burning with a love light that was unmistakable). He closed his arms around her and before she could answer kissed her passionately. "Parepa—do you love him? Are you sure you love this man—this stranger?"

Parepa—"Joseph, I have loved him for four years. From the first time I ever saw him. I'm sorry for you, Joseph—believe me, I am."

Joseph—"Haven't you seen? Oh, haven't you known I loved you, darling? It was only my desire for you that has kept me working so hard to make a success in life. I didn't tell you because I thought surely you had guessed. I wanted to succeed first. Isn't there any hope, dearest, any hope for me at all?" and he hugged her to him, kissing her again and again.

Parepa—"Only if I find him greatly changed, Joseph dear."

Joseph—"Oh God! I hope he is changed."

The sound of the children's voices came floating through the room and found each teacher at their board drawing. The children came in at a side door. Each little girl ran to Parepa with a small bunch of flowers for her. She took each child into her arms and kissed them. Frances was sure she was an angel—and a—mother.

"In as much as ye do it unto one of the least of these ye do it also unto me," he repeated to himself and turning he left the building.

When Parepa came home from Church Mrs Field, who had stayed with Aunt Kate, said, "And did the handsome young fellow find you at the Church, Parepa?"

"What handsome young fellow?" asked Parepa.

"Yer Aunt Kate thought it must be the young man you've been expecting. I told him he'd find you at the Church."

"No, I didn't see anyone. Expect he changed his mind about coming. I wonder where he is now."

Aunt Kate and Parepa ate their lunch talking all the time, about Frances and what he would be like—would he be changed—would he be so kind and so handsome. When would he come again? Surely this afternoon. The afternoon passed—he did not come.

Parepa went to church that evening as usual. She sang a solo and a duet with Joseph. She did not notice Frances slip quietly into a back seat. He sat as one fastened there—immovable as his eyes followed the glances of the two young people.

*That* is *real* love. Love as it should be. Love—his face is shining with a fire that only a real mate's love can kindle. The song Parepa sang was so sweet and she so charming no wonder this normal young chap had fallen in love with her. She was his kind. She should be his wife—I'll not interfere. He sat quietly in his seat, having made his decision and listened to the sermon. "Be not over anxious for this world's goods" was the text. What do all this world's good amount to? Nothing, thought Frances.

As soon as the benediction was pronounced Frances crept out of the church and into the street. He decided to go to Parepa's home and wait for her. He would not let her know he had been to church. When he reached the porch he dropped down on the step completely exhausted. Yes, he was beaten—and rightly. The other chap had no chain around his neck—he did.

As he sat there in the shadows of the trees he could see—Yes, he was sure—it was Parepa and—Joseph. Something took possession of him. He would hide by the side of the porch in the shrubbery and would listen—yes, he would stoop to listen—eavesdrop.

He heard them come up the walk. "Won't you come in?" Parepa was saying. Was hers a sympathetic voice or an understanding one?

"No, let's just sit down here on the porch," he replied.

"I'll run in and see if Auntie is all right and then I'll be right out," she said.

Frances saw him turn and watch her go into the light. He watched her until she was out of sight. Then he said laying his head in his hands, "O God! What have I done to deserve this? Why can't I have her?" and he rose and paced up and down the walk until she came out again.

"How's she feeling tonight?" asked Joseph trying to act his old self.

"Oh, Joe, sometimes I get so discouraged. I don't know what to do. There don't seem to be anything I can do to help her. Truthfully I think the end is near."

"Then what will you do, dearie?" he asked pulling her down beside him on the step and holding her hand in his.

"I don't know for sure but if a certain fellow I know hasn't changed his mind or his manners in the last two years I'm going to get married," she said. "Isn't the moon pretty?"

"Yes, I guess so, but how does it come you never told me about this affair before?" and he held tight to her hand then raised it to his lips and kissed it. She did not object she felt so sorry for him.

"I didn't know you would be interested. I didn't tell anyone. Only Aunt Kate knows," she said. "I'm so sorry, Joe, honestly I am. If you really and truly love me as much as I love Frances I know the pain in your heart. I couldn't bear to think of he and I being separated by another. You have been so lovely to me, Joe, and we have had good times together. I'll never forget you, Joe, never. Will you? Promise never to forget me?" and her eyes glistened in the moonlight with tears. Her voice trembled.

"Parepa will you promise me that if this fellow has changed and you don't care for him so much, that you will come right to me? I know I haven't a thing to offer you yet, but I'm going to have someday. Will you promise, darling?" he asked feverishly.

"Yes, Joseph—I promise." He grabbed her into his arms and covered her face, her neck, her hair, her very eyelids with kisses. She did not resist, she felt sorry for him. She wanted to comfort him—but—how? He held her in silence for a long time then, loosening his grasp and springing to his feet he said, "Good bye darling of my heart, I will never kiss you again unless you ask it."

"Goodbye, my good friend, Goodbye," and as he went through the gate she threw herself on the porch cushions and sobbed and sobbed. Frances's heart was breaking for this wonderful woman whose love he had captured four years ago when she was a mere child.

"Is it right, Oh God, answer. Is it right?" and he turned his face upward as he uttered the most earnest prayer to his God in Heaven that had ever crossed his lips.

Finally Parepa sat up and raised her arms to the sky. "Dearest Lord Jesus, help me to know and do what is right always, Amen." Then she rose and entered the house singing as if gay. "Savior Like a shepherd lead Us much we need thy tenderest care."

Frances listened—it was Aunt Kate saying, "I know why my little girl is so happy. Her lover is coming."

"Yes," she answered.
