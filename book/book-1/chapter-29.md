# Chapter XXIX

Yes, it was. There was no doubt. The figure stood still for some time gazing over the water as if in a dream. Parepa felt a strong impulse to spring to her feet, rush to his arms and live again the ecstasy that was theirs two years ago. He turned, and coming closer to the log, sat down on the opposite end from which Parepa was sitting near.

He was talking to himself now in a low voice. She heard him say distinctly, "She must listen—I must tell her. If she won't hear me I will leave her forever, never to see her again. No real man would ask a girl that he truly loved to marry him without telling her all about his past and his plans for the future." Parepa gasped—she recognized those as the very words Joseph had repeated not half an hour ago. Had he heard?

Each sat quietly for some time. Then Frances rose, drew the locket from his breast and walked to the edge of the river. There he stood silhouetted against the moonlit sheen of the water. Parepa watched him as he gazed long and thoughtfully upon the locket in his hand. Finally he replaced it and began pacing up and down the shore. His back was toward her now. As if possessed she sprang to her feet, spread her long cape with extended arms as if the wings of a huge bird were flapping wildly in the night air, she ran past him. "Catch me if you can," she repeated as she soared toward him in a dance.

"Parepa! Parepa-Roza! Come to me. Come my love!" he cried.

She circled nearer and nearer him and throwing her cape over his head led him to the old log. Seating herself closely beside him she cuddled up to him pulling from his breast the long chain and locket. His arms were tightly around her now and he was kissing her hot lips.

Holding up the locket and kissing it she whispered, "Frances can I see what's in it tomorrow?" she asked. "I really want to know."

"Yes, my darling, you can. In the meantime I want you to prepare to be true to yourself—to him and to me after hearing my secret. Will you promise?" Those words were familiar. Now she was sure he had heard.

Her face turned to his and a long lingering kiss was her answer. They sat there a long time as if each was afraid the sweet spell would be broken.

They heard the clock in the old courthouse strike eleven. "Must go now," said Parepa and as they walked hand in hand she asked, "You don't mind if I ask you to leave me alone when we leave Hayward Street do you Frances? I want to go home alone."

"Familiar words," laughed Frances. "However your wish is granted," and once more kissing her goodbye he said, "You are a funny girl, Parepa."

Parepa found Uncle Will waiting up for her. He assured her of his anxiety for her being out alone so late but she assured him she longed to be alone with herself at times. Told him how when she was a little girl at home she grew tired of having others around her and would creep away from the house and go way down in the pasture and sit on the hill and tell dream stories to herself. "I felt just that way tonight," she added but didn't tell him her game of solitary had been broken into twice that evening.

"Have you decided what you are going to do?" he asked her with deep interest in his voice.

"Not quite, Uncle Will. I have several ideas but haven't exactly decided."

"You know, my dear, if there is anything I can do to help you or advise you I will be most happy to do so," he said.

"Thank you so much, Uncle Will. You have always been so kind to me. I'll never forget it. Good night good kind Uncle," she crossed the room to where he was sitting in his big sleepy-hollow chair and kissed his bald head.

Uncle Will pulled her down on his lap and said teasingly, "Are you sure you are not contemplating matrimony, child?"

"Maybe," she laughed. "All I need is the consent of the groom. That's hard to get." Then she sat looking down at the floor very sad.

"Tell me about it Parepa, my little girl," and he drew her head to his breast and tried to comfort her. "Are you so much in love with this young stranger?"

"Stranger! He is not a stranger. I know him better than I know myself," she sprang to her feet and said again, "Good night, dear Uncle Will."

After she had gone up the stairs Uncle Will sat for a long time in deep thought. He saw her as she was that first day in his candy shop. He recalled her birthday party. The tender care she gave Aunt Kate. Now she was alone at eighteen years of age. He resolved to have a talk with this young Emmerson and find out for himself just what his intentions toward the girl were.

At last he went to bed greatly worked up over nothing that really justified his concern.
