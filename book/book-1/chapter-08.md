# Chapter VIII

After Parepa had left for the "candy store" the doorbell gave a loud ring and in came our hero, Frances. After greetings were exchanged and the evening's experiences talked over Frances became very sober and thoughtful. He seemed to have changed from the young boy to a man of mature years and mind.

"There's something I'd like to talk to you folks about now that Parepa has gone and we have the room to ourselves." Launching into the subject at once he said, "No doubt you know all about Parepa's circumstances and whether or not her folks are able to pay for the education she should be getting at once. It's not right to have her spending one day in that candy shop. I have only a short time to stay around here and I have come to you for an earnest understanding."

"I don't understand what you are talking about," said the bewildered Uncle George.

"Just this. I don't want Parepa to know one word of this. I am well able to help her finish an uninterrupted education. My plan is to have her stay with you and I'll furnish all the money necessary. I can see she is in good hands. Can't we make some arrangements for this? There will be no obligations and I want no credit."

"No! Absolutely no!" said Mr. George Slay and Frances said no more on the subject.

"How long will you be in Peoria?" asked Mrs. Slay timidly.

"Only a week or so if things come around alright. My guardian had a great deal of business to attend to in America and it will depend upon his success in getting it straightened up, when we leave. We will not come across again for two years."

"What do you intend to make your life's work?" asked Uncle George.

"I haven't decided exactly but I am going to make a thorough study of sociology first. Will keep up piano and voice, too."

"We'll all miss you when you leave," said Mr. Slay.

"And you have made my time here more pleasant by taking me under your wing. Isn't Parepa an interesting girl though? So different from any I ever met. I like her immensely. When I come back she will be changed I suppose. Maybe not one bit like the Parepa we know today."

"I don't believe she will ever change. Only, of course, she will be more matured — more sophisticated, but her pure loving heart will never change," and Aunt Kate said it as if she were gazing into the future.

"You are the fellow that will be changed," accused Uncle George, pointing a finger at Frances. "The next five or six years of your life will be years of continual changes. I doubt if you will even think of us after you are gone from here."

"I give you both my promise — as long as I live I shall never forget you and your hospitality. Do you know, or can you believe, that I was never in a real home before in my life? I mean a home where there was a husband and wife. All my life I have lived in hotels or on my private estates where everything is done so formally by servants and caretakers. My guardian is the only real "heart" friend I have. He is a prince of men. No mother could have left her child in better hands. He is carrying out her dying requests to the letter; he lives for me and me only. It's a shame, you see his grandfather was my grandmother's guardian, his father was my mother's guardian, and now he is my guardian. I love him more than life itself. He and I are working out a great plan by which we can be most helpful in the world." He stopped short as if to apologize. "I beg your pardon — I didn't intend to burden you with this story of myself. I must ask you never to tell what I have told you. I never talk about myself. I don't know how I come to let it out."

"That's just all right my boy and if it is any comfort to you to "let loose" don't worry about our letting your secrets pass our lips," said Mr. Slay wishing he would dump all the beans now that he had aroused their curiosity.

Frances rose abruptly and started to the door hat in hand. "Thought if you don't mind I'd go to church with you in the morning," he said.

"We'll be so glad to have you," said Mrs. Slay.

He thanked them and left with a hearty "Goodbye."

Aunt Kate and Uncle George stood and looked at each other spellbound. Finally Aunt Kate reprimanded her husband, "George, dear, you were too hasty in refusing to let him help with Parepa's education. You should have waited until you heard his plans."

"Just what do you think of that young buck anyway?" he retorted.

"I think he's a mystery child, that's what I think. He actually acted scared when he realized he was "telling something." He must be sure of never running out of money," she answered.

"Yes, but I'll bet there's something behind all this secrecy. Why should a young fellow like him have a stone around his neck? I tell you I'm going to see that old guardian of his when he comes to town. Why is he hiding this young fellow here in Peoria while he cavorts around the country "on business"? It's my opinion there's a mystery somewhere."

"Oh, George, you shouldn't judge so quickly. Maybe it's just as he says, they are working out a great plan to do great good. Let's give them the benefit of the doubt anyway."

"Alright just as you say, but for my part I'm just as well satisfied his stay in Peoria is about over," and George Slay lit a cigar and stalked out of the house to walk a mile or so and think this thing out for himself. However, he came home at eleven-thirty no better satisfied than when he left.

At dinner he spoke again of Frances. "He is such a fine, jolly young fellow — clean looking chap and especially well mannered, yet he don't spoil everything by "showing off." I'm going to try to break through the ice enough to learn more about him."

"Now if I were you, I'd let things run along as they are and not worry about his private affairs. That's his business not ours."

"Oh, sure it is, sure it is," said her husband, "guess I'm over curious."

Sunday morning found the four of them in their pew at church. Many eyes turned on the young stranger and especially those belonging to the younger generation. After services several of these whom Parepa had met gathered round to speak to her and to be introduced to the dashing young gallant by her side.

She was real generous and glad to have them meet him. Finally the choir director came toward them. Aunt Kate used to sing under his direction and he always begged her to continue singing as he said, "No voice has ever taken the place of yours." Now he was shaking hands with them. "And who are these fine children you have with you, Mrs. Slay?"

Uncle George beamed on them as he introduced them.

"Couldn't I interest them in my junior choir? We need their voices so much."

"I shall be leaving Peoria soon," said Frances, "'Twould hardly be worth starting now."

"I'd love it," said Parepa.

So it was arranged then and there that she was to attend rehearsal every Thursday night.

Frances sat with them on the porch for some time that afternoon. Uncle George was sure he caught him looking longingly at Parepa several times.

"I must go now, good people," he said, "and write a letter to Daddy Wheeler. By the way did I tell you we're to leave most any day now?"

"Then what will I do for someone to play with?" said Parepa.

"Oh, you will get acquainted fast now that you are going to sing in the junior choir," he returned. "I wish I were going to be here."

"We wish you were too," said Aunt Kate, "we'll all miss you so much. But, of course, you'll write to us."

"Don't forget us when you come back to America," added Uncle George.

"I'll never forget you — and Parepa," taking both hands into his. "You'll write to me real often won't you?"

"Surely," she said and blushed a pretty pink as he squeezed her hands.

"Good night," and he swung himself across the street.
