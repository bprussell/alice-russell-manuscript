# Chapter XVIII

At promptly five-thirty Frances pulled up in front of the Slay home with a beautifully matched span of coal black beauties. Parepa was ready and Aunt Kate stood on the porch and waved a long goodbye as they cantered down the shady street.

"We'll have two hours before dusk," said Frances. "Which way shall we go?"

"Oh, let's ride all through Glen Oak Park. I never get tired of that place. It's heaven to me and it's shady there on the drives," she replied.

"Fine! I like it too," and he pulled his horse closer to hers.

"There isn't so much riding in this country as in England," he said.

"No, it isn't a national sport," she said. "Tell me about England and what you do there. Are we so very different?"

"There's only one thing that's really so *very* different as I've found, and that's—well, that's you," he hesitated.

"Me! Am I really a novelty then? Maybe I should have gone on the stage after all. Be in a cage, you know, and charge so much to see me," she teased.

"No, you shouldn't let anyone see you but me. I was so jealous the other night when everyone went crazy about you, I hardly knew how to control myself. Couldn't bear the thoughts of your making a show for the amusement of hundreds who didn't know what a sweet thing you were. You'll never do that again, will you?" he begged.

"Did I really do so wrong? Did I cheapen myself and disgrace my friends? Really did I? I only wanted to surprise you all, but not one of my dearest friends have offered congratulations."

"Your act was refined and you were wonderfully sweet. I guess that's what I hated about it. Everyone got as much out of it as those who care for you."

They were in the park now at the Perry Street entrance. Their horses fell into a walk as they ascended the hill. "Isn't this wonderful," she gasped. "Seems as if I just want to keep still and enjoy it," and he agreed.

They rode along side by side until they reached the crest of the hill. "Let's go round by the conservatory," suggested Parepa. "Oh, aren't the flower beds pretty?" They followed the road around the pavilion and conservatory then down the hill on the other side to the rustic bridge.

"Let's get off and watch the falls," suggested Frances.

"Fine! I'd love to." He took the reins and tied them to a nearby tree. They had chosen a time of day when the park was most deserted. They stood watching the water trickle over the rocks for some time, neither saying a word. Finally Frances suggested they sit down on the turf near the stream and talk. "We have dreamed long enough," he said. "It's time we got better acquainted, don't you think?"

"I'm enjoying the beauty all around us so much that I can think of nothing else," she admitted.

"And I'm enjoying the most beautiful thing in the park until I can't think of anything else," he said. "That's you."

"Now Frances, don't be silly and spoil everything by talking foolish," and she sank down beside him half curious to hear what he wanted to say. She thought of Uncle Will's advice: "Learn more about him before it's too late," he had said. She settled herself to listen to his story more as a duty to her Uncle than because she really cared to know. In fact she felt herself afraid to hear his story.

"Parepa do you know I can't bear to think we are going to be separated by the big ocean again for two years. There wasn't an hour in the past two years I didn't see you in my mind's eye, and now that I've found you even more beautiful and good I simply can't stand it. Do you feel one bit like that about me, Parepa?" and he kissed the hand he had been holding. "Tell me, dearest, please tell me you do."

"I did miss you like everything and no one else is like you, Frances, but of course we can't always be together. That's foolish for such young kids as us to expect. I think it's great to be together like this and I feel—well I feel so—so safe with you. I know you won't harm me, you know, and I like being with you all alone here in the park." She raised two blue eyes to his and they fastened their gaze as if clinched by a love that only youth knows.

"Parepa!" he cried and clasped her tight to him. She did not object and the kiss that lingered long on their lips told only one story. The story of pure unadulterated first love. The love that can never be forgotten no matter what stormy seas may sail their life boat thereafter.

"Oh Frances, do you suppose this is—is—love I'm feeling?"

Frances laughed aloud. "I'm sure it is dearest and I'm so happy."

"But look Frances it's getting dark. We must be going for Aunt Kate will be worried." They swung into the saddle and cantered up the hill just in time to see a most beautiful sunset.

"Oh! Look Frances, the most beautiful sunset I ever saw. Why do you suppose I love everything that is pretty, Frances?" she asked.

"Because it's just like you," he answered. "You should never have to contend with anything but the most beautiful in your lifetime and if I can help it you won't," he said, then added, "And I'm sure I can."

They left the park from the upper entrance. It was just twilight when they bade each other goodbye.

"Did you have a good time?" asked Aunt Kate.

"Oh, the best time I ever had. We rode and rode and the flowers, the streams, and the sunset were beautiful. Aunt Kate why am I so possessed when I see pretty things? I can hardly tear myself away from them. I forget everything else. I seem immovable."

"I'm glad you can see the beauty the good Lord gives us so freely. Lots of us pass by all that, and miss the most precious things in life," said good kind Aunt Kate.

"Sit down, Parepa, I want to talk to you about some very important things."

"Yes, Auntie," she sang. "I shall be glad to listen."
