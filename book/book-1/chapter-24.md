# Chapter XXIV

"After we had landed and comfortable quarters were located I lived for months as one in a trance. Seeing nothing, hearing nothing, knowing nothing but my baby. The Doctors sailed on the first ship for America. It was an ill fated ship and sank—both Doctors were drowned. This sealed the news of the birth of a living child and its identity was lost.

"I forgot to tell you that only those of our party knew its personnel or the purpose of this voyage. The general opinion of the passengers on the ship was that Anna Bell and I were husband and wife. Our party kept its own counsel and made no new acquaintances. The six of us stayed close to our reservation on the ship. Our meals were served in our apartment and the every wish of our party was catered to at the ring of a bell. None of us were on a pleasure trip. The Doctors centered all their thoughts in study while the rest of us tried to make the time pass as pleasantly as possible for Anna Bell. She having the highest spirits in the party.

"The nurse, Frances, and I lost ourselves in Great Britain for several years. As my baby grew to childhood I could see the features of his mother developing rapidly. He was the same little child my father had reared so carefully in our home. Those snappy dark eyes, curly black hair, ruddy complexion, lithe body, charming ways, and lovable disposition.

"Of course, this child must be raised a real boy, no sissy ways for him. His pleasures, his exercise, his associates must be of the kind that make for the better man. With this end in view I have followed a carefully planned schedule all these years. You can see what the result has been. A bright, alert, well proportioned and well balanced youngster, twenty years old the same day Parepa was sixteen."

"Why didn't you tell us, Mr. Wheeler, and we would have had two birthday cakes," asked Mrs Slay.

"We did not intend ever to tell you but since these two are so wrapped up in each other I felt I should come to you before we leave and tell you, who have been so kind to this young girl, a few of the facts as they exist."

"But what was in the locket, Mr. Wheeler?" asked Mrs. Slay.

"I am afraid I cannot tell you. However, it would greatly affect the home life of these two children should they decide to join hearts and hands for life. That is why I came here. So you would not be deceived by us. Anna Bell was determined her family should not be propagated farther than her son. So far we have executed her every desire. Frances is satisfied it was a wise plan his mother thought out for him and is thoroughly determined to follow it to his death. No doubt Parepa has a strong mother instinct. This could never be gratified," and Mr. Wheeler wiped a tear from his eye.

"I would like to ask you just one question, if I may," said Mr. Slay.

"Certainly," replied Mr. Wheeler.

"Did the operation Frances had gone through before he came to Peoria two years ago render him incapable of being the father of Parepa's children?"

As Mr. Wheeler did not answer at once Mr. Slay said a little irritably, "Answer my one question, please, Mr. Wheeler."

"I can truthfully say it did not in any way incapacitate him for being a father."

"Believe me my dear friends this has been the hardest thing I have ever had to do for Frances. Please do not let he or Parepa know of this visit. I did it that you might not be entirely in ignorance of his origin. Can I trust you to keep as much of his secret as I have disclosed to you?"

"You certainly can," they said in unison.

"We will never betray a word of your confidence, good friend," said Mr. Slay as they bid farewell to this stranger who was playing such a big part in their lives at present.

Aunt Kate set about preparing lunch for the four of them. She was just ready to put the salad together when Parepa and Frances came in radiantly happy and full of the spirit of the day. Frances dropped into a chair beside Uncle George while Parepa went into the kitchen to help Aunt Kate.

"Oh!" she cried. "Salad! Just the kind I made the first night I came to live at your house. Let me put it together, Aunt Kate." She fixed it exactly as she had done two years ago.

"There's something about this lunch that makes me think of the first meal I ever ate here," said Frances. "What is it I wonder?"

"Maybe it's the tumblers like the one you broke when we were wiping the dishes," laughed Parepa.

"It's the salad! That's what it is," cried Frances. "That's what is so familiar."

"I made it then and I made it now. Have I improved any?" the girl questioned laughingly.

"Can't say you have—a salad such as that could never be improved upon. Many a time the memory of that salad has come to me as I claw over the hotel salads trying to relish them!" Turning to Mrs Slay he said, "I suggest you give this girl two years' training in domestic science while I'm away."

"Nothing doing!" cried Parepa. "Of all things on earth I detest it's anything that has to do with housework. Sometimes I think I'm not domesticated at all. When I get married I'm going to marry a man that can afford to hire all that done for us. You'll never find this kid cooking and washing and scrubbing—not much-e-e-e. I'm going to be a wife whose duty alone is to allure and charm her husband," and she placed her elbows on the table, folded her long slender fingers and coyly laid her head on her hands turning a roguish glance on Frances.

"That might work for a while," said Uncle George, "but I never knew a case where it worked for any length of time."

"It will in my case," laughed Parepa, "because my husband wouldn't want to fill an early grave eating my cooking."

"Where did you go this morning?" asked Aunt Kate wishing to change the subject.

"For a long ride in the country. It was wonderful," answered Frances.

After lunch they rested awhile and then dressed to go for a walk in the park. They stopped at the lake and took a rowboat for an hour. Laughed and chatted as if neither of them were thinking about this being their last day together.

Finally Parepa asked, "Did you say the lake on your estate was so big as this?"

"Oh yes, much larger and much prettier. Someday you'll see it, I hope. My sweetheart won't have to wash dishes," and he grinned mischievously at the girl facing him in the boat.

"Do you know Frances, I'm so sorry I let the cat out of the bag about my dislike for housework because Aunt Kate just can't understand any woman not loving her home duties and she will never want me to help her again since she knows I hate it. I do though—I hate it—I hate it—I hate it and I can't help it. I got more than my share of dishwashing to do when I was a little girl, and peeling potatoes! Ye Gods! I've peeled potatoes enough in my young life to fill this lake. And how the flies did bite my bare legs as I sat on the back porch podding peas. I had lots of nice dreams though while I was doing these things. I'd dream and dream about the beautiful house and gardens I'd have when I got married. I'd have goldfish too and a birdcage with little yellow birds in it. And books! Oh what beautifully bound books I'd have. My house had lots of windows and sunshine and beautiful paintings, and wide stair steps with banisters and I had Oh, so many trees and paths in my yard! I remember how I'd imagine myself standing in my yard under the trees singing to the birds in a beautiful voice and the birds would gather round me and sing their answer song. In my dreams I never had to cook, wash dishes, put clean papers on the cupboard shelves (as was my job every Saturday) or scour the wooden handled knives and forks with ashes from the wood stove, wash windows, scrub the kitchen tabletops with sand till they fairly shone, and the cobs and coal—I never had to carry them, in my dreams."

Frances laughed outright at this young girl's frankness. "And what did your fine husband do, and look like in all these mystic moments?" he asked.

"That's the funny part of it, Frances, I can't remember of having one. Seems I was so happy just having all the beautiful things I wanted, my husband was the least of my thoughts. I had children though, the sweetest ones in the world, but really I can't remember a thing about my husband. I can remember too, of how many, many gifts I gave to folks. I was never so happy in my dreams as when I was giving giving giving to someone and doing all sorts of things to make folks happy. Seemed as if the money supply was never exhausted or at least I didn't worry about it as long as I had plenty to give with.

"Wasn't those funny dreams, Frances? I remember of gathering arm after arm full of beautiful flowers and giving them to anyone who loved them. I didn't care to have anything that I couldn't share with others. My pretty house was always full of folks."

"Wasn't that a queer way to be Frances? I don't believe any other youngster ever had dreams like mine. My sisters always dreamed too, but they always had lovers and husbands and babies—lots of them."

Frances and Parepa both fell into silence each were deep in their own thoughts.

Finally Frances broke the spell by saying: "Do you know, Parepa darling, you can have every one of those dreams come true if you want to."

"Oh, go on—there is always a man in the real live dreams and he always has a stomach," she laughed. "That means cooking."

The whistle blew telling them their hour was up for the boat.

Frances rowed to the edge and they left the lake.

"Oh, let's sit in the rose arbor," and Parepa led the way to a seat overlooking the lake from a distant hill. "It's always so pretty here," she said. "I love roses, don't you?"

"They are my favorite flower," he said, "and these that bloom all summer are a great improvement over the old fashioned ones," he said. "But let's talk about something else. Parepa could you be happy all your life if you could have things just as those childish dreams portrayed? I mean if you and I could agree to share our lives together forever? I have told you before I can never be the father of your children—but would you be happy never to bear children of your own? That is what it would mean, and I want you to understand that thoroughly. I don't know why I have allowed myself to so completely fall in love with any girl, knowing this horrid fact as I have all my life."

She made no answer.

"I do not want an answer now, Parepa dear," he said as his arm slipped round her waist. "Wait until I come home again. In the meantime I want you to do something for me."

"And what can I do for you, my dear lord and master?" she asked pushing her tiny hand into his and smiling at him.

"I want you to give yourself up to a glorious good time for the next two years. Go to everything that's jolly, have lots of fellows, take trips, sing and be happy always. Do you promise?"

"Why—why—I'll do my best to please you," she laughed.

"Good! Then you'll sort of forget about me, that is—I mean—you won't feel bound by any promise to me," he said.

"You'll read my letters if I write to you though won't you?" she asked pouting her pretty lips out at him.

"Oh, yes, I want your letters—indeed I do—but let's not mention what we have talked over this afternoon. However, when I come back in two years I shall expect a final answer. It must be a true one then and I promise never to worry you or even see you again if you decide against me. Feel free to decide your own future regardless of my happiness."

"Frances—" she hesitated—"I can tell you my answer right now," and she put her face up for him to kiss. He kissed her over and over again but did not ask her answer.

"Do you know you are different from most boys I know, Frances?"

"Yes, darling, I am different. I know I'm different from other boys," he said sadly.

"You don't understand me," she protested. "I mean I feel different with you. I'm really afraid of other boys—their very look scares me, but with you it's different. I know you won't harm me," and again she squeezed his hand and laid her tawny head on his shoulder.

He kissed her hair again and again. "No, I wouldn't harm one hair on this head," he said.

Again they sat silently basking in their love for one another. Finally Frances said, "What are your plans for study these next two years?"

"I haven't any," she said frankly. "I am going to spend my time helping Uncle George and Aunt Kate. You know I couldn't be an expense to them now that he has had to give up work. Mrs Breen has great plans for she and I to go about giving recitals over the nearby country. She is booking dates for us now. She will be my accompanist when I sing and we will both give readings and short sketches for two. Won't it be fun? I can still keep my job at the candy store."

Again they sat silent Parepa picturing herself on the platform of small town Churches or halls while Frances was trying to plan out a way of leaving a bank account for this independent young thing that she might keep on with her music lessons at least. It was growing dusk now and Parepa sprang from her seat with an "Up! Up! my lad! 'Tis time to flee."

They walked home hand in hand. When they reached the house Parepa recognized the neighborhood Doctor hurrying into their house.

"What can be the matter? Someone is sick," and Parepa broke into a run. As they entered they saw Aunt Kate tiptoe into the bedroom with an anxious look on her face. Parepa followed. The Doctor was bending over Uncle George who was breathing heavily.

"Just a slight stroke," he told Aunt Kate. "He will come out of it all right."

Parepa slipped out to where the boy was standing and told him what she had heard. They went out on the porch and said their rather sad goodbyes. Mrs Slay came to the door with the Doctor and bade Frances goodbye. "I'm sorry George isn't able to see you. You know how much we all love you and wish you your happiness, I'm sure."
