# Chapter XIV

The telephone rang. "That's Parepa calling to see if there's anything I want her to bring home."

"Hello! Oh, is it? So glad to hear your voice. Where are you? Oh! that would be great fun. No, I'll not tell her. Yes, will look for you at eight."

"That was Frances. He's at the Hotel Fey. Wants to surprise Parepa tonight. Said he'd be out about eight o'clock. She surely will be surprised."

"Well, how are we going to contain ourselves until that time comes is what I want to know. Is the 'boss' coming too?" asked Mr. Slay.

"You mean Mr. Wheeler? Oh, he is tired and won't come this time he said. I'm glad of it myself," confided Mrs Slay. Wonder why Frances calls him "dad," pondered Mrs. Slay.

"Oh just natural for him I suppose being with him all his life and never knowing any other dad. You know I feel mighty sorry for that young chap even if he has got all the money he can use," and Mr. Slay lit a cigar and seated himself on the sofa. "Wheeler must have a lonely life, too. I asked Frances once if his guardian had never married and he avoided a direct answer. They seem to be keeping each other's secrets pretty well guarded."

"Yes, I suspect there is a deep mystery connected with both their lives but I can't believe they are indecent people at that," and she shook a sofa pillow hard and finally dropped down beside her husband, tired and flushed.

"We mustn't forget the kids are older now and both have taken on more dignity and reserve," she went on. "You know Parepa will be eighteen the 17th of August. Of age if you please, Mr. George Slay! Our little girl will be of age. I hadn't thought of that before. Well if I do say it there isn't one in a thousand her age as well prepared to make their own living. Just think, George, in two years you and I have made it possible for this dear child to be what she alone knew she could be—a real actress—a real song bird."

"Yes, and there is a real surprise in store for this young chap when he sees her tonight," laughed Mr. Slay. "Let's having her sitting at the piano singing when he comes. You and she singing a duet and I'll let him in."

"Fine! We'll do that and then we will all see the fun. Well, I must get us a bite to eat now," and she hurried to the kitchen all excited.

Mr. Slay left to himself sat to thinking over the day he brought Parepa from her country home in that old tea wagon. He could see the cunning freckles on her nose as she turned her face to his and said, "Yes, and from now on don't you ever forget it, I'm sixteen years old." And I promised her I'd never tell, he mused. And I never have, not even the best friend the child could ever have, my wife. I wonder if I have done wrong. Frances too, thinks she will be of age. I believe the girl believes it herself. Anyway she has never referred to it to me.

When Parepa came home she went about her usual performances innocent of the great surprise in store for her.

At the table that evening Uncle George teased, "Come now, Peppy, tell your old Uncle when the young Lochinvar is coming?"

"Oh, some sweet day by and by," sang Parepa. "Don't know exactly. Will let you know in time to get your 'boiled' shirt on," she laughed.

"Let's see—how old is that young scamp now, 'bout eighteen, eh?" he quizzed.

"I guess so, but don't you know I can't remember of his ever telling us how old he was. Do you, Aunt Kate?" she asked turning to her for an answer.

"Only that when I went over to ask Frances to come to supper with us the night you brought Parepa home with you the old lady told me she thought he was close to sixteen. Don't know how she knew. Just guessed it maybe," she said.

Parepa glanced sideways at Uncle George and he at her. Both knew well what the other was thinking. Then she said, "Do you remember how that Mrs. Jackson guessed me sixteen that day I was in the tea wagon, Uncle George?" she smiled.

"I'll never forget it. Her girl Ruth was sixteen, too, I believe," he chuckled.

"And our little tea wagon darling is soon going to be of age. Eighteen years old, Parepa, in just one more month. Then you will be your own boss. Think of it! Not our little girl any more. Oh! Parepa, Dear little girl, it don't seem possible," and Aunt Kate really felt a tear trickling down her cheek.

"Got a long way to go yet before you are as old as we'ens," laughed Uncle George.

"Yes, or as wise either, or as good to everybody," and she jumped up and kissed one and then the other. "I'm so happy I can hardly stand it and I don't know why. I'm sure it's not my approaching birthday because that seems awfully old for one so young," she laughed.

Only Uncle George knew what she was thinking.

After the work was done up they read the papers and at ten minutes to eight Aunt Kate suggested they sing some together.

"I'll sit on the porch and smoke," said Uncle George.

Parepa liked nothing better so she took her place at the piano and picked out a half dozen of their most beautiful duets. They sang one of them, another and while singing the third one Uncle George and Frances came in. Frances came quietly behind Parepa and clapped his hands over her eyes, kissed her a loud smack, and said, "Guess who?"

"Frances!" she almost screamed and the song of the voice ceased but the song of the heart reigned supreme. "Oh, how did you ever do it?" she asked blushing prettily.

"What a big girl you are. How tall are you?" And he held her off arm's length as if appraising her.

"And what big ears you've got," she said pinching one of them coyly.

"Better to hear you with," he laughed and pulling her down beside him on the sofa said, "You all look so good to me. I feel as if I had come back home."

"You have, my boy, just as long as you behave yourself we'll not object to your calling this home, will we mother? The world has certainly been kind to you these last two years," said Uncle George.

"The best I ever had. Not a sick day and wonderful progress with my studies."

Parepa was most overcome at the sight of so handsome and well poised young fellow. His hair lay in dark waves over his head, his shoulders were square, his cheeks full, eyes bright, and manner—kindly. He's a full blooded all right, judged Uncle George and Aunt Kate was pleased.

They spent the evening talking and laughing over "old times" (two years ago) as though it had been fifty.

Finally Frances left for the hotel saying he would call them up soon and they would all go to the Opera. "He hoped it wouldn't be his luck to find Uncle Tom there. Ha! Ha!"
