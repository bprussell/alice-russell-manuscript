# Chapter XIX

The 'phone rang, Robert answered it. "It's long distance, Mother, they want to talk to you."

Allie took the receiver shakingly in her hand.

"Hello," she said.

"This is a reporter for the Peoria Tribune speaking, Mrs. Bradford. Were you, before your marriage, Miss Alice Parepa-Roza Wesley?" the voice inquired.

"I was," she answered calmly.

"And did you ever reside with relatives in the neighborhood of the Bethany Baptist Church in Peoria?" he quizzed.

"I did," was her answer.

"I want to advise you, Mrs. Bradford, that we received a communication from a party in Arizona, whose name I will withhold for the present, asking us to make an effort to locate you."

"Thank you very much," was her only reply.

"This person, it seems, is in very poor health and is desirous of locating you immediately." A pause followed. "Have you a message?" he asked.

"None, I thank you."

"We published this communication in this evening's paper, hoping to hear from someone who knew of your whereabouts, and have been kept busy answering 'phone calls from friends of yours ever since." He waited for a reply. There was none and he continued. "I'd advise you to get in touch with him as soon as possible. It may be to your advantage."

"Thank you very much," she said as if to close the conversation.

"Would you like to know the signature to the message?"

"You may tell me if you care to," she said.

"It is signed, J.C. Wheeler, Sec."

"Oh, yes," she gasped. "Thank you so much," and she hung up the receiver.

When she faced her family, four pair of inquiring eyes were turned to hers. Before she could answer their unspoken question, the 'phone rang again.

"Peoria calling," said the operator. "Go ahead, please."

"Hello, is this you, Allie?"

"Yes, it is I."

"Well, say," the voice went on, "have you seen tonight's paper?"

"No, I haven't."

"Well, someone in Arizona is looking for you. There's a long piece about it. Can you imagine who it can be?"

Allie recognized the voice of an old schoolmate whom she knew to have developed into a full fledged gossip. Of all people, she was one not to be confided in.

"I expect there are a lot of folks there," she fenced.

"Yes, but this must be a man, and I bet he's rich. The piece in the paper reads as if he might be going to die and wants to leave you something. Wouldn't that be grand?"

"Oh, I'm afraid you are letting your imagination run away with you. I'm sure there is no man hunting me," she answered quickly.

"Well, if it didn't cost so much to talk over long distance, I'd take time to read you what it says in the paper. Really, I must hang up now before I run up a bill. Let me know all about who it is and what he wants when you find out. Will you, Allie?"

"Thank you so much, Bess. Goodbye," said Allie, and she turned again to face inquiring eyes.

"What's it all about, Wifie?" asked her patient husband.

"Oh, it seems as if someone who knew me when I went to the Bethany Church in Peoria is trying to locate me again," she said as she turned to answer the 'phone, which was ringing frantically. The following conversation took place:

"This is Mrs. Bradford?"

"Peoria is calling you, hold the line," said the operator.

Allie heard the coins tinkle into the 'phone box. She hoped no one was going to repeat the news.

"Hello, Parepa. I bet you will never guess who is talking to you."

"No, I don't recognize your voice."

"Well, it's an old friend of thirty years ago. Do you remember Frank Johnston of old Bethany picnic days?"

"Why, yes, I do. But what brings you here?" she asked laughingly.

"Oh, wait till I tell you. In the paper tonight I saw your name in the headlines. I remembered you well and set about to find out your present name. Minnie Bryden told me, and I'm calling up to tell you that I think you are in for some good luck. I hope you will pardon me."

"CERtainly, but what good luck do you refer to? I am altogether in the dark."

"Well, some fellow in Arizona asked the Tribune to try to find you. Seems he has tried to locate you for years. Said he wrote Joe Donald, of course you remember Joe, some years ago, but the letter was returned to him. He has tried radio and everything. Seems he's sick and wants to find you before he kicks off," he laughed.

"Oh, don't say that, I'm sorry," she said.

"I believe I know who the fellow is. Do you remember that young millionaire that use to show up in vacation and take you off your feet? You wouldn't look at the rest of us fellows when he was around." As she didn't answer, he continued, "Now don't pretend you have forgotten him."

"I'm sure I remember the party you refer to real well, but I do not know why I should be subject to all this publicity."

"Well, as I told you, he is mighty anxious to locate you. You can find out all about it by calling the Tribune office."

"Thank you so much, Frank—Mr. Johnston," she faltered.

"Oh, call me Frank. By the way, tell me all about yourself. Married, I hear, got a family?"

"Yes, a real one. The best husband in the world and three wonderful boys, all as big as their mother. How about yourself?" She was surprised at herself—being so friendly with a man she hadn't seen since a boy.

"Oh, I'm married and happy. Two girls and two boys—all grown up and in college. Can't complain. I've been treated mighty good," he said.

"I'm so glad to hear from you again. I often think of those good old Bethany days," she found herself living again in her excitement with dear old Aunt Kate.

"Come down and renew acquaintances. I'll ring off now, goodbye, Parepa, and good luck."

"Thank you. Good night, Frank."

Without turning from the 'phone she called central. "Hello, operator? This is Mrs. Bradford. If there are any more long distance calls come in for me, will you kindly inform them I cannot answer any more calls tonight?"

"All right, Mrs. Bradford. I'll see that you are not bothered anymore."

"Now maybe I can talk to my family," she said, seating herself at her place at the table. Her eyes fell on the locket outlined on the cloth. Quickly she turned away. Why should that haunt her so tonight? "Now I'll try to tell you all I have heard. The first 'phone call was from the Peoria Tribune office. He said they had a letter from someone living in Arizona wanting them to put forth an effort to locate me."

"Why, Mom, what in the world are they going to do with you?" asked Arthur.

"Oh, nothing at all, dear. Mother can't imagine what they want to find her for, but we don't have to worry about there being anything disgraceful about it. Probably some old Sunday school mate or something," she hoped to quiet their fears.

"Maybe they want to kidnap her," laughed Daddy.

"All I know to do is to wait and see," and Allie rose to clear the table. The boys helped her. One after another offered suggestions to what might be the meaning of it all. Allie had little to offer. She knew as well as if she had been told by the party itself. As the last dishes were placed in the cupboard, a sharp rap was heard on the door. Johnie opened it. A stranger entered.

"I may be taking liberties, Mr. Bradford, but I have with me a copy of tonight's Peoria Tribune, which will explain to you my visit here. I have come to interview your wife." He opened it wide and held it out in front of him so they could all read the headlines.

Allie entered the room, and Johnie introduced her to the reporter. He handed the paper to her. Her eyes fell on the bold words, "Search for Alice Parepa-Roza Wesley." Has anybody here seen "Alice"? The following communication was received today by the Peoria Tribune.
