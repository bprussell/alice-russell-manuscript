# Chapter XII

"Well, why don't you and your wife take a trip out there for a few months anyway? You'll know more about how you'll like it by that time," the doctor answered. "You really should go soon."

When Johnie broke this news to Allie, she did not discourage it. Instead she sat about planning a way in which the children's welfare would be looked after during their absence. This settled satisfactorily, she took the matter of funds into her own hands. Obtaining a loan of five hundred dollars from the first person she approached on the subject.

It was the fifteenth of July when they boarded the train bound for Denver, Colorado. Allie had but one thing in mind. Her good husband's health. They must find a health giving location. They both enjoyed the ride through the country immensely. They were real chummy. This was because each was determined the other should not have time to get homesick for the children.

They practiced the strictest economy in traveling, limiting their appetites to the necessities and ignoring the otherwise tempting dishes offered in the dining car. Each seemed to have promised themselves that they would make the other's trip the happiest event of their lives, and each succeeded.

They arrived in Denver about nine-thirty p.m. and were taken at once to a comfortable hotel. They had several families of relatives in this city but preferred to rest up a day or so before making their presence known to them.

The second day they called an aunt by 'phone. When she learned their location, she insisted upon sending a car for them immediately. From that time on, life was a whirl of action. They enjoyed every minute of it. There were several cousins in homes of their own who entertained them royally. They went for long drives into the mountains, took train trips to the "Top O' the World" and other places of interest, and felt on leaving the third week as though they had left nothing unseen. At Pueblo were more cousins. Here they enjoyed camping in the mountains in the summer home of one of Pueblo's leading families. They spent a most wonderful week here. How they ate and slept! Every breath seemed to give new life to the one whose health was the excuse for this trip.

Colorado Springs was a delight from the time they arrived at the Acacia Hotel, which they made their stopping place, until they boarded the train for Boulder. Manitou with its wonderful mineral springs, Pikes Peak, which was visited by Allie only, and the many side trips made by auto made an ever changing panorama of beauty.

They arrived in Boulder on Saturday. The good people into whose home they were welcomed were cousins of Allie's mother. A dear old couple, kindly and entertaining. They had lived here for many years. Mr. Teagarden, having been the leading real estate man of the city, knew the country like a book. He had been instrumental in closing more sales for land and city property than any Realtor for miles around.

When he learned that Johnie was looking for a farm near the mountains, he knew at once the best buy in the country. It joined a farm of his own. Was only a short distance from Boulder and was as much land as Johnie should handle. After riding for several days, they decided on this one, providing they could raise the money from their home and second mortgage to make first payment.

From there they went to Estes Park and spent several days. They were getting anxious to get home now that they had decided what they wanted to do. When they arrived home, they found everything running along as smoothly as if the heads of the family had been in attendance all the time.

The little boys felt very important telling of the things they did to help Auntie and Grandma. Nevertheless, they were happy to have Mother and Daddy home again.

Johnie called on his doctor the next day. The old physician stared at him in wonder, "I can hardly believe it's you," he said. "Surely you have found the right place for you to live." Johnie told him of the plans he had made.

"If I can get the money that is due March first for the mortgage, I'll be able to make this Colorado deal. If not, I can't deal any place."

"I'd suggest that you go out and see the fellow that owns the land your mortgage is on and have an understanding whether or not he will be able to pay it off when due. If he can't pay it, you better sell it to someone for cash. I'll go with you to see this fellow today."

That afternoon they drove to the farm and found the owner busy with his full blood Hampshire hogs, of which he was justly proud. After visiting awhile, Johnie made his errand known, telling the farmer his position and asking him if he were sure he could pay off the seven thousand, five hundred dollar mortgage when due March first.

"Sure, there's no doubt about it," replied the man. "I'll have the cash for you then. Just go ahead with that Colorado deal and don't worry over that mortgage money."

The contract, which had been drawn up before they left Colorado and was ready for Johnie's and Allie's signature when they made sure they could raise the money, called for a payment of two thousand, five hundred dollars down and five thousand March fifth, at which time possession was to be given. The old doctor read the contract carefully.

"Do you happen to have this two thousand, five hundred dollars on hand?" he asked.

"No, we thought we would sell our house and realize about three thousand on it. I'm sure we can sell it," said Johnie.

"Yes, no doubt you can, but a hurried sale is not always a good sale. I suggest you borrow some money and take your time to sell. You will be more likely to get your price," suggested the doctor. "If you will let me, I'll loan you the money, and this contract can be signed and on its way."

So it was settled. In January their city home was sold, possession to be given March fifth. All preparations for leaving Peoria were made.

On February twenty-fifth an old neighbor from the country called on Johnie and advised him to make sure he was going to get the mortgage money. He told how the owner of the farm had lost everything and how his creditors were closing in on him from all sides, "The only way you'll ever get anything out of your second mortgage will be to take the farm back," he said.

This proved to be the case. Not only did he have to take the place back, but there were back taxes, back interest on the first twelve thousand mortgage, and lots of tedious and expensive legal transactions to be paid for. It was impossible to go on with the Colorado land deal. Nevertheless, the court held them liable and the Bradfords found themselves greatly in debt with a load of financial worries such as they had never known before.
