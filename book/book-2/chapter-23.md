# Chapter XXIII

Parepa was shocked at first to see the form of her ardent lover of the past clad in richest feminine garb. Frances held out her arms to her. Parepa fell to her knees beside her and for a long time neither spoke as they held cheek to cheek in fondest embrace.

"So good of you to come, dearest," Frances broke the spell.

"I wanted to come," she said patting her hand.

"You have made me better already. I have gained ever since we got your message. I'm so glad we found you."

Parepa rose and Daddy placed a chair for her.

"We got thirty-two telegrams and letters in answer to our letter to the paper," she said and added, "I think we know all there is to know about you now."

"Then I won't have to tell you," she laughed.

"It seems good to see you looking so well, Parepa. I'm so glad you are happily married and have a family. It was best, wasn't it dearest?" and Frances smiled up into her face that old smile that Parepa never could forget.

"You haven't changed much Frances," she said encouragingly. "I expected to see a real invalid."

"It's my heart," she said. "I have to be careful." Just then a nurse came into the room. Frances introduced them and added, "This is my best girlhood chum. She has come from Illinois to see me."

"That's fine. Now Miss Emmerson I'm afraid you will have to excuse yourself and take some rest." Turning to Parepa she added, "I'm sorry, Mrs Bradford, but doctor's orders are orders you know."

"I'll see you after dinner, Parepa," and Frances was rolled through big doors into her sleeping room.

"Come Parepa to the garden," said Daddy. "I know you will love it."

"I'm crazy about gardens. Frances remembers that, I'm sure," she said. They wandered about for some time, Daddy explaining the rare specimens and origin and how they came into possession of them. It was all very interesting to Parepa.

Finally they came to a most welcome garden seat in the shade of some tall desert shrubs. "Let's set here and talk," he suggested. "There is much I must tell you though I hate to come to the subject. I know it is best to get what business we have to transact done as quickly as possible."

They sat down and he turned to her saying, "The doctors don't encourage me in thinking Frances can live more than a month or six weeks. She may go at any minute. It is her greatest desire to see you and your family well looked after, before that time comes. I think the transfers should be made tomorrow. All that is necessary is for Frances to transfer her holdings in the many banks to you. I'll see that they all go through and that everything is settled before you go home," he said almost in one breath.

Parepa sat silent.

Finally he asked, "Do you understand, Parepa?"

"Yes," she said, "but I have only allowed myself two weeks here."

"That will be long enough. We will fix it up tomorrow. Frances will not have to worry over it anymore. She will be so happy to know you will be looked out for. It has given her lots of pleasure getting things in shape for you. These investments are not due for fifteen and twenty years. They pay four percent and are nonassessable. They are as safe as anything in the world. She has some in the capital cities of every state in the Union and in some of the other large cities. We thought best not to invest much in any one bank. Not to put too many eggs in one basket you know," he chuckled as he saw Parepa's eyes open when large amounts were mentioned.

"You don't mean that Frances has that much money," she gasped.

"Yes, and lots left for herself, should she need it. I am to dispose of it to charities after she is through with it. Come, now, it's most dinnertime," and they strolled into the house.

Parepa and Daddy ate together in the beautifully appointed dining room, with all the service accompanying a formal dinner. Parepa was very tired. She hoped she could gracefully retire early.

This wish was quite naturally granted as Frances was only allowed to visit one half hour after dinner. She went to her sitting room and took from the writing desk daintily perfumed stationery. She wrote a long, long letter to her loved ones at home telling them of the beauty of the country, of Frances's condition and assured them of her own good health. After she had sealed the envelope her maid sent it to the post office with a servant.

There was lots of good reading on her table but she was so tired. "I believe I'll retire now," she told her maid. After a shower and brisk rub she slipped into her nightgown and after her prayers were said got into the most luxurious bed she had ever seen.

"I'll be in this room, Mrs Bradford," said her maid, "should you need me just push this button. Good night."

"Good night dear," and Parepa sank into a restful slumber. She did not waken through the night. It was nine o'clock the next morning before she dressed. Her breakfast was brought to her sitting room. She relished every morsel of it.

At ten o'clock she descended the stairs and found Daddy waiting for her in the library. After cheerful greetings were exchanged between them two nurses entered pushing the couch on which lay Frances. Beautiful as a flower, but as fragile. Her hair was as soft and wavy as when Parepa knew her and not one gray hair could be seen. The nurses left them alone.

"Oh, how beautiful you are Frances!" exclaimed Parepa. "I know you must feel fine today," and she stooped and kissed her.

"I do, thanks to you Parepa dear," she answered. "I'm all ready to hear all about my old sweetheart."

They all three laughed heartily over the joke. It really seemed to them now as if it had been one of fate's huge jokes.

"I want to hear your whole story since we parted. I see many interesting telltale impressions which time has left on your countenance. Has the world dealt a little hard with you sometimes, Parepa?" she asked.

"I wouldn't say hard, exactly," she answered. "But I have seen a great deal of joy and trouble in the last thirty years. Had I not had the companionship of the kindest husband in the world I never could have survived it. You can see we were not blessed with this world's goods. In fact there were times when we hardly knew where the next dollar was coming from. Yet, I have been a fortunate mother and wife and I'm not complaining."

"You have been a brave woman, Parepa. I am going to sign over to you a large amount of money, this very day," she said. "It makes me so happy to do this," pressing Parepa's hand tightly. "I have never forgotten for a minute and my only regret is that I did not find you sooner."

"It is best that you didn't. I have had a lesson in real life that is priceless. If you really want me to have this money I am sure I have a better idea of how to use it in a way that will do the most people the good, for having felt the need of it myself," she assured him.

"You taught me many years ago that 'The earth is the Lord's and the fullness thereof.' I never forgot the many tales you and I had together on this subject. Being convinced that you were right, I have used my surplus income from this money each year for charities. So far as possible I have made these gifts anonymous. I have been so happy in doing this that I feel no reproach against the life my mother chose for me. Our line will soon be broken Parepa. It is best that it should be. You are the one I have chosen to carry on for me. I am giving you this money for your own, to do as you wish with it. I have only one request. That is that you first build for yourself a home such as you and I talked of thirty years ago. I want this home to cost you at least five hundred thousand dollars. It is the only monument I want—just your home. That may seem extravagant to you but you will find it easy to use that much on grounds and buildings and don't forget our lake, our rose arbors, our wonderful trees. I have never forgotten your love for them all," and again they sat silently holding hands. Their memory chasing over the past years together.

"I'll try to carry out your every wish, darling," and Parepa kissed the hand she held tenderly. "But what is there in this world I can do for you in return?" she asked.

"I am the debtor dear," she answered. "I can never repay you for the slant on life you gave me. Of course, you didn't know it but you made it possible for me to go on living when I was ready to give up. Then, you were so brave when we parted. That gave me added determination. I was bound to go on doing all the good I could wherever I could. I have not increased my holdings since then. You remember all the Emmerson money was originally made on turpentine. There is not a stain or taint on it. It was made by fair dealing. You will never have to apologize for it," her eyes beamed. "My passing will close a book which only you and Daddy Wheeler have read. Let me pass quietly. I have given my orders. I do not want any publicity."

Daddy Wheeler entered the room with a stack of official looking envelopes in his hand. "Every one of them is ready for your signature, Frances," he said, "but you must rest until after noon." The nurses entered and rolled her from the room.

"Now, Parepa, we will go over these together so you will understand the business perfectly," and Daddy Wheeler drew his chair closer to hers. He explained to her the process of transferring the bank deposits from Frances's name into hers. "You won't have a bit of trouble taking care of them. The interest on each is payable yearly and they are divided up so some are due the first of every month. The banker will forward you a draft for the interest and you can deposit them wherever you care to. Your income will amount to a great deal per year. It is her wish that you be not restricted in any way. It is yours to do as you like with. However, she has put to your checking account in four banks the amounts of two hundred and fifty thousand dollars for your immediate needs. Here are the bank books and checks. You can use them immediately. They are already in your name."

He had her sign signature cards to accompany each transfer so there could be no question as to her identity. It was a long tiresome job and as she eyed the stack of cards with plainly written Mrs John Bradford on each, she heaved a sigh of relief that it was finished. "How can this money be in my name, now?" she questioned. "How could the bank books be in my name?"
