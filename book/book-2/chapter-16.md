# Chapter XVI

Surely this will be a good year, they would say on counting up the day's receipts. It was as far as the return from the garden was concerned, but it was not a good crop year. The early part of the season was too wet; the later part too dry.

It was the first day of oat harvesting. All the family had helped to get the old binder in running order. Allie mended the drapers while the boys ran errands for repairs as Johnie directed. Helped, I said, but to no avail. It was so antiquated no wonder it felt it had served its time and refused to work any longer.

Johnie inquired the price of a new one. Two hundred and sixty-five dollars was what one cost. Impossible! At the price of oats and the poor crop. They would have to get along the best they could with the old one.

About midnight Johnie was taken suddenly sick. His suffering increased rapidly in spite of all Allie could do for him. She suspected the nature of his trouble and called a specialist from Peoria. After asking some questions, he demanded he be brought at once to Peoria to the Methodist Hospital. There must be no delay. His condition was serious. In an hour he was in his room being examined by the doctor, having made the twenty mile trip in record time.

Allie and Robert, tired and worried from the toils and tribulations of the day and night, waited outside the door while busy nurses flitted in and out of the room. After what seemed hours to them, the doctor came out, solemn faced and serious. "He's a bit more comfortable now," he said. "An operation is inevitable, but we will have to reduce the inflammation first," and, seeing the question in Allie's eyes, he added, "It will take quite some time to bring him around, but I see no reason why he shouldn't come out all right."

Allie most collapsed. *How could he stand another operation?* she wondered. Then, remembering the superhuman endurance he had shown in times before, she took heart again. After Robert had bid his father goodbye and left for home and labor in the fields, Allie sat beside her husband's bed, quietly watching him as he slept from the drugs given him for that purpose.

A review of their whole life together passed through her mind. She studied her husband's features carefully. The world would not call them beautiful, yet Allie knew the moulding of every line of expression on that kindly face had been sculptured there by love's chisel. He did not dwell on his troubles, blunders, losses, or suffering long enough to permit a plastic impression of these unhappy things to be carved into his countenance. About his lips played the sure curves of strength and purpose. He was just a clean gentle everyday man who did his work gladly and happily each day. He had not a college trained mind but an educated heart and a graceful bravery which so many lack.

He turned his head on his pillow now and, opening his eyes, smiled faintly at Allie. Then, putting his hand in hers, he fell to sleep again. It wasn't a soft, well-manicured hand—instead, a calloused, rough one. The hand of one who cultivates and creates from the soil. Fingers of love and understanding were stretched out to touch her own, that better courage come to her. As she sat quietly thinking, these old familiar words came to her.

Why couldn't we shatter all the pain and suffering in the world? Why must we take whatever fate deals out to us in this world? she protested in her mind. Johnie opened his eyes again, "Don't worry, wifie, I'll be all right," he said and went to sleep again.

Morning light was streaming into the room now. A nurse offered Allie a place to rest. "I'll call you when he wakens," she promised. The minute her head touched the pillow, she was asleep. She was simply exhausted from the labor of the day before and the anxiety of the night.

It was nine-thirty when the nurse said softly, "Mr. Bradford is awake now."

Johnie was quite rested and felt comfortable. He insisted that he would be all right now and she should go home and not worry. Allie knew there was no one to get the meals for the boys so consented. She found them cutting the oats as best they could with the old binder. A trip to town for this or that repair ever so often was necessary. It was discouraging, to say the least. Nevertheless, these boys didn't give up. They weren't made of that kind of stuff.

Sometime during each day Allie and the boys visited Johnie. He was doing fine, and the doctor thought he would be able for the operation soon. He was worried over the expense connected with his sickness. Allie tried to tell him there was flower money enough to meet it. How many things she had counted on doing with the "flower money," which was kept by itself in a tin box on her dresser. There was most three hundred dollars now. The insurance was soon due, a tractor payment to be made, and a note for the last cow purchased, which would be due September first. There was no "mine or thine" at Bradfords. Everything was pooled.

All these things she had kept in mind as she placed dollar after dollar in the box. Now it was going just where so much of their hard earned money had gone. This amount would not pay the nurse and hospital bill, yet it represented returns of months of hard work for them. The operation would cost three hundred dollars, the doctor had said in answer to Johnie's question.

Allie and the boys were a discouraged band as they returned home that day.

The oat harvest was over and threshing was well under way by the time the doctor thought best to operate. They hauled Johnie's share of the oats direct to the elevator from the threshing machine and sold them for the low price of twenty-eight cents per bushel. By the time the twine and threshing bills were paid, only enough to pay up the hospital and doctor bills was left from their half of the entire oat crop. They tried to keep this fact from Johnie, but his pointed questions on the subject brought out the undisputed facts. This caused an unfavorable reaction, and his condition became worse.

When the day came for the operation, it was with fear and trembling that Allie waited for his return from the operating room. She knew his weakened condition better than the doctors themselves. There was born in this woman something which could never die; a pity and compassion for the weak, the sick, and the humble. Though she was powerless to prevent it, a mean resentment, too, possessed her now. How could she content herself to wait for the "silver lining" which she and Johnie had so often watched for?

Slowly it came into view. First, a tiny edging of silver shone from beneath the darkness. Then day after day the cloud dissolved into a gleam of shining metal. Johnie is well again.

"'Twas nothing but their untiring faith that saved him," said the old doctor, just as all the doctors before him had said.

The winter passed—spring was being welcomed in by the lengthening days, the singing of birds, and the April showers. Tulips and narcissus were springing from their winter beds and preparing for their grand bow to the hurrying motorists passing by. Once again Johnie and Allie were alive to the work that lay before them.

The boys had taken the responsibility of the farm into their own hands and were willing to put their time and strength against the weather, insects, grain prices, and politics of another year. Again this family was up before daylight, working willingly and trustingly until set of sun, hoping and praying that their efforts would be rewarded substantially when the harvest was over.

Everything they owned but the dahlia bulbs was mortgaged. They gave these securities willingly, for it was not their intention to best anyone.
