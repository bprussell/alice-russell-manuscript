# Chapter VIII

The doctor grew serious as soon as he examined him. "You may as well know it will be necessary for you to be in bed for some time. You have a serious case of pericarditis—that is, an inflammation of the membrane that surrounds the heart—there is also considerable fluid gathered there. You must be kept very quiet," he added.

Then, turning to Allie, asked, "Do you think you can take care of him, or shall I call for a nurse?"

"Oh, no, not a nurse. Let her take care of me. I want her to," pleaded Johnie, and the doctor consented.

"I'll do my best, Doctor, but if you think I'm not capable, we'll get a nurse," and she smiled her most sincere smile at her pale husband laying before her.

"You will be all right if you can stand it. It is going to take strenuous treatment for some time, Mrs. Bradford. The ice cap must be kept over his heart, a complete record kept of everything going in and coming out of his body, and accurate temperature and respiration report kept, his medicine must be given as directed day and night without a miss, beside the many other duties that are bound to present themselves." The doctor looked at Allie as if he expected her to turn the job over to a nurse, but he was mistaken.

"I can do all that, I'm sure," she said, and was rewarded by the relieved expression on her husband's face.

"I'm so glad you will try, Allie," he said weakly as the doctor began preparing the medicines. He wrote down the directions very plainly. Left her a chart for reports, an ice cap and thermometer for her use, and left with a feeling of confidence in her ability to care for her patient.

Kind relatives and neighbors did all they could do to help with the housework and farm work, but Allie was determined not to allow one dose of medicine or one little errand for Johnie to be done by anyone but herself. She made her bed on a cot beside him and so timed herself to his regular needs that she wakened involuntarily at the appointed time.

Days passed—weeks passed. The fluid was gone from the heart linings, only to appear in the linings of the lungs, first one and then the other, then back to the heart. Consultations of doctors were often held.

His condition at no time was such that he could stand the shock of having the fluid drawn from his body. Months passed—Allie was growing pale. She was looking worn and worried. The doctors were pleased with the way their patient was cared for, but they felt concerned about Allie now. She must have some rest. Johnie knew she must too. He would try to give up to having a stranger take her place in caring for him. She had been wonderful—she must rest now.

The doctor suggested it to her. "If I am not doing it well, I will gladly give up to someone else, but I like caring for him myself," she said.

"Well, we'll try a nurse for a couple of weeks anyway. That will give you a chance to catch up on your rest," said the kind old doctor.

It was not only the care of her husband that was telling on her. It was the fact that almost the last hog of the carload had died with cholera. Their best milk cow and baby calf died. The fall pigs were being burned by the pile each day, and grain prices had sunk to a low level. All this Allie had carefully kept from Johnie.

He was asking now, nearly every day, if the big hogs weren't ready to ship. The first of February had come. It was necessary to find a hired man to look after the farming. There was no money in the bank. The nurse and specialists had to be paid—what was she going to do?

When the two weeks were up, Parepa insisted she was able and willing to take up her duties as nurse again. She really had gotten no rest. She had more time to worry about other things. She succeeded in convincing them that she was rested, so it was decided to let the nurse go.

It was a continual fight from then on to master the awful disease that lurked in his body, but on the Fourth of July he was permitted to sit for a short time in a chair. It was over seven months since he had done this.

They were so happy again. Now he would get well. He would be his old self again. The doctor bills and everything else would be paid someday, and they would begin all over again.

On the seventeenth of August (Allie's birthday) he was able to take his first walk to the barn. The crops looked good. He had been told of the livestock losses so was prepared to see the hog pens empty. He was a brave man in more ways than one. He faced the future squarely. "We will make it all right, Allie," he said as he kissed her hand. "It's been hard and lonesome for you, but you've been wonderful, and someday you'll be proud of me. We'll get along. You see if we don't."

The doctor watched his progress carefully. The second year he recommended a change of occupation. Both their fathers had passed away. Their share in the estates was enough to buy the stock of general merchandise in a small town store in the little village of Speer, Illinois. Here Allie helped him, and together they worked long and hard hours.

If they had dared to believe store keeping was going to be an easy job, they were soon disillusioned. They were keeping sacred a secret these days. Whenever an opportunity presented itself, they would stand close together behind the counter and whisper about it. "I hope it's a girl," was Johnie's firm statement, "Just like its mother." And then there was a kiss and a hug before a customer would enter.

Allie didn't care what its sex, just so it was alright. Her whole mind was on this live thing near her heart. It was nearly five years since they were married. Happy, trying years. Years of sacrifice and suffering.

There had been nothing with which to satisfy the old Parepa's love for beautiful things. No time or money for pretty gardens, dainty fancy work, beautiful literature, or even the time to enjoy the sunsets or charming moonlight, which always held her spellbound. Nevertheless, she had not lost her love for beautiful things. Now and then she caught herself gazing into the bygone past. She could see the gardens, the lake, the beautiful mansion among the trees. The wide walks, the stables, with their fine steeds. But never did she see a husband, never did she warm to the approach of one man in these dreams. Never did she see her children playing in the nursery.

Frances had said, "Someday you will be a real mother, a proud one. I hope there will be lots of boys."

Indeed she was proud. So was Johnie.
