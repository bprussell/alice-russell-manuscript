# Chapter XVII

They had many orders for their dahlia bulbs, and now the time had come to open the bulb pit and fill these orders. Their dismay can be imagined when they found every bulb was rotted. They viewed the desolation with tears in their eyes. Allie was so overcome by this loss and disappointment that it was necessary for her to take her bed for a few days. The first time in her life she gave herself up to her feelings. Her courage was completely downed. She had met adversities bravely time after time. Now she was unable to overcome this great shock. Her fires of hope for this new year were burned away, leaving only their gray scattered ashes. It wasn't like Allie to give up this way. It worried Johnie, who depended so much on her strength and encouragement in times like this.

After all, did she not have a right to this state of mind? Why shouldn't she indulge in the resentment she felt against the unfair dealings of fate? Slowly she recovered her strength and courage and once again was ready to carry on just the same, start all over again and trust in God. She did not believe that "standing still and waiting" would bring back prosperity to them.

These simple people lived on faith. They liked the Bible definition of faith: "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." For a few days only their faith was wounded. At heart they were optimists whose sole food was faith. Faith in the God of the birds and of the flowers. Thankful were they always to this God for the free wealth of the beauties of this world that no billionaire even could buy and hide from them. They were ever humbly heroic. When they were troubled or discouraged, they thought of how much worse things might be and thanked their God for the many blessings received.

Time went on—the crops were in and up. Prospects were good up to the middle of June. Dry weather set in and stayed in all summer. Grains averaged about one half crop. Prices remained low. The "golden harvest" seemed anything but.

Allie's garden suffered many casualties through the summer that would try the sturdiest heart. In spite of this fact, she managed, by the kindly thoughtfulness of dear friends who donated to her all their surplus blooms, to satisfactorily fill all orders.

The debts which they had hoped to pay with crop returns must remain unpaid another year. Johnie and Allie sat on their back-porch this beautiful fall evening, trying to think out the best way to face the matter, when all at once Allie jumped to her feet with an exclamation of joy, and, taking Johnie's head between her two hands, she turned his face toward the west. "Look," she cried, "look at that beautiful sunset after this gloomy, rainy day. Isn't it wonderful?"

Johnie pulled her down on his lap and, kissing her, said, in his kindly way, "Yes, wifie, it is beautiful. Do you suppose our gloomy rainy life will end in a sunset as glorious as that?"

"Oh, surely it will, dear," she said, stroking his fast graying hair and patting his cheek confidently. "You know it's a long road that has no turning."

"Yes, but how long before we come to that blessed corner?" he answered wistfully. "You know, Allie, I don't think we are treating the boys right by hanging all this trouble on their young shoulders. They ought to be in school instead of working so hard to pay off the debts of their parents. They are so willing and anxious to see us through, but someday, in later years, they are going to blame us for it, and they have a right to."

"I know it, Johnie, and I have lost a good many nights' sleep over this very thing, but let's not spoil the sunset by worrying now. Some way that sunset brings memories of days gone by. Oh, yes, I remember!" Then she sat quietly thinking. It was the same sunset that she had raved over nearly thirty years ago. The one she and Frances had witnessed as they rode their steeds home from old Glen Oak that evening after pledging their love for each other. What a mess she had made of life. After all, would it have been better had she never been a real mother? What had she to offer these three fine fellows that call her mother? No past, no present, and no future. She loved each one better than her life, yet she seemed so helpless to do the things she would like to do for them.

The sun had sunk behind the horizon now, and Johnie, noticing the tears that just would come in her eyes, said, "Come in now, dearie, it's getting chilly out." Together they entered the house, where the boys were "washing up" for supper. They were a happy bunch. Laughing, teasing, and singing. From all appearances they were as happy as kings. "I guess it's all right, I'm a mother," Allie said to herself.

At the table they related the day's happenings as enthusiastically as if they had been the main actor in a football game. Yes, theirs was a simple life but a true one. They were all smart and quick to learn from life's school. All unknowingly they were storing up rare instruments for future use that could not be purchased on the market. Each was talented with some rare gift of nature. The sweet voice which this mother had lost was recovered by each of her sons. She watched their progress year after year and had prayed for the time to come when her boys should have a chance to cultivate their gifts.

Robert was twenty years old now, and how he worshipped Lindbergh. The mails were full of catalogs from every aviation school he could locate through advertisements. Some of the money he earned by helping neighbors with their work went for subscriptions to the Aerial Digest and other periodicals on the subject.

Richard, at seventeen, vowed his allegiance to the farm. He loved the stock, the fields, the whole outdoors. Baseball, basketball, and volleyball were his favorite sports. The Bradford home was always the meeting place for the young fellows of the neighborhood. Johnie and Allie encouraged the boys in having young company at their home, but not during working hours. They were taught to "Work while you work, Play while you play. That is the way to be—Cheerful and Gay."

Arthur was fifteen now and as changeable in his ideas as he was in his moods. He early declared his dislike for the opposite sex and made his "get away" as soon as he sensed the approach of such. He was in his first year high school now and doing nicely. It was an effort to raise the five dollars per week board this winter, as a good many of the villagers that bought milk from the Bradfords were out of work and could not pay for it. They kept a tin box on the sideboard in which they put the money for this purpose. Sometimes they would lack some and would have to take a chicken or two to town to make up the lacking board money. All this they kept from Arthur, knowing it would worry him.

The fall and winter passed. Spring stood upon the threshold begging admittance.
