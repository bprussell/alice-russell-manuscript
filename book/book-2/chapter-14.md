# Chapter XIV

It was a busy springtime both inside and out at the Bradford home. Everyone in the family set about cleaning up the house and yards. Wagonload after wagonload of trash was hauled away. The landlord consented to paying for the window glass for the four big front rooms upstairs and down. He also had the plaster patched on the walls and ceiling where it was needed. The old rickety window shutters were removed entirely. Every room was scrubbed and washed with scalding water and soap. Lye was sometimes necessary. It was thoroughly aired until no one to see it now would have suspected it had been the haven of rest and birthplace of the feathered tribe for years.

Allie enjoyed every minute of the work. She liked to see the grain of the beautiful woodwork appear from beneath the years' accumulation of dust and dirt as she plied her washcloth back and forth again and again over a small surface of window casing or door panel. She was thankful this house had been well built and of finest material. Allie appreciated this fact.

It took nerve to attack a job of this kind. Only one with the gift of foresight to see ahead the reward of their labors would have attempted it.

Johnie bought paint for the woodwork and floors where it was needed and with clean though inexpensive paper put on the walls they were ready to begin living again. They chose shades and tints of yellow (the color of sunshine and cheer) for the hangings.

New window shades of sand color overdraped with plain homemade deep cream colored marquisette curtains hung straight and full at each shining window. Next came the floor coverings. Linoleum rugs answered the question for two of the bedrooms. The other two floors were painted entirely with three coats of paint and varnish so two or three homemade rag rugs of harmonizing color answered the purpose there.

Allie found she could utilize many of the things she had had no use for since leaving their Peoria home. The large twelve by fifteen living room rug fitted nicely into the color scheme of the large airy old parlor as did the drapes, cushions, and table scarfs.

"I'm enjoying fixing up this old house more than I did our brand new one in Peoria," she told Johnie as she hung the rich well chosen pictures on the living room walls. Most of these had been given them when they were married. Johnie had found some wonderful old picture frames in the attic of this old house that had probably been the property of the third or fourth generation back. He recognized their value at once. Taking them apart he cleaned the glass and wooden parts, polished them till they shone and placed in them some copies of beautiful old paintings for which he had a weakness.

These quaint frames and pictures called forth the exclamation and admiration of their friends. Allie had never used the many beautiful articles left to her by Aunt Kate. Now she brought them forth and together they worked day after day placing a bit of pottery here, a blooming houseplant there, attractive wall pockets on the dining room, and bedroom walls, pretty cushions where they were most attractive, beautifully bound books (a hobby of Aunt Kate's) where their richness was most conspicuous, pretty vases and bronzes, a wonderful old brass candelabra, with some rare china and crystal pieces scattered here and there, made these old rooms fairly gleam with splendor.

The Wedgwood vase given "Parepa" on her sixteenth birthday by Daddy Wheeler was brought from its long resting place where it had lain wrapped round and round with one of Aunt Kate's velvet dresses ever since her death. Inside the vase were packed the opera glasses and the bracelet. In the same box the morocco-bound set of Dickens and the beautifully framed etching. All these presents were given her on her sixteenth birthday. Now she was forty-three years old. Could it be possible twenty-seven years had passed since that happy birthday party? She glanced into a mirror. Yes, that reflection answered her question. It was that long ago.

In the bottom of that box were the diaries of those four years which Uncle George had given her each New Year. Yes, and a chest was there, not a large cedar chest, not a chest of silver, but a chest of letters. The key dangled by a chain from the lock. Would she open it and read just a few of them? She held it in her hands awhile then caressing it put it with the bracelet and the diaries back in the box. She could not resist opening the leather case which held the beautifully jeweled pearl opera glasses before replacing it.

A card fell on her lap. She read it: Dear Parepa-Roza: "With best wishes for your future happiness and a fond memory of an evening with 'Uncle Tom' and you."—Frances.

She raised the glasses to her eyes and in memory saw the picture of a young girl so happy and free, dressed in a fluffy pink dress and a large leghorn hat with flowers around the crown and hanging from the brim over her shoulder. The picture changed! Now the same face was tear stained, the pretty pink dress wet and mussed. She was laying in kind old Uncle George's arms riding home from the show disgraced.

She replaced the glasses and suddenly she clasped the lid shut and laid it in the box beside the other gifts, as a child would have done had he been caught prying into places he had no business to be. "I will never peek at them again," she told herself. All these years I have refrained from opening that box. I must never do it again.

She took the Wedgwood vase, the picture, and the Dickens works downstairs and proudly displayed them and their cards to her boys and husband. The card still tied to the handle of the vase was inspected by her husband: "To My Dear Little Girl. A Rose for every year. To wish you joy, my Dear—Daddy Wheeler."

"And who in the world is Daddy Wheeler?" he asked. "I never heard of him before?"

Allie told them about her birthday party made up mostly of Aunt Kate's and Uncle George's friends, she said, and they were satisfied.

Other boxes of Aunt Kate's treasures were opened and put into use. "The boys are old enough now to be careful of them," she said. Her bedrooms gave splendid opportunity for the expression of her artistic nature. She had great fun redecorating old dressers, chest of drawers and bedsteads to fit in the color scheme of each room. The results were marvelous. For the first time in her married life she had the opportunity to give full rein to her gift for color and design. She kept the financial problem always in mind. Johnie and the boys expressed their delight at each completed task which greatly pleased this wife and mother of the pent up talents.

Her kitchen was made as pretty as was possible. There being no modern mechanical conveniences limited many helpful possibilities throughout the house. Nevertheless it was clean and practical and "pretty" which to our old Parepa-Roza meant so much.

The boys were old enough now to bring young company to their home. They were proud of it. Their parents encouraged young company. They all enjoyed the sports of the day, too.

Allie treasured the companionship of her many friends in the church, Delphian Society, Woman's Club and Home Bureau. Johnie never feeling quite up to keeping in step with the men of his age in their many activities was in sympathy with all movements that made for a better community. His quiet genial nature made a friend for him of every acquaintance. Sickness, hard luck, and worry had taken their toll from both husband and wife. Their staunch sticktuitiveness and refusal to be beaten down were most astonishing. The successive comebacks after each serious illness were little short of miraculous.

"Life is so sweet even at its worst," Johnie would say, "I'm going to hang on to it as long as I can."

It was the middle of April now. The clicking of the oat seeder and the put put put of the tractor could be heard on every side. Young chickens were being hatched and demanded lots of care.
