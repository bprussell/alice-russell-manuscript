# Chapter XXV

"You are to wear it this afternoon. It is Miss Emmerson's wish," she said.

"All right," Parepa answered. "I'd do anything to please her. Is there any jewelry to be worn with it?" she asked, remembering the cheap beads (one of Uncle George's tea ticket premiums) she had worn that night.

"There is none here," she answered and the subject was dropped.

"It is one-forty-five now and you are due for luncheon at two. Just let me change your pumps," so saying she slipped the new nude pumps on Parepa's feet. The messenger was clearing the room of what boxes were there and the modistes were leaving.

"You must wear the hat to match, Mrs Bradford. She wants you to," and so saying pulled the soft filmy thing over her beautifully dressed white hair. Touching a little soft powder to her face she said, "There now, you are ready. May I go down with you? I would like to witness Miss Emmerson's pleasure," she pleaded.

"Of course you can," and taking her maid's arm they descended the stair. Before they reached the second step Parepa noticed the forms of two men standing at the foot looking up. One was Daddy Wheeler, the other—Could it be possible—was Frances! Dressed and looking like his old self. His eyes shining, his cheeks flushed.

"Frances! Can it be you? Can it be you are able to do this—this joke?" she said, wondering what her maid would think, but Nanettezen had turned and was going to her room.

Frances extended his arms and Parepa snuggled into them. He kissed her lips again and again.

"Just for today, Parepa—just for today," and led her into the dining room. Daddy Wheeler left them alone. The servants seemed startled at the picture they made. "We are only rehearsing an old play of our childhood," Frances told the butler. "Just place the first course and leave us until I ring."

They were alone now. Each had so much they wanted to say yet neither could express themselves. They caught each other's eye and sat spell bound.

Frances gasped—"You are beautiful, Parepa, as beautiful as ever you were."

"If I hadn't lost my voice, Frances. You know I have never sang since we parted. But you are beautiful too, my old lover. Time has been kind to you. You are just as clever too. How did you dare to do this? Your physician; does he know?" she asked.

"Yes, she knows. You are my physician. You are making me well," and he reached across the table for her hand. She had not noticed the little jewel box he held. "There," he said, "are the jewels I bought for you in France and expected to give them to you on our wedding day. Then—when I looked into your Sunday School room and saw and heard a real natural love laid at your feet I knew I wasn't right. It mustn't be. Joseph Donald truly loved you, Parepa," he added. "I was hiding beside Aunt Kate's porch the night he gave you up to me. It was brave characters like his and yours that made me strong to live my life as I was born to live it. I'm not complaining. I am happy. Oh, so happy. And so are you, I know you are."

"Yes, Frances, I am happy. Knowing you has never lessened my happiness. I think it has helped me a great deal. You were real clever about this surprise. How did you happen to plan it?" she asked.

"Doesn't it remind you of anything, Parepa?" he said plaintively.

"Indeed it does. The night at the Fey Hotel in old Peoria and then the theatre. Could I ever forget Uncle Tom? Ha! Ha!" They both laughed heartily. "I hope you won't cry all over your pretty pink gown," he teased.

"Poor Uncle George! How he tried to comfort me," she laughed.

"Yes, and how I wished I was in his place going home; holding you in his arms. And do you remember the night on the river, and old Glen Oak park? And—you, and the sunsets? I live them over and over. Parepa, dearest, it's been the very life of me." He rang for the next course.

As they sat waiting, Parepa opened the jewel box. There was a necklace, bracelet, broach, and two rings all of platinum set with diamonds. They were of unusual design, and when Parepa raved over their delicate filigree, Frances said, "Do you recognize any one's name there?"

Parepa studied the pattern for some time. "Yes," she cried, "Here is my name woven into the necklace and in the bracelet too."

"Yes, and into both rings and the broach," he added. Together they deciphered them.

"Now can you find my name any place?" he said, as if they were working over a puzzle.

"Sure enough! Just as plain as day!" she said. "After one gets use to it, it's woven right into mine," she said excitedly.

"That was the way I thought my life would be too when I drew that design," he said.

"You drew it! Why Frances you are an artist. They are simply wonderful!" and she held them up in her hand. "Just see them sparkle and laugh."

"Yes, they are symbols of happiness. You will wear them won't you Parepa?" his voice was pleading.

"Yes, Frances, I will wear them today for old times' sake."

He rose to his feet and coming to her side said, "Please, Parepa, let me put them on you just as I had planned to do—then?"

She let him have his way. He put the ring having two diamonds cuddled closely together on her finger first. When she offered her left hand for this he said, "No that is your heart hand. That belongs to your husband alone. It is your blood hand. My rings cannot grace those fingers now. I will put them on your friendship finger." As she slipped the guard ring, encircled with small diamonds on next to the first, he said, "This one represents the care and protection I intended to give you." He slipped the bracelet over her hand saying, "To protect your pulse," and laughed.

Next, he took the broach from its cozy nest and pinned it in place. Lifting the sparkling necklace he said, "I like this the best of all. Do you know why?" and fastening it about her neck he stopped and kissed her.

"I haven't the least idea," she confessed.

Taking his place at the table again he said, "Because there was room for more of our good times together on it. Each diamond represents a memory of things that made us dear to each other." "Here," he said, drawing a small beautifully bound booklet from his pocket, "is the history of our gems. I give it to you. It has been a great comfort to me these years. Now it is yours."

"Oh, Frances! You have had all these things most thirty years! How wonderful! I shall be so glad to have them for the sentiment they hold. Thanks a hundred times." They rose to leave the table. "There is one more thing," he said and placing his hand on his breast, "It's the locket. Would you care for it?"

"Of course I do if you want me to have it, but I wonder if you shouldn't wear it. You are the last link in your chain. Why not keep it with you? I think that would be better," she said convincingly.

"Yes, it would. I'll do that," he said as matter of fact as if he were deciding which apple he would eat. They were in the library now. Daddy Wheeler was waiting for them.

"Well, my children, did you get enough to eat?" he laughed. Looking at Frances he asked, "How did you stand the exertion?"

"Fine! I have felt fine and had a most wonderful time." A nurse entered and laughed as she asked Parepa if they went through their old play all right.

"Yes, without a mistake," she answered. "Frances had a wonderful memory."

"I'm sorry to have to interrupt now, but don't you think it best for you to change your theatrical garb and take your rest? You know you haven't taken a dozen steps a day for six weeks. If you don't abuse yourself maybe Doctor will let you try it again soon."

As Frances hesitated she said, "Come now, I know Mrs Bradford will excuse you."

"Oh, certainly I will. It's been wonderful to have been together so long." She rose to go to her room. Stooping to kiss Frances she said, "Goodbye until tomorrow. Get a good rest and be ready for a new day. Thanks for our good time. Can I take my hat off now?" she laughed.

As the nurse helped Frances to his room Parepa went across to Daddy's side. "How are you feeling this afternoon?" she asked, taking his hand in hers.

"Fine!" he said. "I always feel fine if Frances is happy. I'm sure she was. I was afraid it would be too much for her, but she was bound to do it."

"She seemed perfectly at ease. I couldn't notice the least thing wrong," and Parepa turned to go. "I'll see you in the morning Daddy. I have some writing I must do and then I think I'll rest until tomorrow if you have nothing for me to do."

"No, just do as you like. I expect you are tired," and he waved a good bye to her. When she reached her room she felt relieved not to find her maid there. She wanted time to remove her jewelry first. She placed one after another in the case, a queer feeling came over her as she counted the sparkling diamonds in the necklace. "Our good times," she said and closed the lid. This fifty year old Parepa was as happy as any sixteen year old girl ever dared to be. She was sure her voice had come back to her while Frances was putting the jewelry on her. Nanettezen came in. After she had changed Parepa's attire to one of new comfortable lounging robes she asked for the evening off.

"Certainly you can," and Parepa was glad again that she would be left alone. In the deep silence of her room her thought fell to memories of the past. She wondered if it was right for her to guard them so sacredly. She was sure she did not love Frances in the same way she did her husband. That kind of love died long before her love for Johnie was born. What was it then, that annoyed her?

She sat at her table and wrote a long loving letter to her husband and children. She longed to be with them. They needed her now when financial troubles were assailing them. Then it dawned on her that she was rich, that she could send them a check that would relieve all their worries of that kind. Taking a Phoenix bank check book from the drawer she deliberately wrote five checks for one thousand each. She told them to take each one to a different bank in Peoria to have them cashed. That much would pay up their most annoying debts.

When she finished her letter she slipped into one of her beautiful new silk bed suits and went to bed. So tired was she in mind that slumber came immediately.

The sun was streaming in at the windows of her airy chamber when she awoke the next morning. Nanettezen gave her a shower and massage. She felt wonderful. Her breakfast was irresistible. She was ashamed to send back empty dishes every morning but she seemed ravenous. A tap at the door. It was one of the nurses.

"Could you come to Miss Emmerson's room Mrs Bradford?" she said. "She wishes to see you."

Parepa drew her dressing gown about her and descended the stairs with the nurse. "Is she not so well?" Parepa asked.

"A slight attack," the nurse answered and opened the door into Frances's room. From her bed she smiled at Parepa as she entered. The nurse slipped from the room and left them alone. Parepa stooped to kiss the sweet face, "Not so good today?" she asked.

"Not quite. Can't get up today they tell me. It's all right though. I had one great day yesterday. Can stand it to lay in bed a day as punishment," she laughed.

"You surely look fine. Hope you had a good rest."

"Oh, I did Frances. I didn't waken once. This climate seems to agree with me," she said.

"I'm glad to hear you say that. I hoped it would," she answered.

Daddy Wheeler came into the room smiling, "So you two old pals are together so early this morning, eh?" he joked. "How you feeling by this time, Frances?"

"Good! Only a little weak. How's yourself?" she said sweetly.

"Fine!" and the old man stooped and kissed Parepa on the cheek. "What are we going to do when she goes home?" he said. "We will surely miss you. Won't we Frances?"

"And I'll miss you both. I wish you knew my husband and boys. They are wonderful fellows," she said.

"I thought of them in the night and how good it was of them to let you come to me. I know it must have been a great sacrifice for them to give you up for so long. Be sure and let them know I appreciate it. Now I'll tell you why I called you. I forgot to tell you that you are to have everything on this place and in the house. You can do as you please with them only that I want your boys to have all my masculine possessions. My saddle, my watches, rings, guns, cameras, sport goods, and everything I have that I kept as keep sakes when I came to America five years ago. I have them packed just as they were in England for shipping. There are eighteen large boxes of them. I shall order them taken to the express station soon. I hope they will get a lot of enjoyment out of them," and she smiled as Parepa assured her that they would.

"Each one is labeled so they will know their value," she said. "I want you to call the housekeeper, the yardman and the butler, please Daddy. I want to introduce them to their new mistress."

When Daddy left the room Frances turned to Parepa and said, "I'll make it plain to them that you are the new owner of everything I possessed. They are all good servants and I have arranged an annual income for each of them as long as they give satisfactory service. It does not bind you to keep them, but I'm sure they will want to stay. It's better than to change to strangers. You will need more help when you are here but you could bring some of your own along. These three can keep up the place during your absence. They understand the hiring and managing of help. I trust them to do all of it.

"I suggest you move the more expensive articles to your Illinois home. I have labeled most everything of importance so you will know their origin and value." She stopped as if out of breath.

"Dear, dear Frances. How can you plan all these things so deliberately? It makes me feel badly to have you do it. It's you I want more than anything in the world, and I'm afraid you are exerting yourself too much for your own good."

"Don't think I'm ungrateful dear, but you must rest your mind from all these worries," pleaded Parepa.

"Worries? Why Parepa this is the greatest pleasure I have. Please let me indulge in it. I can see your home in my mind's eye, all surrounded by roses and trees and beauty."

Daddy Wheeler brought Mrs Johnson, the housekeeper, in first.

"Mrs Johnson I want you to meet your new mistress—Mrs Bradford. I know you will love her and she you," said Frances.

Mrs Johnson bowed and smiled.

"I'm sure I shall," said Parepa as she rose to take her hand.

"I have told her of the arrangements you and I have made for your staying here as long as she needs you," said Frances. "And Mrs Johnson please remember this. I have given to Mrs Bradford everything I own on this place and in the house. It's hers to do as she likes with. She has been my very best friend for thirty years and I want you to remember that I give her everything."

"I surely will Miss Emmerson. I'm glad she will be the mistress of 'Kashema.' I'll do everything I can to please her. I hope you will spend a great deal of time here, Mrs Bradford," she said turning to Parepa.

"Thank you. I think I shall," she answered.

The butler entered and Parepa was surprised to hear him introduced as Mr. Johnson. So they were man and wife. No one would have suspected, so smoothly did affairs operate in this household.

The yard man came in. He was apparently twenty-four years of age.

"This is Jimmy, Parepa," said Daddy. "Jimmie Johnson."

"So glad to meet you, Jimmie," and Parepa crossed the room, hand extended. "Are you a son of these two?" she said indicating the housekeeper and butler.

"Yes, Mrs Bradford, they are my parents," and his very voice was filled with pride.

"I'm so glad you are all of a family. It will be much easier. I see now why this home is conducted so smoothly. Efficiency is written all over you. I assure you all I'm very happy that Miss Emmerson has been so thoughtful as to have arranged for you to stay permanently," said Parepa.

"Now, we better look over the agreement together," suggested Daddy Wheeler. "So there can be no misunderstanding." He opened a drawer and drew out the papers. The original of each for Parepa and a duplicate copy for each of the three. After handing each their copy Daddy read from Parepa's. It was a simple form of arrangement yet it seemed complete in every detail. Parepa wondered at the matter-of-fact way they discussed affairs pertaining to Frances's departure from this life.

"Now, I want this to be plainly understood by all here. Parepa, I mean Mrs Bradford, here, is right now the owner and full possessor of this estate and everything, omitting nothing, that I have owned. I have given it all to her. Is that plain to you all?" asked Frances.

"I suppose you mean all household goods, too," said Jimmy.

"Yes, everything. I have them all catalogued in a book which I will give to her today. She can do just as she pleases with everything. You three will be responsible for their care the same as you have been these past five years, according to the agreements," Frances answered. "You may go now," and she sank back in her pillows as if weary.

"You are so good Frances. Heaven is right on earth where you are," and Parepa rose and walked to her bedside, taking her hand in hers she said, "I must let you rest now, darling. I want you to feel good this afternoon. I have a surprise for you." Kissing her she said, "Now don't ask me to stay longer. I know you are tired. I'll see you at three this afternoon; until then, good bye," and she turned and blew a kiss to her from her hand.

After Parepa read the letter from home, she was sitting at her writing desk ready to answer it when she heard the knocker on the door.

Daddy Wheeler entered carrying the catalogue of articles in the household. "Tomorrow we will start going over the house and viewing each thing so you will remember them. There are pictures of most of them," he said. "Here are the servants' agreements," he said laying them on the table. "Frances had a serious time last night," he added.

"Did she suffer terribly?" asked Parepa.

"No, she never suffers. Just collapses and it takes strenuous treatment by the nurses to bring her around again. We never know what a day will bring forth," he added thoughtfully. "Did I ever tell you her wishes about her burial, Parepa? You know Parepa, Frances has a true religion all her own. Not one influenced by a creed or order. It is such a live thing to her that all else is dominated by it. It is the back ground that serves and supports her in all times of test or trial. Her love for the Supreme Being, her Father, her Helper, is a most natural thing for her. He understands her frail creation. In silence she goes to him and unburdens her longings and becomes refreshed.

"Her 'home made' religion is clothed with love. It is her property, and was created out of beauty, kindliness, and regard for the rights of others. I have always noticed that her own burdens disappear as she lightens the burdens of others."

"No Daddy, you didn't tell me. Has she planned that too?" she asked.

"Yes indeed. They are very simple. She wants to be taken to a little church yard in the country about fifty miles from here, and wants no service of any kind. She says she and her God understand each other and that is all that is necessary. I have promised to carry out her wishes to the letter. She wants no procession—only her servants and I to follow her. There must be no publicity. These three Johnsons and I were all that knew her wishes. She wants to be buried at night and hopes it will be moonlight. I suppose that sounds unchristian to you, but I have a great deal of respect for her wishes. She has a reason for them all. A better life was never lived. She has been a great blessing to humanity. Very few know from whose hand they benefited. 'The Lord knows,' she would say, 'and that is all that matters.' I gave up my whole life to her. It has been worth it. I shall never regret it." He spoke calmly as though talking of the weather.

"You are both wonderful. I love you as a father, Daddy, and I'm sure no one but our God knows how to diagnosis Frances's and my love for each other."

Her maid came in from her morning walk and Daddy Wheeler left the room. Parepa lounged lazily on her couch. Her heart and mind had grown ripe and were mellowed by the turns of fate.

Finally sleep came to her closed eyes. In her dreams she and Frances were in the boat on the Illinois River in Peoria. They were singing love songs. When the maid roused her to dress for luncheon she was laying in Frances's arms while the boat drifted where it may.

"Oh!" she said sleepily. "My voice! I have it again! It has come back to me!"

Her maid thought she was still dreaming but Parepa knew better. She could sing.

"Please wear the pretty black today," begged Nanettezen as she finished dressing her hair. "You were so lovely in it."

"It's an evening gown but I'll wear it anyway," Parepa agreed.

"There now, look in the glass." Parepa did look. "Yes, it was becoming," she must admit. As usual Daddy was waiting for her at the foot of the stairs.

"What a beautiful woman you grew to be, Parepa. Only a good woman could be so truly charming. Refinement, truth, love, courage, faith, yes, and charity all shine from your countenance," he said as they walked together to the dining room.

"I try to be all of that Daddy dear, but the charity has all come my way. I'm going to tell you something to laugh about Daddy," she said as she was seated at the table. "Do you know that we didn't have the money for my trip out here and I borrowed it from the Sunday School collection box? My boy is Treasurer. I worried all the way here about how I'd pay it back. I'll double it now," she laughed.

"Yes, and I'll double it too," he said. They lingered long at the table. Daddy was his jolly self and being much traveled and very well educated he interested Parepa greatly.

"Let's go see that cactus I've been telling you about," he said so they left the table. They strolled some way before they came to it. It was far above Parepa's head. She laughed as she thought of her petted house plant of the same variety. She was feeling fine when they got back and bound to carry out her intention of surprising Frances.

When she heard the roll of his couch coming into the library she took her seat at the piano and began singing, "When Love Comes in the Moonlight." Her voice surprised herself. It was as clear in tone as it had been when a child. When she had finished Frances begged her to sing again and again. Often she joined in a few weak strains.

"Oh, that was grand of you, Parepa. Why hadn't we thought of that before?" asked Frances.

"You forgot that I haven't sang for nearly thirty years, Frances," she said. "My voice came back to me yesterday when you were fastening the necklace around my neck. From now on we are going to forget about me and think altogether about your good time. Aren't we now?" she begged.
