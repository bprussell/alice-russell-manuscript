# Chapter XX

> Tucson Arizona  
> Jan. 30, 1932
>
> Editor, Peoria Tribune  
> Peoria, Illinois
>
> I am endeavoring to ascertain the present whereabouts of Alice Parepa-Roza Wesley, who resided with relatives in your City, in the vicinity of the Bethany Baptist Church during the years 1898 to 1903. At that time she studied dramatic Art with Mrs Rose Mendenhall Breen and Piano and voice with a Mrs Slay.
>
> Though only a young girl she earned her way and paid for her lessons by working in a candy store on Main Street opposite the Courthouse.
>
> I learned many years ago that her relatives died and she became a schoolteacher in the West.
>
> I have tried repeatedly the last five years to locate her by radio, correspondence and the press. I remembered the names of Arthur Youngblood and Joseph Donald, friends of hers. I wrote them hoping to locate her but the letters were returned. No doubt she married. I never did.
>
> It is very much to her interest that she be found. As she will remember I have no living relatives. I have been very successful financially. My health has failed me. I have heard my physician's warning. It can't be long.
>
> I assure you any effort you may put forth to locate her will be greatly appreciated.
>
> Thanking you.
>
> I am truly yours,  
> F.L. Emmerson
>
> (By J.C. Wheeler)

Allie handed the paper to her husband and sat down in a chair by the table.

"Do you know who this man is, Mrs Bradford?" the reporter asked.

"I can think of no man who would be so interested in me and my family," she answered. "I must ask you to refrain from questioning me further. I do not care to discuss this thing with anyone but my family at present. I thank you for your interest in me, and if you will leave your name I will gladly give you the story when there is anything to tell. Believe me sincere when I tell you I have answered your questions honestly," she rose as if to show him out.

That he was disappointed was plain to be seen. She added, relenting, "If you want to know about my family and myself I shall be glad to answer your questions." Before he went he had quite a lengthy story, and seemed satisfied.

After he had gone a knock on the door brought Allie face to face with her old coworker of candy store days. She recognized him at once and welcomed him heartily. After being introduced to her family he said, "Have you seen the paper?" and pulled one from his pocket.

"Yes, one of their reporters was just here," said Johnie hoping to quiet him on the subject.

"When I read it I figured it would be good news for you and I hoped I'd be the first to tell you," he said.

"That's mighty good of you, but I've done nothing all evening but answer 'phone calls and entertain callers. Will you excuse me if I don't talk about it anymore? You know I appreciate your interest, don't you?" and she dropped wearily in her chair.

"Oh, sure and I know you must be tired out. I'll be going right back. I only wanted you to get in touch with this fellow before he passes on. You know folks," turning to Johnie and the boys, "she was a mighty pretty little girl when I knew her."

"I think she is yet," and Johnie put his arm around her lovingly.

"I can see she made no mistake in choosing her life mate," he said as he turned to leave. "Good night and the best of luck."

"Thanks and Good night," they said and closed the door.

"Now, Mother, who in the world do you suppose this is that is looking for you and do you suppose we will get lots of money?" asked the restless young Arthur.

"Now, all of you sit down and be still and let me do the talking without being interrupted," she commanded and they obliged. "Now I want you all to promise me that you will not talk about this thing to anyone."

"What if they ask us?" inquired Richard, "then we'll have to talk or else be rude."

"Well, that being the case the only thing I know to do is to not tell you anything. Then you can't tell what you don't know," she answered.

"Oh, yes, Mom, come on now and tell us who you think it is," coaxed Robert.

Johnie said nothing.

"Now let's all go to bed and wait until we hear from this strange person. Maybe he wants to give us a five dollar bill, who knows? Wouldn't that be kind of him? Anyway we'll just all wait and see, won't we?"

They were an excited bunch of boys as they tripped up the stairs to bed. Voices could be heard calling back and forth across the hall to each other for an hour. After the lights was out and they were in bed Allie asked, "Do you think it's right for me to write to this Mr. Wheeler, Johnie?"

"That depends upon the kind of people they are. Does this man Wheeler happen to be the one that gave you that Wedgwood vase for your birthday when you lived with Aunt Kate?" he asked.

"Then he remembered," she thought. "Yes, the same old Daddy Wheeler. Let's see—he must be eighty-two or three years old by this time." Then patting his cheek tenderly she said, "He was Frances's guardian and Secretary then too. I'll tell you all I know if you will promise never to tell the boys or anyone else that I told you." After a long silence she said, "Do you promise?"

"Yes, but I can't understand all this secrecy," he said in a voice with a quality in it she had never heard before.

"Can he be jealous?" she thought. She began at the beginning. The first night she met Frances at Aunt Kate's supper table. She took him through the five years of their acquaintance. Of their love and devotion, then the fact that Frances could never be the father to her children, and the parting never to meet again. She had never told this before. Then kissing her husband as she lay in his arms she could feel his muscles which had been taut relax.

"You, my dear husband, was my reward for doing the right thing. You and our fine boys, I ask no other. All the money I might get from this source would never change me. You believe me, don't you dear?" and she squeezed his hand.

"Darling you know I believe you. I am wondering how you have ever stood it to go through life in the humdrum way we have when you could have had everything one could ask for in this world. Oh, wifie, I haven't been worth it. I didn't know. Forgive me, can you?"

"Not everything," she said. "Everything but children," she whispered. "I am happy, You know that. No one could have made me happier even in our most discouraging times. I imagine our financial worries are over now. Frances was many times a millionaire when I knew him. You and I are going to enjoy life from now on. You are going to get well and strong and I'm going to have 'pretty things.' All I want—no limit. Ha! Ha! Our boys are going to have the education they are entitled to and 'whoopee,'" she cried tickling him until he most rolled out of bed. "Ain't we got fun?"

"Now we can give and give and give. We'll help others that are having the same hard time that we are. Oh, won't it be great, Johnie? Just think! After all these long years Frances had remembered me."

"Yes, dearest it is great, but no one that ever knew you could forget you," he answered.

"No time for taffy now, my dear. The thing for us to do is to figure out a way to quiet this thing. I don't like this publicity—yet—."

"Why don't you leave at once for Arizona?" asked Johnie. "I'll take you to the train in Peoria and you will be there in a few days. No one needs to know you have gone." He turned his flashlight on the alarm clock beside the bed and found it was one-thirty.

"I don't want to go without you," she said.

"You must though," he said, "one of us must stay with the children."

"Do you suppose I could catch that three-forty-five out of Chillicothe?" she said. "It will only give me an hour and a half to get ready."

"Of course you can. I'll get right up and call the boys and you'll be off before you know it," and he was out of bed with a leap. She began gathering her clothes together then asked, "But Johnie, we haven't the money. It will take a lot for the ticket you know," and she sank down on the bed. Tears began to flow down her cheeks.

Robert had heard them talking and came down to see what it meant. "Your mother was going to leave for Arizona on that three-forty-five from Chillicothe but we haven't the money to buy her ticket," Johnie explained.

"How do you know this man will give you that much money after you get there?" inquired Robert.

"Robert dear, can Mother trust you to keep a secret?" and Allie looked into the honest face of her oldest son.

"Yes, Mother, I know you wouldn't want me to keep it if it wasn't the best thing to do," he said.

"You must not even tell the brothers. This person that is looking for me is many times a millionaire. There is no doubt about that," she said.

"I've just thought of a way you can go, Mother," he said. "I have about sixty-five dollars in the Sunday School treasury box upstairs. I'll loan it to you and I have thirty-six of my own you can have. If you get lots of money out there you can pay a big interest for the use of it."

"Oh, that will be fine," and she sprang out of bed and dressed for the train trip, tossing articles of wearing apparel in the open suitcase as she rushed from one place to another, while Robert and Johnie shook the banks empty and all loose change in the house was gathered together for the trip. Johnie got the other boys out of bed and they were made to understand that Mother felt she should go to see this old friend who was so sick. These boys were not given the impression that there was any money interest in the trip. They promised not to tell anyone that their Mother had gone.

"We'll be back home before the neighbors are out of bed this morning," said Johnie, and Robert drove the old Ford at a lively rate of speed to the depot.

"I'm glad you have taught us how to help you cook, mother," said Richard.

"Yes, and how to do everything else in the house," added Arthur whose main activity was dishwashing.

"You must not worry for a minute about any of us while you are gone. We are well able to get along and we won't starve," offered Robert.

"How I wish you were all going with me," she said, kissing one and another.

"It can't be this time I know," she added, "but next time we will all go."

They had just ten minutes to buy the ticket and bid goodbyes.
