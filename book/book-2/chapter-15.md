# Chapter XV

Gardening time had come. This was the greatest pleasure Allie could ask for. She loved her garden. Every woman needs a hobby, especially in the middle years of her life after her big job of raising her family is done. Allie's hobby was her garden. In its changing blooms she created the expression of the beauty and tranquillity which life had so often perversely denied her. She craved it so hungrily.

This farmer's wife, who is primarily an artist, planted her very soul in the seed bed and the flowers that sprang from it represented her moods and manners for she used her garden, not alone as a comfort and pleasure, but as an exhaust or safety valve. When things indoors became boresome, when the boys were on a tear, running through the house throwing water on each other; when the cake fell, when the gasolene engine refused to run the washing machine then Allie wisely turned her back on it all and rushed to her garden. How she uprooted the weeds! In every place a root had been she dropped a bit of temper. After having buried her troubles she satisfies her better nature by transplanting and nursing the baby plants. Before she returns in the house she is feeling like a different woman. Her grouch is gone and she goes about making the best of things; really enjoying her work.

She had a definitely planned garden this year. It was planned with the idea of commercializing on it. She had the most of it to do with her own hands as the men folks were busy continually in the fields. They were all working hard from daylight until dark these days. The crops and garden looked good and their hopes were raised to the highest pitch.

Allie had conceived an idea by which she could market her flowers in Peoria. She sent letters to many offices of Doctors, Lawyers, Insurance Companies, Beauty Parlors, etc. offering to supply them with desk bouquets at regular intervals from May first to November first. She received many orders and was delighted. She named her garden "Wonderland," and her bouquets "Alice in Wonderland." Every bouquet built by her had attached to it a dainty little card reading "I am an Alice in Wonderland Bouquet. There's lots more of us at home." On the opposite side was printed her address.

The idea was wholly original with her as was everything she did. It fell to Robert or Richard's lot to take Allie and her flowers to Peoria early each morning. The customers were more than pleased with them and it wasn't long before the Wonderland gardens were well known. People came for flowers for their parties, weddings, funerals, etc. until very few blooms were wasted. Being located on a hard road made it easily reached by the buying public. A large sign on the corner called the attention of the passerby and was the means of many a sale.

Allie was bound her garden venture would succeed. Otherwise she could not have hurdled the obstacles that beset her efforts. Her garden was an everchanging picture. She got as much pleasure in hearing the exclamation of passersby as they raved over the beauty of her garden as she did from the most elaborate sale. "It helps to make the world brighter," she would say, "I'm glad I have a hand in it."

The boys had cleaned and raked the large lawn and after a few mowings with the lawn mower had as pretty a lawn as one could ask for. They were all very proud of their achievement. People would linger on the lawn and in the old mansion marveling at the wonderful old structure.

It usually fell to Johnie's lot to entertain the men folks while Allie waited on the lady customers. He enjoyed this companionship; this exchanging of ideas; these friendly talks. In this way he was brought in contact with men of all professions, politics and religions. Comfortable chairs, swings, and benches were placed in groups on the lawn and were enjoyed by the lingering visitors.

During the flower show season the Alice in Wonderland Bouquets were conspicuous by their many blue and red ribbons. It was not unusual for the sweepstake prize to be taken by them. The Dahlias were always winners wherever exhibited. Allie received many orders for the bulbs. At the end of the season when old Jack Frost had done his worst and her flowers lay flat and subdued under the destructive wave of his wand, Allie found that the returns from this small patch of ground far exceeded that of all the oats marketed from both farms. With a keen eye for business she planted thousands of tulips, narcissus and hyacinths for the next Spring's blooming. Also rows and rows of perennials for marketing. Only the best varieties were included in her collection. She studied long and earnestly over the huge pile of catalogs sent her.

Johnie and the boys had built a fine new hotbed from which she could sell annual flower and vegetable plants the next Spring. They also built a rose arbor in the yard and added a small latticed porch on the front of the house by which one could enter the large front hall through the massive double doors. These doors had not been opened only for ventilation for years. Entrance having been made through a side door. All these improvements were made at the expense of the Garden. Winter passed. March first came. The time of financial reckoning for all farmers.

Notes were due at the bank, grains were still lower at the elevators. At best the Bradfords must fall far behind in their obligations. It had been a hard year on all the family. Up every morning by day break work until dark every night, day after day, week after week, month after month. Not one cent had gone for cigars, drinks, unwholesome amusements or riotous living in any form.

What were they to do? They willingly turned over all the money they had raised to their creditors just as they had done for years but the bank saw fit to start foreclosure on their farm mortgage. After employing a lawyer to advise them it was thought best to sign the farm over to the bank. This left them without a farm of their own, but still plenty of debts. If they had been the kind of people who give up to discouragements they certainly would have "folded their tents" as the Arabs and as silently stole away.

Not so, this family. They rented another eighty acre farm near them making two hundred and forty acres in all and set about to try again. The garden brought early returns in cash. The list of contracts signed for office flowers more than doubled over the previous year. Early customers to the garden realized the superior quality of plants and perennials and purchased with a lavish hand.

Rock garden plants found a ready sale, too.

Johnie gave most of his time to cultivating and transplanting the flowers now.
