# Chapter XIII

They moved onto the farm and set about their new duties with a heavy heart but with a determination to make good if such a thing be possible, handicapped as they were.

The little boys were overjoyed. They got a great thrill out of caring for the pigs, calves, and chickens. Their many little helpful steps made the burden of chore time lighter for their parents.

It was such fun going to the timber for the cows. Their father bought a small pony for them, and what sport it was to play cowboy. Johnie went in for dairy products, while Allie raised poultry and flowers—dahlias being her specialty. Selling the bulbs in the spring through advertising. She found great pleasure in the study of plants. No time was wasted by any member of this family. Everyone was made to feel responsible for the performance of certain duties, and no shirking was countenanced, no matter how slight.

There was plenty of time for good healthy sport, and these three brothers succeeded in having their share of fun together. What one couldn't think of, another could. Thus, they grew into chubby healthy fellows. Their religious training was not neglected by any means. Every Sunday found the Bradford family in their pew at the Edelstein Congregational Church.

Four years passed. It had taken every ounce of effort and a lot of careful scheming to make the interest each year. It was discouraging times. Land had taken a desperate downward slide. Grain and produce prices did the same thing, but everything the farmer needed to buy was ridiculously high. Machinery reached the peak in price, and manufactured foodstuffs went the limit. All this, after years of long hard days' work, was most discouraging to these patient long suffering tillers of soil.

Robert had just completed his first year in the Princeville High School. In fact, the very day school closed, his father took desperately sick while riding a plow in the field. He was rushed at once to a doctor, who, realizing the seriousness of his condition, hurried him to the Peoria Methodist Hospital. It was decided best, after three weeks' observation, to take him to Mayo Brothers Clinic at Rochester, Minnesota.

He was there nine weeks. After a most serious experimental operation, his strength came back to him slowly. After his return home, it was necessary for his physician to care for the wound and prescribe for him daily. It was over a year before his services were considered unnecessary. The expense of this sickness ran well into the thousands. It plunged them into debt deeper than ever. There seemed no use to try to keep on. Yet could they give up?—No, not they.

These people knew no such word as fail, and so gritty was the whole family that they seemed to double their efforts. Robert, young as he was, became the head of farming operations, Richard was not far behind, keeping up a man's end of the work. Milking the cows, separating the milk, slopping and feeding the hogs, cleaning stables, and doing all kinds of helpful things about the place. Little as Arthur was, he was eager to do his bit, and he especially liked tending the garden and chickens. He was his mother's helper too and could perform any household duty as efficiently as any girl his age.

The boys loved their parents and the kind encouraging words of appreciation given them only served to make them more determined to be helpful.

Two years passed. It was impossible to raise the interest. Grain and produce prices had gone still lower, but foodstuffs, clothing, machinery, and necessities did not drop accordingly.

One day the owner of a grain farm nearby stopped to see if Johnie would rent his farm and move onto it. He thought he could farm both farms satisfactorily by hiring some help. Johnie and Allie went to their banker about the proposition. After talking it over for and against, they came to the conclusion that it was a good idea to try it. The banker agreeing to loan the money for the extra equipment necessary to operate the farm. March first found them moved into a fourteen room house built in 1863 with a large old barn one hundred and twenty feet long of equal date. A showplace between Chicago and Peoria in its day. The buildings had been sinfully neglected. There was no sign of really homey instinct having manifested itself on this farm for many years.

About four of the rooms had been occupied by the late renters. On account of many many missing windows throughout the house, the pigeons, owls, sparrows, and many other of the winged family made their homes in whichever apartment best suited their taste. After being kept awake by the billing and cooing of the pigeons, the boys sat about hunting birds' nests. Over one hundred pigeon eggs and many young birds were gathered from the attic.

The wind sent a weird and mournful wail throughout the upper story. The shutters which hung by one or more hinges banged incessantly throughout the night. The house was so cold it was impossible to warm even the large dining room with the one wood stove which they had brought with them. For days they wore their sweaters and overshoes to keep warm.

Arthur said, "Even the rats and mice have to run fast in this house to keep warm." He started trapping them the second day and proudly displayed the results of his labors to callers, much to the embarrassment of his mother. Most folks would have been discouraged on the start, but not so these people.

Allie, with her keen eye for beauty, realized the possibilities here. In the first place, the location was ideal—the view most wonderful. She could see the beauty in the grand old fireplaces, the massive woodwork, the many long windows most reaching the floor, the old fashioned stairway, the transomes over the doors, the large hallway, the spaciousness of the whole house. As she stopped before a window, she viewed the lawn (or could we call it a lawn) although it had not felt the cut of a lawn mower's sharp blades for years (if ever). It could be made beautiful in a year's time. Allie was sure of that. It was so large and had just the right slope. The trees were beautiful, great tall old maples flanking each side of the lawn, reaching out great arms as if to protect and shelter this pitiful old structure.

Oh! Now she finds her garden spot. A level space laying north of the lawn and extending to the roadside. Right now she could see it ablaze with color. Yes, she was happy! This was the dream garden of her childhood. She could see it all now—how kind of fate to hold it back until her riper years.

There was the place for the rock garden and there would be the lily pool, of course, there would be lots of birds. And what is that way down the hill west of the house? A pond—my lake! She almost cried, clapping her hands. She turned and was about to run to find Johnie and the boys in her glee. Then she stopped—they wouldn't see it—they wouldn't understand, she said to herself. They are all men.

For the first time in her life, she was overcome with lonesomeness. Lonesome for something—she didn't quite know what. They were all men—that was it. She was alone after all—after all these years—alone. Where were the twin girls of her dream days—*her* girls?

Frances had laughed when she told him about them. He had said he hoped there would be lots of boys. He had gotten his wish. Strange she had never remembered of Frances having said that before.

She walked to another window and looked out. Yes, she was alone. There, by the barn, stand her husband and his three fine sons, which she had given him, talking, planning, and cooperating together. She was alone. They are all men—they can't understand, and for a short moment tears sprang to her eyes. She quickly wiped them away. My garden! My flowers! she cried. They are my lovely daughters! They are my companions. I'll nurse them, I'll caress them, I'll raise them up into beautiful characters, I'll talk to them, I'll play with them, I'll train them, I'll teach them right from wrong. Oh, we will be the best companions! They shall be as good and grand as my wonderful sons. I shall be satisfied. I shall give to my world two rare gifts, honest upright men, beautiful, understanding flowers. My gifts—to my God. And she "Thanked God For a Garden!"
