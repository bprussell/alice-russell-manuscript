# Chapter XI

One afternoon about three o'clock Allie was surprised to see Johnie come swaying into the house. "I'm so sick," he said and throwing himself on the couch he cried, "Call the Doctor quick." Before the Doctor arrived Allie had helped him to his bed.

"It's the 'flu,' that wicked flu," said the Doctor handing Allie the thermometer to read. It stood at 105 degrees. He gave strict orders for his treatment and left with a grave face. "Call me at once if he shows any signs of being worse," he said as he left.

His head pained him until he could hardly endure it. Allie did just as the Doctor directed but at eight o'clock he became delirious. She called the Doctor. At that time the flu was sweeping the country from coast to coast. Doctors drove from place to place for days without sleep. They were rushed beyond their strength trying to give relief to the suffering.

When he came he had a nurse with him. "I was afraid of this," he said. "He's a very sick man. We'll do our best to save him."

Allie had put the boys to bed and had sent Mr. Brown and Rice to a restaurant for their dinner. She was almost overcome by the shock of the afternoon. Johnie was raving now. What would she do! The nurse worked faithfully but the whole night was a battle.

Days passed! Johnie would become conscious at times only to lapse into unconsciousness without the least warning. The young men very kindly offered to take their meals out until he was better.

On the seventh day Robert came down with the same thing. The following day Arthur took it and on the tenth day Richard was a victim. Poor Allie was distracted. Every bed had to be stripped of its linen every day and washed. Nothing was allowed sent to a laundry from a home where flu was prevalent. She finally secured the help of a woman who came each day and confined her labors to the basement where she washed, dried and ironed the many articles of clothing and bedding. The little boys were desperately sick. The Doctor and nurse spent anxious hours over each one in turn. Allie was continually waiting on one or the other. The young men didn't take it, neither did Allie.

It was weeks before Johnie was able to be up. Then he was so weak it wasn't thought best for him to go down stairs for another ten days. The little boys gained faster and were now ready to start to school. They went one whole week when Richard developed an awful cough. It was whooping cough. In due time the other boys took it. Weeks and weeks of strangling, coughing, and restlessness followed. Many sleepless nights passed as she watched over them.

It was spring now and the Doctor who had been keeping a close watch on Johnie's improvement declared him able to go back to his duties at the Avery Company. The boys were a hard proposition these days. They had been so sick and were weakened in strength and disposition from the aggravating cough.

Allie was tired out. "I'm well, but so tired," she would tell folks when they spoke of their alarm for her. One bright morning in May the boys were playing in the back yard when Allie thought Robert looked flushed in the face. He was crimson. She brought him in and although he declared he wasn't sick she called the Doctor.

"Nothing else but the measles," he said casting a sympathetic look at Allie. "Must go to bed, young man," he said and remembering what a laughing hilarious bunch of boys they were added, "And I'll dish you up some giggle pills."

One boy followed another until the three were in bed. Mr. Rice and Mr. Brown were unable to stay at the Bradford home during this siege so decided to return to their homes in Boston. This worried Allie greatly as she was concerned about the Doctor bills, etc. that had accumulated the last few months. She knew full well it would be some time before they would get boarders like that again.

After the sign was removed from the house, the fumigating done, and things back to normal Allie sat down to look the situation squarely in the face. She knew better than the Doctor or even Johnie himself that he had never regained his strength or spirit since his attack of the flu. Another thing she was sure of—her boys were growing up. Robert was ten now, and the City was no place for him. All three of these young rascals were entirely too rambunctious for their activities to be confined to a forty by one hundred and twenty foot city lot.

"What they need is acres instead of feet," she thought. That afternoon John came home about three-fifteen. Allie knew without being told that he was sick.

"Doctor thought I better come home and rest up the rest of the week," he said in an apologetic voice. "I'll be all right then, just tired, I guess."

Allie knew better but was too wise to say so. On Monday morning he was in his usual place feeling quite rested. The Avery Company's Doctor called him to his office to have a talk with him.

"I've been thinking a great deal about you, Mr. Bradford," he began, "and I've decided the best thing for you to do is to change climate for a while, try Colorado a few years." He watched his patient's face closely. For a moment he sat staring vacantly into space.

"I can't quite figure how I can make a change of that kind now," he said.

"Oh, sure you can," and the old Doctor began to make plans for him.

"It's the money," he said. "We have only a second mortgage of seven thousand five hundred dollars at interest, and a small equity in our home here. It would be impossible to cash in on them at their real value now."
