# Chapter X

Their farm was sold, and with the little money realized above the first mortgage and the second, which they took themselves, they made a first payment on a six room home in a new addition to Peoria called Hanssler Place.

It was great fun furnishing this new home. Allie studied long over draperies and floor coverings. She had to be so very careful not to overstep her limited funds allotted for this purpose. However, when the job was done, it proved the artistic nature of this homemaker and mother. Every room showed definite planning.

When the man came from the drapery department of Peoria's largest store to hang the living room drapes, he showed his surprise and pleasure at the wonderful color combinations throughout the house. "You are primarily an artist, Mrs. Bradford," he told her. "Do you mind my bringing our manager to look this over?"

She consented, and the next morning they arrived. The manager was genuinely enthusiastic over the "completed picture," as he called it. "It is unusual indeed to find such harmony throughout the whole house. Would you consider an offer from us, Mrs. Bradford? We are greatly in need of someone like you who understands color and its effect. I'm sure we could make you an interesting proposition."

Allie was greatly pleased but reminded him of her family, "I'd love doing it," she said, "but you know three lively boys keep me busy. It would be out of the question."

"Think it over," he said as they left the house. "We'll make it worth your while."

Robert was nine, Richard six, and Arthur four years old now. Johnie was fortunate in finding not too strenuous employment at once.

The Avery Manufacturing Company, where he worked, was located on North Adams Street, some blocks from their home, but Johnie preferred to walk to and from work, saying the exercise and fresh air did him good. No doubt they did, but there was another reason. The carfare he saved helped to make the monthly payments on their home. Before they knew it, they were overwhelmed with gas, electric, water, paving, sewer, and tax bills.

Allie began to wonder what she could do to help out. She thought about the offer made her by the furniture company. "All you would have to do," they had said, "is to make yourself thoroughly acquainted with our stock of curtains, draperies, floor, and wall coverings. Prepare yourself to go to the new homes in the city as we direct you, and make suggestions to the owners of color schemes for the interior decoration of the whole house. We will send a man along from the department to make an estimate of cost." For this service she was to receive six dollars a day.

There would probably be an average of three days a week. "If I could go from nine to eleven in the morning and from one to three in the afternoon, I might try it," she thought. "I couldn't be away when the children are home from school," she decided, so gave up the whole idea.

"I wonder if I could study up and give entertainments in the evening when Johnie is home with the children? No-o-o," she decided, "Johnie must have his rest."

"It will have to be something I can do at home," she pondered, then the thought came to her, why not rent out the spare bedroom. Anyway, she'd try an ad in the evening paper. She did. That evening she was kept busy answering the 'phone or doorbell. The room was a large one on the second floor with ample closet space and lots of windows. Of all the inquiries, Johnie and Allie liked the two young men from Boston, who were employed by the government in the after war reconstruction work at the Holt Manufacturing Company. These young fellows had been staying at one of the best hotels in the city and begged Allie to give them breakfast, pack a lunch, and serve them an evening dinner. For this they offered what seemed to Johnie and Allie a huge sum. Allie was afraid to tackle it. She thought her way of cooking was countrified and told them so.

They both declared their willingness to risk it. So it was settled, Monday morning found them "moved in." It was a lot of extra work for Allie, but she undertook it cheerfully. The young men, Mr. Rice and Mr. Brown, were more than pleased with the homey atmosphere of the place and bragged continually on the fine meals served them.

Johnie and Allie decided to make the thirty-five dollars per week paid by them keep the table for the seven of them. Allie run a bill at the corner grocery store and paid every Saturday night. Things were at the high peak at that time, and, try as she would, the thirty-five dollars would not reach. She always bought what she knew to be good value and such nourishing foods as she wanted her family to enjoy.

Butter was seventy-five cents a pound, sugar, if you could get it at all, was "out of sight." No good meat was less than fifty cents a pound, milk was sixteen cents a quart, flour was two dollars and fifteen cents for a twenty-four pound sack, eggs, potatoes, and fruits were accordingly high. It was not unusual to have to add an extra ten or fifteen to the thirty-five. Thus, costing an average of about seven and one half dollars per person per week.

They were all a healthy, happy family. Month after month passed.

One evening Johnie came home delighted. Kissing his wife, he announced the fact that he had been given a generous raise. Evidently his boss had recognized his sterling worth and steadfastness. Allie could hardly believe it could be so much. Shop work was so new to him, she had thought it would be a long time before he would be given a more remunerative position. His new duties were not so strenuous, but there was more responsibility connected with it.

Everything ran smoothly these days. Allie had joined the Athenian Delphian Society, was enjoying the social duties connected with the Arcadia Presbyterian Church and the Parent Teacher's Association of the Columbia School. She guided her children carefully through the confusing lower grades and was a real companion to them. No question, however intricate, was passed off unanswered. In this way they grew through boyhood with no mysteries of life tormenting their mentalities.
