# Chapter IV

He was so surprised and happy to see her. His eyes sparkled as the love light shone in them. "'Twas mighty good of you to meet me, girlie," he said in his old sincere way. "I thought 'twould be a little late to call on you at your boarding house, so I decided to stay here tonight and have a glorious day with you tomorrow."

"I was going away for the Fourth, but when I got your letter, I decided I'd come here and meet you and stay at the hotel myself until Saturday. Then I'll go on to Chenoa to visit. There is really nothing of interest in Normal for you to see, so I thought we'd just have a better time together here in Bloomington. Tomorrow we can see the town together," and she felt that old time chumminess coming back. *It was nice,* she thought—*but—I must tell him tomorrow.*

They went to the hotel and each registered for their rooms. They washed up then met in the lobby to go for a walk. They found some seats to rest in and were soon chatting away about home folks and doings, both really enjoying themselves.

Parepa unveiling new virtues in her partner as they sat there side by side in the moonlight. It was a night that makes lovers, a night that makes dreams. Johnie was dreaming—Parepa was too. But what was she dreaming? She could feel a confusion of dreams. Finally she felt an arm around her and a hand turning her face up to—yes, it was Johnie's lips to be kissed. She sank her head on his shoulders and sat silently as she pressed her hand into his to hold. She was safe with him—

She could feel his heartbeat. It was a true heart, a pure heart, a clean heart. She had known this man from childhood, he was everything that's good, not a blot, not a stain on his character.

What did she know about Harold's past? Nothing—but, of course, he is good too—she'd not doubt that.

Finally Johnie asked, "Have you been thinking real hard about what we were talking about the last time we were together, Allie?" and he gave her a hug and squeezed her hand lovingly.

Parepa wished he would grab her and run far, far away with her. Why, oh, why was he so slow? She found herself on fire at his slightest touch. Why couldn't he see! Why couldn't he feel, and she turned her head from him when she really wanted to throw herself into his arms and cry take me! take! and keep me. He was so dear, so disturbing.

"No, Johnie, I haven't thought anything about it. You know I'm going to teach another year. There will be time enough later," she said calmly.

She could feel his arm loosening, his handclasp weakening. His heart sank. "Maybe I shouldn't have come?" he said, "maybe you would have had a better time with someone else. Tell me, Allie, shall I go home in the morning? I don't want to spoil your good times: really I don't. I was foolish enough to think maybe you would have some good news to tell me. I guess I'm too anxious. I really don't want to hurry you. I do so miss you, Allie, you don't know how I've missed you," and he rose from the seat and helped her to her feet.

"I'm really glad you came, Johnie," she said. "I would have been lonesome too if you hadn't come. Tomorrow we will have another good visit."

They walked back to the hotel chatting together about this one and that one at home and parted, wishing each other a good night.

When Parepa reached her room, she threw herself on her bed and wept bitter tears. She didn't know why she wept. She couldn't explain her feelings to even her own satisfaction. She knew she was madly in love, but with whom? She was afraid it was Johnie. She wasn't sure—for hours she tossed on her bed. Why was he—how could he be so patient—so willing to wait for her decision? Why didn't he urge her—force her—make her see his worthiness? Finally sleep overcame her, and she lay as a punished child, sobbing as she slept.

In another room of that same hotel, a young man lay awake, feeling happy in the thought he was so near to the girl he loved. She was glad he had come—she had said so—tomorrow we will have a good visit. Will she love me sometime? Of course she will—I can wait. She must have her own time—I'll not coax her. Someday she will be my wife—I must bide my time. He dropped to sleep in a peaceful, restful, confident state of mind that is the possession of only such honest, fair minded men as Johnie Bradford.

The next morning at ten o'clock, two young people met with the spirit of the day ringing in their ears. Horns and whistles blowing, flags flying, drums beating, firecrackers deafening them. Such crowds! It certainly was no place for confidence. No place for them.

They lunched at noon, then took a streetcar to its farthest destination, where they left it and strolled far out into a woods. Allie loved this—she always did. So did Johnie—this being alone with the girl of his heart, so far from everyone else. They chatted along, laughing and joking as they went until they reached a winding stream.

"Oh, let's sit down here under these trees and watch the water fall. Isn't it charming out here?" and Allie clapped her hands like a little child.

"I like it too," he said, seating himself close beside her, and he did.

On the other side of the fence the cows and horses nibbled contentedly at the green grass. Companionship seemed essential to their happiness. A solitary tree, standing apart, looked sad and lonesome. Allie fell to thinking about the great necessity of companionship to one's happiness. She came to realize a really good companion is a rare find. It takes so many ingredients to make one. Tolerance, consideration for the feelings of another, honesty of conduct, the ability to overlook, the knack of understanding, the courage to be one's self, and many more qualities to make a genuine companionship. She looked into the face of her friend by her side and saw there all the things involved in a real companion. The lasting things, the things that count.

He turned to her with the question, "A penny for your thoughts. You are surely having some deep ones. Most forgot I was here, I think. Out with them. What were you thinking about?" and he shook her as if trying to waken her from a dream.

She laughed and said, "You'd laugh at me if I were to tell you. You'd think I was silly."

"Try me and see," he answered, pulling some green grass and tossing it in her lap.

"Well, then, I was thinking how lonesome it would be in this world if one had to live alone—always—like that tree over there by itself. It actually looks sad, don't it, Johnie?" and she looked up into his eyes for his answer.

"I think I know something about how it feels. I've had a sample of it lately, and if it wasn't for a hope that always stays with me, I'd be as sad and unsociable looking as that tree." He gave a long sympathetic look at the tree then turned to Allie and said, "There's really no excuse for anyone being lonesome."

"Do you really believe that, Johnie?" she asked.

"Yes, I believe it as truly as I believe anything. Only those without hope need be lonesome, and everyone should have hope. If they haven't, they are 'hopeless'—entirely hopeless. You get what I mean?" he asked.

"I—I guess so," she answered. She realized she lacked one thing in her make up which she would give anything to acquire. That thing was understanding. Their conversation fell in less formal channels. All the old crowd was discussed. This one was going with that one, so and so had a new buggy. One old neighbor had died, and his will was discussed as well as what his children would do with what they got.

No one was forgotten. Allie asked about them all. They laughed and talked until most dark.

"Was there anything Allie would like to do tonight—any place she would like to go in particular?"

"No, I just like to visit," she told him as they strolled toward the car line. There had been no ardent love making, no pushing his suit, or reference made to their engagement.

It was a most restful, pleasant and enjoyable afternoon. Such was the result of a perfect companionship with a master mind—an understanding mind. Not a trained or highly educated mind, not the "master mind" one reads about in the 'ologies, but one controlled by the natural mastery of events as they shaped his character.

The next afternoon found Parepa boarding the train for Chenoa. Her visit there was one round of delight. Her cousins were a jolly lot. She was feted in grand style. Was introduced to the elite of the town and made the usual "crush" on the most eligible young man. It was a dash of high life—a complete change for Parepa, who enjoyed it all but was glad when she found herself back to her studies again.

She wondered if she were really glad Harold would get back tonight. One thing she was sure of, she would not marry anyone until after her school year was finished.

That evening at the club she came in for the usual "roasting" from the "gang." They told her how sad she looked, how lonesome she was, how she had pined away these last few days for her Harold.

Finally there was a commotion in the hall. One of the boys got up and hurried out.
