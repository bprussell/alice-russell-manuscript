# Chapter XXIV

"Oh, that was easy," the old fellow chuckled. "When Frances found out your present name she at once wrote checks to me for the amounts and hurried me off to Phoenix with two of them, each on different banks there, and to Tucson banks with the other two. You will have to sign the signature cards and I'll send them in to the banks today. I deposited two hundred and fifty thousand dollars in each of the four banks for you as a checking account. You can do as you please with it. Leave it in Arizona banks or change it to your home banks. My advice to you in these days of bank failures all over the United States is, never keep a great amount in any one bank and never let one bank know how much you have in another bank. Always be fair with the government. Keep your business affairs as simple as possible. Avoid complications. As soon as you return home employ the very best secretary you can find to take care of your affairs. Never turn your business over to anyone, I mean by that, keep your authority and don't allow yourself to be talked out of or into anything. Use your own best judgment always but be sure it *is* your best. Be fair and true to yourself and others and most of all to your God. Be not overanxious for this world's goods. Be satisfied with fair returns. Never plunge or speculate. Always remember we are all 'Tenants of the Lord'—caretakers for him. These are the principles on which the Emmerson fortune has always been handled. You can never go wrong by following them.

"Here, take care of these, they belong to you," he said handing her the deposit books and checks. "Both Frances and I feel sure she could not have trusted her fortune to better hands."

"Oh, thank you Daddy," she said. "I know you shall not be disappointed in me. It's hard for me to believe it's true."

"There, there now, you will come to it in time. I suppose this is a little sudden," and patting her hand he said, "You better rest awhile now, we will dine together at two."

Parepa slowly ascended the stairs and on entering her living room saw on the table a letter from home. Its sides fairly bulged with its messages. Her maid stood waiting. "I'll rest an hour then dress for luncheon," she told her and taking her letter from its envelope she laughed as she recognized the handwriting of four of her kingdom. Each had added their bit to assure her she need not worry about them. She kissed each one then slipping into her kimona she relaxed on the lounge.

The doings of the forenoon had no room in the thoughts of this woman. Tears crept into her eyes as she read over and over each of her family's messages. She was homesick. Yes, there was no doubt about it. She gave way to a torrent of tears. Try as she would she could not control them. Her maid asked timidly, "Are you sick, Mrs. Bradford?"

"Oh, so sick," she answered. "The worse sickness in the world. Homesick," and again she shook with sobs. "I'll tell you what to do," suggested the maid. "Let me give you a thorough conditioning," and she passed into the dressing room.

Parepa heard the water running in the bathroom. She knew she was going to be better for the attention given her. She was surprised to see how restful running water was to her nerves. Her mind had been taken entirely from her family. Her maid chatted to her as she patted here and there, rubbed vigorously this place and that, and finally laying one wave after another in what she called, "My lady's beautiful white hair." When she finished there were no tear stains left. Parepa felt keenly the need of new clothes. Hers she knew must appear very shabby to her maid and her friends.

"Now you look as sweet and fresh as though you hadn't been sick," she laughed.

"Thank you, that's nice of you to say that. Now I think I'll write a letter home," she said and sat about it. It was a long loving letter. She did not think it important to tell them of the financial transactions of the morning. "Frances has looked out for our future finances abundantly," was her only reference to it. When she had sealed her letter Nanettezen took it to a servant and it was sent at once to the post office.

At two o'clock she and Daddy dined together in a little portico. Parepa was delighted with the lightheartedness of her companion. He joked and laughed with her as one many years his junior. Finally he asked, "Do you like it here, Parepa?"

"Oh, yes, I do," she said. "I only wish my husband could see it. He'd love this setting. He's crazy about mountains," she said.

"Well, no doubt he will see it a plenty. I just got your deed back, for it—it's yours now. Frances wanted you to have it. It's the only real estate she owned now. She thought you might like to spend some time here each year. She hopes you will. She has become more attached to it than any estate she ever owned. Promise her you will spend some time here after she has gone. It will please her so much," and the old man's eyes shone with joy at Parepa's surprise and delight.

"Oh, Daddy Wheeler! I would rather have it than anything in the world! How can Frances be so thoughtful of me? I don't deserve it," she reached over and laid her hand on Daddy's. "Are you sure you, yourself are well looked after? Have you plenty? I'd love to look after you myself," and she patted his hand in a most kindly way.

"That's just like you. Wanting to mother me," he laughed then added, "Thank you. I appreciate your thoughtfulness but I have my plans all made dear. After Frances leaves me I am going to turn everything I own over to charities—all but a few million which I shall give to an old people's home, where I expect to reside the remainder of my days. My duty to this world will have been fulfilled at Frances's death. I shall remain inactive thereafter. It is my wish that it be this way."

Parepa marveled at the calm and unaffected way in which both Frances and Daddy talked of their passing. Never a fear or worry entered into it. She was sure they were both anxious to go. "What a strange way to feel," she thought. They were through eating now, she took Daddy's arm and they went in the library to find to their surprise Frances sitting at the table ready to sign the legal papers which Daddy had prepared.

"Are you sure you are able to sit up like that, darling?" asked Daddy.

"O' yes," she laughed. "I knew I'd surprise you, but I feel so strong today," she answered.

"Are you sure you are not trying to show off?" laughed Parepa as she kissed her cheek tenderly.

"Sure as the world. Now let's get busy." Daddy witnessed the signature to each officially, Parepa watched the proceeding with sadness in her heart. Why must such good people as these be taken from this world? Their every desire is to do good for others. I'll try my best to carry on for them, she vowed silently as Daddy declared, "That is the last one."

They were each placed in an addressed envelope and sealed. "I'll take them right into town and get them on their way," he said, rising and bidding them goodbye. "They will all be returned in ten days or two weeks."

After he had gone Parepa turned to Frances and said, "Now darling let me make you comfortable on your couch," and she took her by the arm and led her to it.

"I'm so glad to have that tended to. Now we can visit and enjoy ourselves the rest of your stay, can't we dear?" and Frances smiled up at Parepa.

"I'm so glad you feel stronger Frances and I hope you will get well enough to go home with me," she offered.

"No, I will never be able to travel. I know my limitations. I am happier than I have been since we parted."

"Oh, Frances, it was so grand of you to deed this wonderful estate to me. I fell in love with it at sight and I'm going to love it as long as I live."

"I'm glad you will. It has been a real home to me. I would like to have you keep three of my servants here as I know they can be trusted to care for the place. I have looked out for them well, with that understanding," and she smiled again at Parepa.

"You haven't forgotten anything that will make it easy for me, Frances. I don't understand how you could be so thoughtful of every little detail."

"It's all I've had to do these last five years. I have given my whole time to it and enjoyed every minute I spent doing it. Someday while you are here I'm sure I'll be able to show you around the place. You have made me so much stronger."

"I wouldn't have you exert yourself for the world, Frances, dear," she said as the nurses came in to take her for her rest.

"You will not be able to visit with your friend more today," the nurse said. "You have had more than your share today."

"You better stroll about the grounds a bit Parepa," said Frances. "Goodbye for today," and with a kiss they parted. Parepa did as Frances suggested. She walked for over an hour through the entrancing gardens. Once in a while she would come upon a group of men working at some duty or another on the estate. It all seemed like a dream to her now. She could not seem to grasp the reality of the thing. As she went into the house, the thought came to her, "This is my house, what will I do with it?"

The evening passed and the morning broke fair and fine. In the distance the irrigated land showed signs of vegetation. Parepa sat for a long time studying the view from her window. The panorama of beauty almost took her breath away. She knew this tract of raw desert land had been selected and purchased by Frances and Daddy because of its natural beauty and suitability to their needs. From her windows she raved over the incomparable beauty of the colorful mountains. Their musical names—Santa Ritas, Tortillitas, Santa Catalinas, and Rincons—interested her greatly. She knew they were steeped in tradition, history, romance, and adventure. She hoped she would learn a little of their story while there. And to think this place is mine. It was beginning to dawn on her now.

Frances had named it "Kashema" meaning "Home, a place of rest, repose, security, and peace." Surely that is what it meant to her. She rose and her maid put her through the usual morning routine.

After breakfast she visited for an enjoyable hour with Frances and Daddy. She was convinced that Frances was really better. She seemed stronger each time she saw her.

"Daddy, you call your chauffeur and take Parepa a ride over the county," suggested Frances when the nurses came for her. It was a most welcome treat. Daddy was in his glory. He enjoyed the animated exclamations of joy and surprise that came from Parepa's lips as she breathed in the beauty around her. They rode until nearly two o'clock. After lunch she spent the afternoon alone with Frances who was rolled to the patio, a beautifully green spot landscaped with native plants. It was a most wonderful day, and when Parepa retired that night she felt quite acquainted with the house and grounds.

She had but a few moments with Frances the next forenoon as she overslept and had to excuse herself that she might write to the home folks. She would see Frances at two when they were to dine together. After she reached her room, her maid waited patiently until she had written her letter. After she had sent it to the post office she suggested to Parepa that she give her another conditioning treatment. Parepa was delighted. The bath and massages made her feel so rested. Just as the body powder had been applied, the tap of the door knocker called her maid out to her sitting room door. She heard a commotion and slipping into her dressing gown stepped into the room. To her surprise she saw two messenger boys carrying many boxes containing ladies' wearing apparel complete from shoes to hats. Nothing was lacking.

Two lady modistes followed. They were from the most exclusive shop in Tucson.

"A surprise for my lady," said her maid. "Miss Emmerson's surprise for you. You are to choose what you like."

Parepa always craved lovely clothes. She was fitted first with lingerie. Beautiful filmy stuff. Parepa loved the feel of it. A half dozen matched ensembles were chosen from the lot. Then three form fitters or foundation garments (we use to call them corsets) were selected. Hosiery came next. Three pair each of six shades seemed necessary. Their attention was turned to the dresses. Her room looked like a stylish downtown shop. Every place one could be hung up was filled. Beautiful dinner dresses, street dresses, sport dresses, morning gowns and dance frocks. First she selected a dark blue street suit with shoes, bag, umbrella, gloves, that would match. Then a pretty green figured silk with hat, shoes, and accessories for afternoon. A black dinner gown of most beautiful soft silk and lace draped her figure now. She was radiant. The excitement brought out an animated expression which made this mature woman flush and sparkle as a young maiden. A black picture hat, patent leather pumps, a string of pearls, and such a dear little broach seemed to say finish to this outfit.

One white and one orchid silk morning dress, one sport outfit (chosen for fear of offending Frances if omitted), a gray voile with rose figures and trim, two light wool suits with accessories were all Parepa felt necessary. Four dozen of the most exquisite 'kerchiefs were placed on the table for her. She had not given a moment's notice to the dainty ruffled and belaced shell pink creation hanging by the door. It was meant for a younger woman, she thought and passed it by. One of the modistes took it from its hanger and said, "You must try this one, Miss Emmerson ordered this one especially for you."

"Oh," exclaimed Parepa as she recognized the pink coloring of the cheap dotted swiss dress she had worn to the dinner at the Fey Hotel thirty years ago. Frances never forgot that dress. Then they had gone to the theatre and "Uncle Tom."

The other modiste lifted a large white chiffon hat from a box, around the crown and hanging from the brim was a wreath of tiny pink roses. "To match," she said as she held it beside the dress.

Would Parepa cry? No not now, some other time—but not now. Now she would be brave and do anything to please Frances. She slipped into it, it was so pretty.

"Look in the glass," her maid suggested. "You are beautiful." "Miss Emmerson will be so pleased," said the pretty modiste.

Parepa could not believe it was she, when she glanced into the mirror.

"I'll keep it," she said and motioned the maid to loosen it.
