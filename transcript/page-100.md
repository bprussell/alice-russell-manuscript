     It was arranged that between coarses each of the party were to
tell their first impressions of Parepa.  Uncle George stood up after
the fruit cocktail and said:  "I believe I should have the privilege of
telling you first how this little blue eyed, rosy cheeked freckled
nosed, curly haired youngster came to be in our midst these last two
year.
     You see it was this way.  I was in the habit of making my brother's
home an over night stopping place once very two weeks as I drove the tea
wagon through the country for over fifteen years.  I took a great fancy
to little Alice Parepa-Roza from a tiny baby to the day she came home
with me in the tea wagon.  I always called her my girl.  I happened to
be at brother John's when they named her.  The Alice was for an eastern
Aunt and the Parepa-Roza was given her more as a joke, for the time
being, and not as a permanent name.  You see the real Parepa-Roza was a
great Prima Donna and most beautiful singer.  As she was crossing the
ocean in a large ship it sank.  She sang to the frantic drowning crew
and voyagers until she herself sank to a watery grave.  This little
baby here, " and he reached over and patted Parepa's head, "cried so
hard  and all the time that her father  said she had lun⟦unclear: g⟧s that would
make her a singer like the famous Parepa-Roza.  It was decided to name
her Parepa-Roza.  That was about sixteen years ago."  He gave Parepa
a mischievous glance.
     "She grew and grew like a weed.  Seemed to be large for her age
always; at fourteen she finished grade school.  On her birthday two
years ago I coaxed the consent of her parents to let her come home with
me to stay until my return trip two weeks later.  After a lot of discussion
and advice they consented.  I can see her yet sitting there beside me
in that  old covered tea wagon.
