     "Lean on me, dear, we'll soon be to the car track and you will
feel better when you get home."
     They were silent on the car though Frances was deeply concerned
about the pale, trembling, young girl at his side.  When they left the car
he asked," Is it because I greived you, darling?"
     "Yes," she answered, "I think it is."
     "But I can't go on taking your sweet kisses and your unselfish love
without feeling like a thief.  I <u>am</u> a thief, I <u>am</u> a thief to have hoped
⟦strikeover: hoped⟧ we would ever be more than friends.  I have stolen the most precious
thing a girl has to give  --  her love.  Oh, dearest, forgive me!  Forgive
me.  I beg of you forgive⟦strikeover: ness⟧⟦pencil: me⟧."  His whole being serged with anguish.
     They were home now and Frances went into the house with her and
called Aunt Kate who was preparing for bed.  Parepa lay limp on the couch.
Frances had wet his handkerchief and placed it on her head and was
kneeling beside her when Aunt Kate came in.
     "She took sick in the park.  I think she feels better now though.
Don't you Parepa?" he asked.
     "Yes, I'll be all right.  I guess I'm tired out," and she closed
her eyes.  "All I want is to sleep and sleep and dream and dream," she
drawled.  Then she lay still in a peaceful slumber.
     Aunt Kate removed her slippers and loosened her clothing.  "She
will be all right, there I think," she said.
     "Had we better call a doctor?" he asked.
